## What are the Federalist Papers?

*The Federalist* is a series of 85 essays published in New York newspapers between October 1787 and May 1788. They were later collected into a book. All were signed "Publius," a pen name honoring Publius Valerius Publicola, one of the founders of the ancient Roman Republic.

Behind the pen name were three men. **Alexander Hamilton** wrote 51 essays, **James Madison** wrote 29, and **John Jay** wrote 5. Their goal was practical: convince the people of New York to ratify (approve) the new Constitution written in Philadelphia that summer. The essays answered critics, known as Anti-Federalists, who feared the new national government would be too powerful.

Along the way, Publius produced something more lasting than a campaign. The papers explain the reasoning behind the separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism, an independent judiciary, and much more. Judges, including Supreme Court justices, still cite them when deciding what the Constitution means.

## Why rewrite them?

The original essays are hard. Their sentences are long, their vocabulary is from the 1700s, and they assume readers know about events and history that few people know today. Standard readability formulas put most of the papers at a college or graduate-school reading level.

That means most Americans never read the actual arguments behind their own government. They hear quotes, often out of context, and summaries, often slanted. This project tries to close that gap.

## How we adapted the text {#how-we-adapted}

Each plain-language version follows a few rules:

- **Grade 8–10 reading level.** That's about the level of a good newspaper. We check every paper with the Flesch-Kincaid formula, and each paper's page shows its estimated grade. (Commentary is checked too. Long word-for-word quotations from the 1780s are left out of the measurement.) We don't go lower on purpose. Simplifying further would mean cutting the qualifications, examples, and reasoning that make the arguments worth reading.
- **Follow the original, step by step.** We keep Publius's order, his main examples, and his voice ("I," "we," "you"). We break up long sentences, replace old words with modern ones, and explain references a modern reader wouldn't know. We don't skip arguments because they're difficult.
- **Keep the text and the commentary separate.** The main text is our best effort to say what Publius said. Everything in a labeled box, such as *Commentary*, *Background*, or *Why it still matters*, is our explanation, not his. Commentary points out when the authors were wrong, when their arguments were controversial, and how things turned out.
- **Always show the original.** Every section links to the exact paragraphs it covers. Use **Side by side** to read both at once, and check us whenever a passage matters to you.

Any rewrite is an interpretation. We've tried to be faithful and fair, but if you think we got something wrong, please [open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/jmagjohn/federalist-papers-made-clear/issues).

## Reading the papers honestly

Publius was writing persuasion, not a neutral textbook. He wanted to win a vote, and he presented his side's case in its best light. Some predictions in the papers turned out to be wrong. For example, Hamilton thought the Electoral College would almost always produce a president of excellent character, and Madison thought a large republic would keep national factions from forming.

The papers also come from a society that denied basic rights to most of its people. Women could not vote. Enlightened leaders, including Madison, owned enslaved people. Native nations appear mostly as threats. Where the papers deal with slavery, as in No. 42 and No. 54, our commentary says so plainly. Understanding these blind spots is part of understanding the founding.

## Is every paper finished?

All 85 papers have a full plain-language version, a summary, and the complete original text. We wrote them in order of importance, starting with the essays most often read, taught, and cited. Like any interpretation, they can always be improved, so corrections are welcome.

## Sources

The original text comes from the Project Gutenberg edition of *The Federalist Papers* (EBook #1404), which is in the public domain. Paragraph numbers follow that edition. Authorship follows the standard modern attributions. Twelve essays (Nos. 49–58, 62, and 63) were claimed by both Hamilton and Madison; most historians, supported by statistical studies of word use, credit Madison.

## Help out

This is an open project. The plain-language versions are simple text files in the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/jmagjohn/federalist-papers-made-clear), and the README explains the format. Corrections, new adaptations, and better commentary are all welcome.
