:::summary
Critics feared that the national government's power to tax would wipe out the states' power to raise their own money. Hamilton says the states would keep that power completely, with one exception: taxes on imports and exports. The Constitution doesn't merge the states into one nation. It creates only a partial union, so the states keep every power they had before, unless it was given *exclusively* to the national government. Hamilton explains the three ways that can happen: the Constitution says so directly, it grants a power to Congress and forbids it to the states, or state use of the power would flatly contradict the national power. Taxing ordinary goods fits none of these. Both governments can tax at the same time, which is called **concurrent** power.
:::

:::keypoints
- Hamilton doesn't think the national government would really try to starve the states. The people, the risk of angering the states, and the obvious need for local government would stop it.
- But he accepts that the states **should** have an independent power to tax, and says they **would**, except for taxes on imports and exports.
- The Constitution makes only a **partial union**. The states keep every power that wasn't **exclusively** given to the national government.
- A power is exclusive in **three cases**: (1) the Constitution says so in clear words; (2) it gives the power to Congress and **forbids** it to the states; (3) state use of the power would be **completely contradictory** to the national power.
- Examples: Congress's "exclusive legislation" over the national capital (case 1); duties on imports (case 2); a "uniform rule of naturalization" (case 3).
- Taxing everything else is **concurrent**: both governments can do it.
- The ban on state import taxes is a **"negative pregnant"**: by denying one thing, it confirms another. Forbidding states to tax imports implies they *can* tax everything else.
- Possible **inconvenience** between the two tax systems isn't enough to take away a state's power. Only a true **constitutional contradiction** can do that.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 31](/papers/31/), Hamilton described the critics' main fear: that the national government would use its unlimited power to tax to take every source of revenue for itself and leave the states helpless. Here he begins his answer by looking closely at what the Constitution actually says.

This essay is one of the clearest early explanations of how American federalism divides power. The idea that states keep all powers not given away was later written into the Constitution itself in the **10th Amendment** (1791).
:::

## The states keep their power to tax {orig=1}

I don't believe the state governments face any real danger from a national power to control how they raise money. I am convinced that several things would completely block any abusive use of such a power: the will of the people, the great risk of provoking the anger of the state governments, and a general belief that local governments are useful and necessary for local purposes.

Even so, I am willing to grant, in full, the reasoning that says each state should have an independent and uncontrollable power to raise its own revenue for its own needs. And having granted this, I say that under the Convention's plan the states would keep that power in the most complete and unlimited sense, with one exception: duties on imports and exports. Any attempt by the national government to limit the states in using that power would be a violent seizure of authority. No article or clause of the Constitution would justify it.

:::vocab
Revenue: Money a government collects, mainly through taxes, to pay for its work.
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
:::

## Partial union, and three kinds of exclusive power {orig=2}

If the states were fully consolidated, or merged, into one complete national sovereignty, the parts would be completely under the whole. Whatever powers they kept would depend entirely on the national will.

But the Convention's plan aims only at a partial union, or partial consolidation. So the state governments would clearly keep all the rights of sovereignty they had before, except those that the Constitution delegated EXCLUSIVELY to the United States. This exclusive delegation, or rather this transfer, of state sovereignty would exist in only three cases:

1. where the Constitution, in clear words, gives an exclusive power to the Union;
2. where it gives a power to the Union in one place, and in another place forbids the states to use the same power;
3. where it gives a power to the Union that a similar power in the states would make absolutely and totally CONTRADICTORY and IMPOSSIBLE.

I use these strong words to separate the last case from another that may look like it but is really quite different. That is the case where both governments share a power, and this might sometimes cause one government's POLICY to get in the way of the other's. But it would not create any direct contradiction in their constitutional authority.

Here are examples of the three cases of exclusive national power.

**The first case.** The next-to-last clause of Article 1, section 8, says that Congress shall exercise "EXCLUSIVE LEGISLATION" over the district that will be set aside as the seat of government.

**The second case.** The first clause of the same section gives Congress power "to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts and excises." And the second clause of section 10 of the same article says, "NO STATE SHALL, without the consent of Congress, lay any imposts or duties on imports or exports, except for the purpose of executing its inspection laws." From this comes an exclusive national power to tax imports and exports, with that one exception. But this power is limited by another clause, which says no tax or duty may be placed on goods exported from any state. Because of that limit, the national power now covers only DUTIES ON IMPORTS.

**The third case.** Congress has the power "to establish an UNIFORM RULE of naturalization throughout the United States." This power must be exclusive. If each state could set its own DIFFERENT RULE, there could not be a UNIFORM RULE.

:::quote
...the State governments would clearly retain all the rights of sovereignty which they before had, and which were not, by that act, EXCLUSIVELY delegated to the United States.

Meaning: The states keep every power they already had, unless the Constitution clearly gives that power to the national government alone.
:::

:::vocab
Consolidation: Merging the states into a single national government.
Sovereignty: Supreme, independent political authority.
Delegate: To hand over a power or task to someone else.
Exclusive power: A power that only one government may use.
Impost: A tax on imported goods; a duty.
Excise: A tax on goods made or sold within a country, such as liquor. Collecting it often required inspectors to enter homes and businesses.
Inspection laws: State laws for checking the quality of goods, such as flour or tobacco, before they were shipped. States could charge small fees to pay for these inspections.
Naturalization: The legal process by which an immigrant becomes a citizen.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's three examples became real parts of American government.

- **The capital.** In 1790, Congress chose a site on the Potomac River for the "seat of government," and the government moved to Washington, D.C., in 1800. Because Congress has "exclusive legislation" there, the District of Columbia is still not a state and has no voting members in Congress, which remains a subject of debate.
- **Import duties.** The Constitution's ban on state import taxes ended the trade wars of the 1780s, when states taxed each other's goods. The ban on export taxes (Article 1, section 9) was demanded mainly by Southern states, whose economies depended on shipping crops like tobacco and rice overseas, crops largely grown by enslaved people.
- **Naturalization.** Congress passed its first naturalization law in 1790. It allowed only "free white" persons to become citizens. Racial limits on naturalization lasted, in various forms, until 1952.
:::

## Ordinary taxes are shared {orig=3}

There is a case that may seem to resemble the third one but is in fact very different. It bears directly on the question we are discussing: the power to tax all goods other than exports and imports. I argue that this is clearly a **concurrent** and equal power, held by both the United States and the individual states.

The clause granting the taxing power plainly contains no words that make it EXCLUSIVE to the Union. No separate clause or sentence forbids the states to use it. In fact, a clear and decisive argument for the opposite comes from the limit placed on the states regarding duties on imports and exports. This limit admits that, if it weren't there, the states would have the power it takes away. It also admits that, for all other taxes, the states' power is not reduced at all.

Any other reading would make this limit both unnecessary and dangerous. It would be unnecessary because, if giving Congress the power to lay such duties already shut out the states, or even placed them under Congress in this area, there would be no need for the limit. It would be dangerous because adding it leads straight to the conclusion I have just described. If the critics' reasoning were right, the authors could never have meant that conclusion: that the states, in every case the limit doesn't cover, share the power of taxation with the Union.

The limit amounts to what lawyers call a NEGATIVE PREGNANT. That means a DENIAL of one thing that also AFFIRMS another. It denies the states the power to tax imports and exports. And it affirms their power to tax all other goods.

Someone might argue that it was meant to shut the states out ABSOLUTELY from taxing imports and exports, while leaving them free to lay other taxes SUBJECT TO THE CONTROL of Congress. That would be mere trickery. The limiting clause only says that the states may not lay such duties WITHOUT THE CONSENT OF CONGRESS. If we read it the way just described, the Constitution would be adding a formal rule just to reach a very absurd result. The states could tax imports and exports WITH THE CONSENT of Congress. And they could tax every other good UNLESS Congress STOPPED them. If that was the intention, why add the clause at all? Why not leave it to what the critics say is the natural effect of the original clause giving the Union a general power of taxation? Clearly, this could not have been the intention, and the clause can't be read that way.

:::quote
The restriction in question amounts to what lawyers call a NEGATIVE PREGNANT that is, a NEGATION of one thing, and an AFFIRMANCE of another...

Meaning: When a rule says "you can't do this one thing," it suggests you *can* do the things it doesn't mention. Forbidding states to tax imports implies they can tax everything else.
:::

:::vocab
Concurrent power: A power that both the national and state governments can use at the same time, such as the power to tax.
Negative pregnant: A legal term for a denial that also implies something. "You may not tax imports" implies "you may tax other things."
Sophistry: Clever-sounding arguments that are actually misleading or false.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is reading the Constitution the way a lawyer reads a contract, which makes sense, since he was one of New York's leading lawyers. His key point is that the authors went out of their way to forbid the states from taxing imports. If the national taxing power already shut out the states, that rule would have been pointless. So the rule is evidence that the states keep every other kind of taxing power.

Hamilton's view won out. Today, Americans pay many taxes to both governments on the same things. Most states tax incomes, and so does the national government. Both put taxes on gasoline. This is concurrent power in action.
:::

## Inconvenience is not contradiction {orig=4}

Some say the state and national taxing powers would clash. But they can't clash in the way needed to shut the states out.

It is true that a state might put a tax on some particular good that would make it UNWISE for the Union to add a further tax on the same good. But that wouldn't mean the Union had no constitutional power to add one. How large a tax should be, and whether either side should raise it, would be questions of good judgment for both. There would be no direct contradiction of power.

The particular policies of the national and state tax systems might sometimes fail to line up exactly. They might require each side to hold back for the other. But a mere chance of inconvenience in using a power is not enough. Only a direct constitutional contradiction can, by implication, take away and end a right of sovereignty that already existed.

:::commentary
Here Hamilton draws a fine but important line. Two governments taxing the same thing might be *inconvenient*. If both tax whiskey heavily, for example, the total tax might be too high. But inconvenience is a problem for lawmakers to work out with good sense, not a reason to take away a state's constitutional power.

Courts have sometimes found real conflicts, though. In *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819), the Supreme Court ruled that Maryland could not tax a branch of the national bank. Chief Justice John Marshall wrote that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," so a state can't use its taxing power to cripple a lawful part of the national government. That bank, the Second Bank of the United States, followed the model of the first national bank, which Hamilton had created as Treasury Secretary.
:::

## The Constitution itself confirms the rule {orig=5}

The need for shared power in certain cases comes from dividing sovereign power between two governments. And the rule that the states keep, in full force, every power not clearly taken from them and given to the Union is not just a theory that follows from that division. The whole document containing the proposed Constitution plainly accepts it.

In that document, the Convention gave general powers to the national government. But wherever it thought the states should not have the same powers, it took great care to add clauses forbidding the states to use them. Section 10 of Article 1 is made up entirely of such clauses.

This fact clearly shows what the Convention intended. It gives us a rule for reading the Constitution that comes from the text itself. That rule supports the position I have taken and disproves every theory to the contrary.

:::commentary
Article 1, section 10 lists things the states may not do. For example, they may not make treaties, coin money, or tax imports without Congress's consent. Hamilton's point is that if the Convention had wanted to keep the states from taxing other things, it knew how to say so. It wrote a whole list of such limits and left ordinary taxes off it.

The same principle was soon written into the Constitution. The **10th Amendment** (1791) says that powers not delegated to the United States, nor prohibited to the states, "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."
:::

:::today
The system Hamilton described is the one Americans live with today. Both the national government and nearly every state collect taxes, often on the same income, purchases, and property. Debates continue about where the lines should be. For example, courts and lawmakers have argued over when states may collect sales taxes on goods bought online from sellers in other states. Some people think state and national tax systems should be better coordinated. Others value the freedom of each state to set its own taxes and compete with other states. Hamilton's basic rule, that the states keep every power not clearly taken away, is still the starting point.
:::
