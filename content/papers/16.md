:::summary
Hamilton continues his attack on the main flaw of the Articles of Confederation: Congress makes rules for **states**, not for **people**. When a state refuses to obey, the only way to enforce the rule is with an army, and that means **civil war**. Such a war would probably break up the Union. More likely, the states would simply stop obeying one by one, and the Union would die a slow death. The answer is a national government that acts **directly on individual citizens** through its own laws, officers, and courts. Hamilton then explains why that system would be far safer, because a state that wanted to block national laws would have to openly break the Constitution.
:::

:::keypoints
- Lawmaking for states as groups is the **"parent of anarchy."** It leads to states failing their duties, and the only cure is force, which means **civil war**.
- In such a war, guilty states would join together, big states would recruit neighbors, and **foreign powers** would happily help. The first war would likely end the Union.
- The more natural death is slower: obedient states would **copy the disobedient ones** instead of fighting them.
- It's often impossible to tell whether a state *won't* pay or *can't* pay.
- Enforcing laws on states would require a huge **standing army**, which would turn into a **military despotism** and is not even possible.
- A real government must **reach individual citizens** through its own courts and officials, just as state governments do.
- Under the new system, states could no longer block national laws by quietly **doing nothing**. They would have to **openly resist**, and judges and the people would stand against them.
- No government can prevent every great revolution, so it's unfair to blame a plan for not doing the impossible.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues the argument of [No. 15](/papers/15/). There Hamilton said the "great and radical vice" of the Articles was that Congress could only make requests of the states. Here he asks what would happen if Congress tried to *force* the states to obey. Many Anti-Federalists wanted to keep the old system, where the national government dealt only with the states, but give Congress more power within it. Hamilton argues that this approach could never work.

The essay appeared in December 1787, about a year after Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts had shown how fragile order could be.
:::

## The parent of anarchy {orig=1-2}

The principle of making laws for states, as political communities, has had certain effects in our own experience. The same effects appear in every other confederate government we know about, and they appear in exact proportion to how much each one relied on that principle. These examples deserve a separate and detailed study. Here I will just note one thing. Of all the ancient confederacies that history describes to us, the Lycian and Achaean leagues seem to have been most free from this mistaken principle, as far as we can tell from what remains of their records. And for that reason, they are the ones political writers have praised most, and most deserved it.

This bad principle may be called, truly and strongly, the parent of anarchy. We have seen that failures by the members of the Union are its natural and unavoidable children. And whenever such failures happen, the only remedy the system allows is force. The immediate result of using force is civil war.

:::vocab
Lycian Confederacy: An ancient union of cities in what is now southern Turkey. Montesquieu praised it as a model, partly because larger cities got more votes.
Achaean League: An alliance of Greek city-states (about 280–146 BC) that was eventually conquered by Rome.
Anarchy: A state of disorder with no effective government.
Delinquency: Failure to pay what one owes on time.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton promises a "distinct and particular examination" of other confederacies. That study comes in Nos. 18–20, which Madison and Hamilton wrote together. They cover ancient Greek leagues, the Holy Roman Empire, Switzerland, and the Netherlands.

He praises the Lycian and Achaean leagues because, in his view, they relied least on the system of governing members only as whole communities. Our knowledge of these ancient leagues is limited, as Hamilton himself admits ("as far as there remain vestiges of them").
:::

## Why war against the states would fail {orig=3}

Next we should ask how well such a hateful tool of government would even work in our case.

Unless the national government always had a large army at hand, it either couldn't use force at all, or using it would mean a war between parts of the Confederacy over broken promises. In that war, the strongest side would probably win, whether it was the side supporting the national authority or the side resisting it.

The failure to be punished would rarely be limited to one state. If more than one state had neglected its duty, being in the same position would lead them to unite for their common defense. Even without this sense of sympathy, suppose a large and powerful state was the offender. It would usually have enough influence with its neighbors to win some of them over to its cause.

False arguments about a danger to everyone's liberty could easily be made up. Believable excuses for the offending state's failures could easily be invented. These would alarm the fears, stir up the passions, and win the goodwill even of states that had done nothing wrong.

This would be even more likely because a large state's failures might sometimes come from a deliberate plan by its rulers. They might want to escape all outside control so they could pursue their own personal power. To do this, they would probably make secret deals ahead of time with leading men in nearby states.

If they couldn't find allies at home, they would turn to foreign powers for help. Those powers would rarely mind encouraging fights within a Confederacy whose firm union they had so much reason to fear.

Once the sword is drawn, people's passions know no limits. Wounded pride and angry resentment would push the states under attack to any extreme. They would do whatever it took to avenge the insult or avoid the shame of giving in. The first war of this kind would probably end with the breakup of the Union.

:::vocab
Specious: Seeming true or reasonable at first, but actually wrong.
Aggrandize: To increase one's own power, wealth, or importance.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction here is striking. He describes guilty states banding together, leaders claiming to defend "the common liberty," and foreign powers taking advantage of the split. Something like this happened in 1860–61, when Southern states left the Union together, claimed to be defending their rights, and hoped Britain and France would support them.

But the Civil War did not come about the way Hamilton describes. It was not a war to make states pay their share. It came after decades of conflict over slavery, and it began when Southern states tried to leave a Union that already acted directly on individuals. So the Constitution did not prevent every war between the states, but it did avoid the kind of constant conflict Hamilton feared here.
:::

## The slow, natural death of the Union {orig=4}

This would be the Confederacy's violent death. Its more natural death is the one we now seem about to experience, unless the federal system is quickly rebuilt in a stronger form.

Given the character of this country, it's unlikely that the states that obeyed would often want to go to war against the states that didn't. They would be much more ready to take the milder path. They would put themselves on equal terms with the offending states by copying their example. In this way, the guilt of all would become the protection of all. Our past experience has shown this attitude at work in full.

There would also be a problem that could never be solved: knowing when force could fairly be used. Failing to pay money would be the most common kind of failure. In those cases, it would often be impossible to tell whether a state didn't want to pay or simply couldn't. The excuse of being unable to pay would always be available. A case would have to be very obvious before anyone could prove the excuse false with enough certainty to justify the harsh step of using force. This problem alone, every time it came up, would give the majority in the national council wide room for party interest, favoritism, and oppression.

:::commentary
"The guilt of all would thus become the security of all" sums up the free-rider problem from [No. 15](/papers/15/). If every state is behind on its payments, none can be punished, because no one is left to do the punishing.

Hamilton's point about "won't pay" versus "can't pay" was real. In the 1780s, many states had little hard money, and their citizens were struggling with debt and high taxes. Some states truly couldn't pay their full requisitions. Others chose to spend their money on their own needs first. From the outside, the two looked the same.
:::

## An army to enforce every law? {orig=5-6}

It hardly needs proof that the states should not want a national constitution that could only be kept running by a large army, always ready, to carry out the government's ordinary requests and orders. Yet this is the plain choice facing those who want to deny the national government the power to reach individuals.

Such a plan, if it could work at all, would quickly turn into a military despotism. But in fact it could never work.

The Union wouldn't have the resources to support an army big enough to keep the larger states within the limits of their duty. Nor would it ever be given the means to create such an army in the first place. Think about how many people and how much strength several of these states have right now. Then look ahead to what they will become, even fifty years from now. Anyone who does this will immediately reject, as foolish and unrealistic, any plan to control the states by laws that act on them as whole communities and are enforced by force against them as communities. A project like this is hardly less fantastic than the monster-taming spirit of the mythical heroes and demigods of the ancient world.

Even in confederacies whose members were smaller than many of our counties, making laws for sovereign states and enforcing them with military force has never worked. It has rarely been tried against any but the weakest members. And in most cases, attempts to force stubborn and disobedient members to obey have set off bloody wars, with one half of the confederacy raising its flags against the other half.

:::vocab
Standing army: A permanent, professional army kept even in peacetime.
Despotism: Rule by one person with unlimited power.
Demigod: In ancient myths, a being who is part god and part human, such as Hercules.
:::

:::commentary
The "monster-taming" heroes are figures like Hercules, who in Greek myth killed the many-headed Hydra and other monsters. Hamilton is saying that controlling big states like Virginia, Pennsylvania, or Massachusetts with an army would be about as realistic as a myth.

Notice that Hamilton is turning a common Anti-Federalist fear against them. Opponents worried that the new Constitution would lead to a standing army that would crush liberty. Hamilton replies that it is *their* system, the one that acts on states, that would need a giant army to work at all.
:::

## The cure: reach the citizens directly {orig=7}

To a thoughtful mind, the result of these observations must be clear. If it's possible at all to build a federal government that can manage our common concerns and keep the general peace, it must be built on the opposite of the principle that the Constitution's opponents defend. This applies to the matters placed in its care.

It must carry its authority to the citizens themselves. It must not need any state laws in between. It must have the power to use the ordinary officers of the law to carry out its own decisions. The dignity of the national authority must be shown through the courts of justice.

The government of the Union, like the government of each state, must be able to speak directly to the hopes and fears of individuals. It must be able to draw on the passions that have the strongest influence on the human heart. In short, it must have all the means, and the right to use all the methods, of carrying out its powers that the state governments have and use.

:::quote
It must carry its agency to the persons of the citizens. It must stand in need of no intermediate legislations; but must itself be empowered to employ the arm of the ordinary magistrate to execute its own resolutions.

Meaning: The national government must be able to make laws that apply directly to people and enforce them through its own officials, without waiting for the states to act.
:::

:::vocab
Magistrate: A government official who enforces the law.
Intermediate legislation: Here, laws that state legislatures would have to pass before a national decision could take effect.
:::

:::commentary
This is the heart of the change the Constitution made. Under the Articles, if Congress needed money, it asked the states, and each state legislature had to pass its own law to raise it. Under the Constitution, Congress can pass a tax law, and federal tax collectors, federal prosecutors, and federal courts can enforce it on individuals. No state has to act in between.

Article VI of the Constitution backs this up with the **Supremacy Clause**. It makes the Constitution and federal laws "the supreme Law of the Land" and binds state judges to follow them.
:::

## "Couldn't a state still resist?" {orig=8-10}

Someone may object to this reasoning. They might say that if any state turned against the Union's authority, it could block national laws at any time. That would bring things to the same point of force that the old system is blamed for.

This objection looks reasonable, but it disappears the moment we notice the basic difference between mere NON-COMPLIANCE and DIRECT and ACTIVE RESISTANCE.

If the national government needs the state legislatures to step in before its measures can take effect, the states only have to NOT ACT, or ACT EVASIVELY, and the measure is defeated. This neglect of duty can be hidden behind fake, empty measures. That way it won't be noticed, and so it won't make the people worry about the safety of the Constitution. State leaders might even take credit for their secret violations of it, by pointing to some short-term convenience, exemption, or benefit for their state.

But suppose national laws don't need the state legislatures to step in. Suppose they act directly on the citizens. Then the state governments couldn't stop them without an open and violent use of unconstitutional power. Doing nothing or dodging wouldn't work. The states would have to act, and act in a way that left no doubt they had invaded national rights.

An experiment like this would always be risky. It would face a constitution that can defend itself to some degree, and a people informed enough to tell the difference between a legal use of power and an illegal seizure of it. To succeed, it would need more than a party majority in the state legislature. It would also need the support of the courts and of the people as a whole.

If the judges weren't part of a conspiracy with the legislature, they would declare that majority's resolutions contrary to the supreme law of the land, unconstitutional, and void. If the people didn't share the attitude of their state representatives, they would act as the natural guardians of the Constitution. They would throw their weight onto the national side and give it a clear advantage in the contest.

Attempts like this wouldn't often be made lightly or carelessly, since they would rarely be safe for the people who made them. The exception would be cases where the federal government used its power like a tyrant.

:::quote
The plausibility of this objection will vanish the moment we advert to the essential difference between a mere NON-COMPLIANCE and a DIRECT and ACTIVE RESISTANCE.

Meaning: It's easy for a state to block a law by quietly doing nothing. It's much harder, and more obviously wrong, for a state to actively stop federal officers from enforcing a law on its citizens.
:::

:::vocab
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Void: Having no legal force; canceled.
Unconstitutional: Against the Constitution, and so not valid.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point is clever. Under the Articles, a state could defeat Congress just by sitting still. Under the Constitution, a state that wanted to stop a federal law would have to do something visible and aggressive, like passing a law to block federal officers. That would make the conflict public, and it would put the state clearly in the wrong.

Notice also that Hamilton expects **judges** to declare unconstitutional laws "void." This is an early statement of **judicial review**, which he develops fully in [No. 78](/papers/78/).

History tested this argument. In 1832, South Carolina declared two federal tariff laws "null and void" inside the state. President Andrew Jackson threatened to use force, and the crisis ended in a compromise. In 1957, Arkansas Governor Orval Faubus used the state's National Guard to keep Black students from entering Little Rock's Central High School after courts had ordered it to integrate. President Eisenhower sent federal troops to protect the students. In both cases, the state's resistance was open and direct, just as Hamilton predicted. Whether the people would side with the nation, as he hoped, depended on the issue and the time.
:::

## Rioters, rebels, and revolutions {orig=11}

Suppose opposition to the national government came from the disorderly behavior of stubborn or rebellious individuals. It could be overcome by the same means the state governments use every day against the same problem. The courts and officers of the law serve the law of the land, whatever its source. They would surely be just as ready to protect national laws as local ones from attacks by lawless private people.

Then there are the small uprisings that sometimes disturb society. These may come from the schemes of a small faction, or from sudden bad moods that don't spread to the community as a whole. The national government could draw on far greater resources to put down disturbances like these than any single state could.

Finally, there are the deadly feuds that, at certain times, spread like a fire through a whole nation or a very large part of it. They may come from serious reasons for discontent given by the government. Or they may come from some violent popular outburst that spreads like a disease. These don't fit any ordinary rules of calculation. When they happen, they usually amount to revolutions and the breaking apart of empires. No form of government can always avoid or control them. It is useless to hope to guard against events too great for human foresight or caution. And it would be foolish to reject a government because it couldn't do the impossible.

:::vocab
Insurrection: A violent uprising against a government.
Sedition: Speech or action that stirs up rebellion against the government.
Faction: A group of citizens, either a majority or a minority, united by a shared passion or interest that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton divides resistance into three sizes: individual lawbreakers, small local uprisings, and nationwide revolutions. The new government could handle the first two. The third, he admits, no government can always prevent.

The new government soon faced the second kind. In 1794, farmers in western Pennsylvania rose up against a federal tax on whiskey. President Washington called out about 13,000 militia troops from several states, and the **Whiskey Rebellion** collapsed with little fighting. Hamilton himself, then Secretary of the Treasury, rode with the army. The national government acted against individual rebels, not against the state of Pennsylvania, just as this essay describes.
:::

:::today
The difference between quietly not cooperating and openly resisting still matters in American government. The Supreme Court has ruled that the federal government generally cannot "commandeer" state officials, meaning it can't order them to carry out federal programs. For example, in *Printz v. United States* (1997), the Court struck down part of a gun law that required local sheriffs to run background checks. So states today can sometimes refuse to help enforce federal laws, as some have done with immigration or marijuana laws. But federal officers can still enforce those laws themselves, as Hamilton's plan intended. Supporters of this arrangement say it protects the states' independence. Critics say it lets states undercut national policy.
:::
