:::summary
Madison continues his argument that the states are not in danger. He starts with a reminder: the national and state governments are both just **agents of the same people**, and the people decide which one to trust. People will naturally feel closer to their states, which handle their daily lives. Members of Congress will also bring local loyalties with them. If the national government ever tried to grab power, the states could resist it easily: by refusing to cooperate, raising alarms, and coordinating with each other, as the colonies did against Britain. And even if the national government built a large army, it would face about half a million armed citizens in state militias, led by officers the states appointed.
:::

:::keypoints
- The national and state governments are **"but different agents and trustees of the people."** The people, not the governments, hold the final authority.
- People will feel the **strongest attachment to their states**, which handle their daily concerns and offer more jobs and connections.
- Even during the Revolution, when Congress was most important, people's loyalty **returned to their states**.
- If people ever came to trust the national government more, it would only be because it **governed better**, and that would be their right.
- Members of Congress will bring **local loyalties** with them, so they'll be slow to attack state rights.
- States have powerful ways to **resist unpopular national measures**: public anger, refusing to cooperate, opposition from governors and legislatures.
- A national power grab would trigger **united resistance** from all the states, like the resistance to Britain.
- No national army could defeat **half a million armed citizens** organized in state militias.
- Either the national government will depend on the people, so it won't try to oppress them, or it won't, so the people and states will defeat it.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues the argument from [No. 45](/papers/45/). Anti-Federalists warned that the national government would gradually swallow the states, and that it might use a standing army to crush any resistance. Madison answers both fears. His discussion of armed citizens and state militias is often quoted today in debates over the Second Amendment, which Madison himself drafted in 1789.
:::

## Both governments serve the same people {orig=1}

Continuing the subject of the last essay, I'll now ask whether the national government or the state governments will have the advantage in the people's affection and support.

Although they are chosen in different ways, we must think of both as basically depending on the great body of the citizens of the United States. I'll assume this here about the national government and save the proof for later.

The national and state governments are really just different agents and trustees of the people. They were created with different powers and designed for different purposes. The Constitution's opponents seem to have completely forgotten the people in their arguments on this subject. They have treated these two governments as rivals and enemies, with no common master to control their efforts to take each other's powers.

These gentlemen must be reminded of their mistake. They must be told that the final authority, wherever borrowed authority may be found, belongs to the people alone. Whether either government, and which one, manages to expand its power at the other's expense won't depend just on which one is more ambitious or clever. Truth, as well as respect, requires us to assume that in every case the result will depend on the feelings and approval of the people they both serve.

:::quote
The federal and State governments are in fact but different agents and trustees of the people, constituted with different powers, and designed for different purposes.

Meaning: The national government and the state governments don't own any power of their own. Both are working for the people, who can decide which one to trust with what.
:::

:::vocab
Trustee: Someone trusted to manage something on behalf of others.
Derivative authority: Power that is borrowed or handed down from somewhere else. Here, the governments' power is derived from the people.
Constituents: The people a government or representative serves.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Madison's most important ideas about federalism. People often picture the national and state governments as two powers fighting over territory. Madison says that's the wrong picture. The people are the boss, and both governments are their employees. If the people decide one level of government does a job better, they can shift their trust to it. That idea is sometimes called **popular sovereignty**.
:::

## People will feel closest to their states {orig=2-4}

Many reasons, besides those already mentioned, make it nearly certain that the people's first and most natural attachment will be to their own state governments.

- More people will expect to rise into positions in state government.
- State governments will hand out more offices and salaries.
- State governments will handle and protect the more personal and everyday interests of the people.
- The people will know the affairs of their state governments more closely and in more detail.
- More of the people will have ties of personal friendship, family, and party with state officials.

So we can expect the public's loyalty to lean most strongly toward the states.

Experience says the same thing. The national government so far has been very flawed compared with what we can hope for under a better system. But during the war, especially while its paper money still held its value, it was as active and important as it could ever be in the future. It was also pursuing goals meant to protect everything precious to the people and to win everything they wanted.

Even so, after the short-lived enthusiasm for the early Congresses faded, people's attention and loyalty always turned back to their own state governments. Congress was never the darling of public favor. And politicians who wanted to build their careers on their neighbors' feelings usually opposed proposals to expand Congress's powers and importance.

People may someday come to favor the national government over the states. If so, it will only be because they see overwhelming proof that it governs better. And in that case, surely the people shouldn't be prevented from giving most of their trust where they find it most deserved. But even then, the state governments would have little to fear. By the nature of things, national power can only be used well within a certain range.

:::commentary
Madison's point about paper money refers to the "Continental" currency Congress printed during the Revolution. At first it worked, but Congress printed so much that it became nearly worthless by 1780. That's where the old saying "not worth a Continental" comes from.

Notice how fair Madison is here. He doesn't say people *must* always prefer the states. He says that if the national government earns more trust by governing better, people have every right to give it that trust. Over the next two centuries, especially during the Civil War, the New Deal of the 1930s, and the civil rights movement, many Americans did come to look to the national government for things they once expected from their states.
:::

## Members of Congress will bring local loyalties {orig=5-6}

The remaining points of comparison are the willingness and the ability of each government to resist and block the other's actions.

We've already shown that national officials will depend more on state officials than state officials will depend on them. We've also shown that the people, whom both depend on, will lean toward the states. To the extent these causes affect how each government feels about the other, the states clearly have the advantage.

But there's another very important reason the advantage lies with the states. The members of the national government will generally bring with them a bias in favor of the states. Members of state governments will rarely bring a bias in favor of the national government. A local spirit will certainly be much stronger among members of Congress than a national spirit will be among members of state legislatures.

Everyone knows that a large share of the mistakes state legislatures make come from members sacrificing the broad and lasting interests of the whole state to the narrow interests of their own county or district. If they don't think broadly enough to care for the welfare of their own state, how can we imagine they will make the prosperity of the whole Union, and the dignity and respect of its government, their main concern?

For the same reason that state lawmakers won't care enough about national goals, members of Congress will tend to care too much about local goals. The states will be to Congress what counties and towns are to state legislatures. Decisions will too often be made based on their likely effect, not on national prosperity and happiness, but on the prejudices, interests, and goals of the individual states and their people.

What has Congress generally been like? Its records, and the honest admissions of people who have served in it, show that its members have too often acted like partisans of their own states, rather than as fair guardians of a common interest. Local interests have sometimes been wrongly sacrificed to make the national government stronger. But for every such case, the nation has suffered a hundred times from too much attention to local interests.

I don't mean that the new national government won't follow a broader policy than the current one has, much less that its views will be as narrow as the state legislatures'. I only mean that it will share enough of both spirits to be unwilling to invade the rights of the states or the powers of their governments. Meanwhile, the state governments' motives to increase their powers by taking from the national government won't be held back by any similar bias among their members.

:::commentary
This prediction has largely come true. Members of Congress today are famously focused on their home states and districts. They fight for local projects, local industries, and local jobs. Former Speaker of the House Tip O'Neill summed it up: "All politics is local." Madison saw this as a protection for the states, though he also admits it can hurt the national interest.
:::

## How states can resist national overreach {orig=7-8}

But suppose we admitted that the national government might be just as eager as the states to stretch its power beyond proper limits. The states would still have the advantage in the means of defeating such attempts.

If a state passes a law that is unfriendly to the national government but popular in that state, and that doesn't too obviously violate state officials' oaths, it is carried out right away by state officials on the spot. Opposition from the national government, or interference by national officers, would only fire up everyone in the state on the state's side. The harm could hardly be prevented or fixed, if at all, without using measures that must always be taken reluctantly and with difficulty.

On the other hand, suppose a national measure is unpopular in particular states. That will almost always be true of an improper measure, and it may sometimes be true even of a proper one. Then the means to oppose it are powerful and close at hand:

- the people's unrest;
- their dislike of national officers, and perhaps their refusal to cooperate with them;
- the disapproval of the state's governor;
- obstacles created by the state legislature, which would often be added in such cases.

In any state, these would create difficulties that can't be dismissed. In a large state, they would be very serious obstacles. And where several neighboring states felt the same way, they would present obstructions the national government would hardly want to face.

But ambitious attacks by the national government on the states' authority wouldn't just stir up opposition from one state or a few. They would set off a general alarm. Every state government would take up the common cause. Letters would be exchanged. Plans of resistance would be coordinated. One spirit would inspire and guide them all. In short, fear of national oppression would produce the same alliances that fear of foreign oppression produced. And unless the national government gave up its plans, the same appeal to force would be made in this case as in the other.

But what kind of madness could ever drive the national government to such an extreme? In the struggle with Great Britain, one part of the empire was set against another. The larger part invaded the rights of the smaller part. The attempt was unjust and unwise, but it wasn't completely unimaginable in theory. In the case we're imagining, who would the two sides be? A few representatives of the people would be set against the people themselves. Or rather, one set of representatives would be fighting against thirteen sets of representatives, with all the people they jointly serve on the side of the thirteen.

:::vocab
Encroachment: Gradually taking over someone else's power or territory.
Interposition: Stepping in between. Later, some Southern leaders used "interposition" to claim states could block national laws they thought unconstitutional.
Chimerical: Imaginary; wildly unrealistic.
:::

:::commentary
Madison describes what political scientists today call **state resistance** or even **"uncooperative federalism."** States can refuse to help enforce national laws, file lawsuits, pass conflicting laws, and coordinate with other states. This happens often today, on issues from immigration to marijuana to environmental rules.

But this passage also has a darker history. In 1798, Madison and Jefferson wrote the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, claiming states could declare national laws unconstitutional. Later, leaders like John C. Calhoun stretched this into the theory of **nullification** (that a state could refuse to obey national law) and finally **secession**. In the 1950s and 60s, Southern segregationists used "interposition" to resist school desegregation. Madison himself, late in life, rejected nullification and secession. He said the states could protest and resist through legal means, but not cancel national law on their own.
:::

## Could a national army crush the states? {orig=9}

The only argument left for those who predict the destruction of the state governments is the fantasy that the national government might first build up a military force for its ambitious plans.

If it were still necessary to prove this danger isn't real, these essays would have been written for nothing. Think about what it would require. For a long enough time, the people and the states would have to keep electing, one after another, men ready to betray both. Those traitors would have to steadily and systematically follow a fixed plan to build up the army. And the state governments and the people would have to quietly and patiently watch the storm gathering, and keep supplying the materials, until it was ready to break over their own heads. To everyone, that must seem more like the confused dreams of crazed suspicion, or the exaggerations of fake concern, than the sober worries of a true patriot.

As unlikely as this is, let's assume it anyway. Let a regular army be formed, as large as the country's resources allow, and let it be completely loyal to the national government. Even then, it wouldn't be going too far to say that the state governments, with the people on their side, could defeat the danger.

According to the best estimates, the largest standing army any country can support is no more than one percent of its total population, or one twenty-fifth of the people able to carry weapons. In the United States, that would mean an army of no more than twenty-five or thirty thousand men. Against it would stand a militia of nearly half a million citizens with weapons in their hands. Their officers would be chosen from among themselves. They would be fighting for their shared liberty, united and led by governments they love and trust. It's doubtful that a militia like that could ever be conquered by so few regular troops. The people who know the most about our recent successful resistance against British forces will be the most likely to deny that it could.

Americans have the advantage of being armed, which the people of almost every other nation lack. On top of that, they have state governments they are attached to, which appoint the militia officers. That forms a barrier against ambitious plans that is harder to overcome than anything a single, unified government of any kind could offer.

The kingdoms of Europe have built up their armies as large as their resources allow, yet their governments are afraid to trust their people with weapons. And it's not certain that, with weapons alone, the people couldn't throw off their oppressors. Now imagine they also had local governments they chose themselves, which could unite the people and direct their strength. And imagine those governments appointed militia officers loyal to them and to the militia. Then we can say with great confidence that every tyrant in Europe would quickly be overthrown, despite his armies.

Let's not insult the free and brave citizens of America. They would be at least as able to defend the rights they have as the subjects of tyrants would be to win theirs back. And let's no longer insult them by imagining they would ever allow themselves to reach the point of having to try, by blindly and meekly accepting the long chain of sneaky measures that would have to come first.

:::quote
Besides the advantage of being armed, which the Americans possess over the people of almost every other nation, the existence of subordinate governments, to which the people are attached, and by which the militia officers are appointed, forms a barrier against the enterprises of ambition...

Meaning: Americans are unusually well protected against tyranny for two reasons: many of them own weapons, and they have state governments that organize the militia and appoint its officers.
:::

:::vocab
Militia: Ordinary citizens who are trained to serve as soldiers when needed, rather than full-time professional soldiers.
Standing army: A permanent, professional army kept even in peacetime.
Regular troops: Full-time professional soldiers.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the most frequently quoted passages in debates over the **Second Amendment**, which reads: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed." Madison wrote that amendment about a year and a half later.

People read this passage in different ways. Some emphasize "the advantage of being armed" and see it as support for an individual right to own weapons, a view the Supreme Court adopted in *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008). Others emphasize that Madison is describing **militias organized and officered by state governments**, and see the right as connected to that collective, organized defense.

It's also worth noticing how much the world has changed. Madison's math compared 25,000–30,000 soldiers armed with muskets against 500,000 citizens with similar muskets. Today's U.S. military has more than a million active-duty members and weapons no private citizen could match. That changes how his argument applies, though people disagree about what follows from it.
:::

## The argument in a nutshell {orig=10-11}

The argument on this point can be put in a very short form that seems completely convincing. Either the national government will be designed to depend enough on the people, or it won't. If it does, that dependence will keep it from forming plans the people would hate. If it doesn't, it won't have the people's trust, and the state governments, backed by the people, will easily defeat its schemes to grab power.

Add up everything in this essay and the last one. It seems clearly proven that the national government's proposed powers are absolutely necessary for the Union. And they pose little threat to the powers the states keep. All the alarms that have been raised about a planned destruction of the state governments must, in the kindest interpretation, be blamed on the imaginary fears of the people who raised them.

:::commentary
Madison's final argument is a neat logical trap. Either the government answers to the people (so it won't oppress them), or it doesn't (so the people won't support it, and the states will stop it). It's persuasive, but notice the assumption: that the people as a whole will always be on the side of liberty. Madison's own [No. 10](/papers/10/) worried that majorities could oppress minorities. A national government that depends on a majority might still oppress a minority, and the states might help rather than stop it, as Southern states did under slavery and segregation.
:::

:::today
Madison's prediction that people would feel closer to their states has partly faded. Today, many Americans know more about national politics than about their own state legislature, partly because national news dominates the media. Yet states remain powerful, and when the national government does something unpopular in a region, states often resist, just as Madison described. They sue in court, refuse to cooperate, and pass their own laws. Madison's idea that both governments are "agents and trustees of the people," and that the people decide how to divide their trust, remains one of the clearest ways to think about American federalism.
:::
