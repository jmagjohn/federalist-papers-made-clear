:::summary
Hamilton explains why the Constitution makes the Senate the court that tries impeachments. Impeachable offenses, he says, come from "the misconduct of public men," or the abuse of a public trust. Because they are political, these trials will stir up strong party feelings, and the danger is that the verdict will depend on which party is stronger rather than on guilt or innocence. The Senate is dignified, independent, and large enough to judge fairly between the accused and the House of Representatives, which brings the charges. The Supreme Court would be a poor choice, partly because a convicted official can still be tried in the regular courts afterward. Hamilton ends by reminding readers that no plan is perfect, and that a few flaws are no reason to reject the whole Constitution.
:::

:::keypoints
- Impeachment is for offenses that come from **the abuse or violation of some public trust**. Hamilton calls them **political** because they mainly injure society itself.
- Impeachment trials will divide the public into parties, so there is a real danger that **party strength, not the evidence**, will decide the outcome.
- Impeachment is a **national inquest** into the conduct of public officials, so it belongs to the people's representatives: the House accuses, and the Senate judges.
- The Senate is **dignified and independent** enough to judge fairly between one accused person and the House.
- The Supreme Court would lack the needed **courage and public authority**, and it is too small for such a weighty, loosely defined task.
- Conviction only **removes and disqualifies** an official from office. The person can still be **tried in the ordinary courts**, so the same judges should not handle both trials.
- Having the **chief justice preside** over a president's trial gives some benefits of the Court's involvement without the drawbacks.
- Even if a better plan could be imagined, that is no reason to reject the Constitution. No human plan is perfect.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues Hamilton's discussion of the Senate. [No. 62](/papers/62/) and No. 63 explained why a Senate was needed, and in No. 64 John Jay defended the Senate's share in making treaties. Now Hamilton turns to the Senate's role as a court. Under the Constitution, the House of Representatives has "the sole Power of Impeachment," meaning it can formally charge an official. The Senate has "the sole Power to try all Impeachments." Conviction requires a two-thirds vote of the senators present. The Constitution lists the grounds as "Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors."

The idea of impeachment came from England, where the House of Commons could charge the king's ministers and the House of Lords would try them. At the Convention, George Mason pointed to the impeachment of Warren Hastings, a British official in India, which was then under way in Parliament. Mason wanted to add "maladministration" (bad management) to the grounds for impeachment. James Madison objected that such a vague word would let the Senate remove officials whenever it pleased. The Convention settled on "high Crimes and Misdemeanors" instead. Critics of the Constitution worried that giving the Senate this power, on top of its roles in treaties and appointments, made the Senate too powerful. Hamilton answers some of those worries here and more of them in [No. 66](/papers/66/).
:::

## The Senate's last power: trying impeachments {orig=1}

The plan of the Convention gives the Senate two more powers of its own. First, it shares with the president the power to appoint officials. Second, it acts as a court to try impeachments. In making appointments, the president will be the main actor, so the rules about them are best discussed when we examine the executive branch. So I will finish this topic by looking at the Senate's role as a court.

:::vocab
Impeachment: The process of formally charging a government official with serious misconduct. If convicted, the official is removed from office.
:::

## Why impeachment trials are so hard to get right {orig=2-3}

In a government that is entirely elected, a well-designed court for trying impeachments is very much to be desired. But it is just as hard to create. Such a court deals with offenses that come from the misconduct of public men. In other words, they come from the abuse or violation of some public trust. These offenses can rightly be called POLITICAL, because they mainly involve injuries done directly to society itself.

For this reason, prosecuting them will almost always stir up the passions of the whole community. It will split people into groups that are more or less friendly or hostile to the accused. In many cases the prosecution will become tied up with factions that already exist. Then all their hatreds, loyalties, influence, and interests will be thrown onto one side or the other. In such cases there will always be a great danger that the decision will depend more on which party is stronger than on real proof of innocence or guilt.

This trust deeply affects the political reputation and career of every person who works in public affairs. Its delicacy and importance speak for themselves. It is also easy to see why it is hard to decide where to place it in a government based entirely on regular elections. In such a government, the most prominent people will too often be the leaders, or the tools, of the cleverest or the largest faction. For that very reason, they can hardly be expected to stay neutral toward the officials whose conduct is being examined.

:::quote
The subjects of its jurisdiction are those offenses which proceed from the misconduct of public men, or, in other words, from the abuse or violation of some public trust. They are of a nature which may with peculiar propriety be denominated POLITICAL, as they relate chiefly to injuries done immediately to the society itself.

Meaning: Impeachment is for officials who betray the trust the public has placed in them. These wrongs are "political" because the main victim is the whole society, not one person.
:::

:::quote
...there will always be the greatest danger that the decision will be regulated more by the comparative strength of parties, than by the real demonstrations of innocence or guilt.

Meaning: Hamilton warns that impeachment trials may be decided by party loyalty instead of by the facts.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the most quoted passages in all the Federalist Papers, because the Constitution never fully defines "high Crimes and Misdemeanors." Hamilton's description suggests that impeachable offenses are about the *abuse of public office*, and do not have to be crimes listed in the criminal law. Many scholars agree with this reading. Others argue that the phrase should be read more narrowly, closer to serious crimes, so that impeachment is not used simply because the majority dislikes an official's policies. This debate has come up in every modern presidential impeachment.

Hamilton's warning about parties also proved accurate. In practice, votes in presidential impeachment trials have often followed party lines, though not always perfectly.
:::

:::vocab
Jurisdiction: The area of authority of a government or court.
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
:::

## Why the Senate was chosen {orig=4-6}

The Convention, it appears, thought the Senate was the best place to put this important trust. Those who best understand how hard the problem is will be the slowest to condemn that choice. They will also be the most willing to give fair weight to the arguments that probably led to it.

What, we may ask, is the true purpose of impeachment? Isn't it meant to be a NATIONAL INQUEST, an investigation on behalf of the nation into the conduct of public officials? If so, who could be more proper investigators for the nation than the nation's own representatives?

No one disputes that the power to start the inquiry, that is, to bring the impeachment, should belong to one house of the legislature. Don't the same reasons strongly suggest that the other house should share in the inquiry too? The model that this institution was borrowed from pointed the Convention in that direction. In Great Britain, the House of Commons brings the impeachment, and the House of Lords decides it. Several state constitutions have followed this example. Both Britain and those states seem to have seen impeachment as a bridle, or restraint, that the legislature holds over the executive officers of the government. Isn't that the right way to see it?

Where else but in the Senate could we have found a court dignified enough, or independent enough? What other body would feel CONFIDENT ENOUGH IN ITS OWN POSITION to stay fair, without fear and without outside pressure, between an INDIVIDUAL who is accused and the REPRESENTATIVES OF THE PEOPLE, HIS ACCUSERS?

:::commentary
Hamilton's phrase "national inquest" explains why the Constitution splits the job between the two houses. The House works like a grand jury: it investigates and decides whether to bring charges (the "articles of impeachment"). The Senate works like a trial jury and judge combined: it hears the evidence and votes on whether to convict. Hamilton calls impeachment a "bridle" held by Congress over "the executive servants of the government," but it applies to federal judges too. In fact, the only officials the Senate has ever convicted and removed from office have been federal judges.
:::

:::vocab
Inquest: An official investigation, especially one meant to find out whether someone is to blame.
House of Lords: The upper house of Britain's Parliament, made up mostly of nobles and bishops in Hamilton's time.
Tribunal: A court.
:::

## Why not the Supreme Court? {orig=7}

Could the Supreme Court have been trusted to fit this description? It is very doubtful that its members would always have the great courage this hard task requires. It is even more doubtful that they would have enough public respect and authority. On some occasions, that authority might be essential to get the people to accept a verdict that went against charges brought by their own direct representatives. A lack of courage would be fatal to the accused. A lack of authority would be dangerous to the peace of the public. The only way to avoid both dangers, if they could be avoided at all, would be to make the Court much larger than a sensible concern for cost would allow.

The nature of an impeachment also requires a large court. It can never be tied down by strict rules the way ordinary cases are. In ordinary cases, the prosecutors must define the offense precisely, and the judges must interpret it strictly. These rules limit the judges' freedom in order to protect the individual. In an impeachment, there will also be no jury standing between the judges who pronounce the sentence and the person who must receive it. A court of impeachments must have an awesome power of judgment. It can bring either honor or disgrace to the most trusted and most distinguished people in the community. That kind of power should not be given to a small number of people.

:::commentary
Notice what Hamilton is admitting here. An impeachment trial is not like a normal criminal trial. The offenses are not defined precisely, and the senators act as both judge and jury. Hamilton sees this as a reason to give the job to a large body. Today the Senate has 100 members, compared with 9 Supreme Court justices.

The Supreme Court has agreed that the Senate has broad freedom here. In *Nixon v. United States* (1993), a case about a federal judge named Walter Nixon (not the president), the Court ruled that it would not review how the Senate chooses to conduct an impeachment trial, because the Constitution gives that power to the Senate alone.
:::

:::vocab
Fortitude: Courage in the face of pain, danger, or pressure.
:::

## A second trial would follow the first {orig=8}

These points alone seem enough to show that the Supreme Court would have been a poor substitute for the Senate as a court of impeachments. But one more point makes the case even stronger. The punishment for being convicted on impeachment does not end the offender's penalties. He will be sentenced to be shut out forever from the respect, trust, honors, and salaries of his country. After that, he can still be prosecuted and punished in the ordinary courts.

Would it be proper for the same people who had decided his reputation and his most valuable rights as a citizen in one trial to also decide his life and property in another trial for the same offense? Wouldn't there be every reason to fear that a mistake in the first verdict would lead to a mistake in the second? Wouldn't the strong bias of the first decision tend to outweigh any new evidence that might change how the second case looked? Anyone who knows anything about human nature will answer yes to these questions. They will easily see that if the same people judged both cases, the accused would largely lose the double protection that two separate trials are meant to give him. The loss of life and property would often be decided, in effect, by a sentence that on paper only removed him from his current office and barred him from holding office in the future.

Someone may say that a jury in the second trial would remove this danger. But juries are often influenced by the opinions of judges. Sometimes they are led to give "special verdicts," which leave the main question for the court to decide. Who would want to risk his life and property on the verdict of a jury guided by judges who had already decided he was guilty?

:::commentary
Hamilton is describing a key feature of the Constitution: "Judgment in Cases of Impeachment shall not extend further than to removal from Office, and disqualification" from holding office in the future. The Senate cannot fine or jail anyone. But the Constitution adds that the convicted official can still face "Indictment, Trial, Judgment and Punishment, according to Law" in the regular courts. Since the Supreme Court might hear the appeal in such a criminal case, Hamilton argues it should not also have decided the impeachment.
:::

:::vocab
Ostracism: Being shut out from a group or from society. In ancient Athens, it meant being banished from the city by a public vote.
Emolument: Salary or profit from holding an office.
Special verdict: A jury's finding on the facts of a case that leaves the final legal decision to the judge.
:::

## Why not combine the Court and the Senate? {orig=9}

Would it have improved the plan to join the Supreme Court with the Senate to form the court of impeachments? This would certainly have had several advantages. But wouldn't they be outweighed by the serious disadvantage I just described, of having the same judges take part in both trials the offender might face?

The plan of the Convention gets some of the benefits of joining the two bodies. It makes the chief justice of the Supreme Court the presiding officer of the court of impeachments. At the same time, it mostly avoids the problems of fully merging the Court into the Senate. This was perhaps the wise middle path. I won't comment on how loudly people would have complained about the judiciary if its power had been increased so much.

:::commentary
Hamilton slightly overstates the plan here. The Constitution says the chief justice presides only "When the President of the United States is tried," not in every impeachment. In other trials, the vice president or another senator presides. One reason often given is that the vice president, who normally presides over the Senate, would gain the presidency if the president were removed.

The chief justice has presided over the trials of Andrew Johnson (1868), Bill Clinton (1999), and Donald Trump's first trial (2020). Trump's second trial, in 2021, took place after he had left office, and a senator presided instead.
:::

:::vocab
Judiciary: The branch of government made up of courts and judges.
:::

## Why not a separate court? {orig=10}

Would it have been better to form the court for impeachments out of people completely separate from the other branches of government? There are weighty arguments both for and against such a plan. Some people will not think it a small objection that it would make the machinery of government more complicated. It would add a new part to the government, and that part's usefulness would be doubtful at best.

But here is an objection that no one will think unimportant. A court formed this way would either be very expensive, or it might suffer from many accidents and problems in practice. It would have to be made up of one of two kinds of members. They could be permanent officers who live at the seat of government and are paid regular salaries. Or they could be certain officials of the state governments, called in whenever an impeachment was actually pending. It is hard to imagine any sensible third option that would be very different.

For the reasons I have given, the court ought to be large. So the first plan will be rejected by anyone who compares the country's needs with its ability to pay for them. The second plan will be supported only with caution by anyone who seriously thinks about these problems:

- the difficulty of gathering men who are spread out over the whole Union;
- the harm to innocent people from long delays in deciding the charges against them;
- the advantage to guilty people from the chances for scheming and corruption that delay would give them;
- and in some cases, the harm to the state from keeping officials out of action for a long time. These might be officials whose firm and faithful performance of their duty had made them targets of an angry or scheming majority in the House of Representatives.

That last possibility may seem harsh, and it may not often come true. But we should not forget that the demon of faction will, at certain times, spread its rule over every large group of people.

:::commentary
Hamilton raises a point worth noticing: impeachment could be misused. A majority in the House might impeach an official not for real wrongdoing but because he had done his job well and made enemies. This is one reason the Constitution requires a two-thirds vote in the Senate to convict. It is much harder to reach than a simple majority.
:::

## No plan is perfect {orig=11}

But suppose that one of the alternatives I have examined, or some other plan, would be better than the Convention's plan on this point. It still would not follow that the Constitution should be rejected for that reason. If people decided to agree on no part of government until every part had been adjusted to perfection, society would soon fall into anarchy, and the world would become a desert.

Where can we find the standard of perfection? Who will try to bring the conflicting opinions of a whole community into the same judgment about it? Who will persuade one conceited planner to give up his INFALLIBLE standard for the FALLIBLE standard of his even more CONCEITED NEIGHBOR? To succeed, the opponents of the Constitution must prove more than that certain parts of it are not the best that could be imagined. They must prove that the plan as a whole is bad and harmful.

:::commentary
Hamilton often ends with this argument: the Constitution doesn't have to be perfect, only better than the alternatives. The sarcasm about "infallible" planners is aimed at critics who each had their own favorite fix, but who could never agree with one another on a single better plan.
:::

:::vocab
Anarchy: A condition with no effective government, where disorder rules.
Infallibility: Being incapable of making mistakes.
:::

:::today
The House has impeached three presidents: Andrew Johnson in 1868, Bill Clinton in 1998, and Donald Trump in 2019 and again in 2021. In each case the Senate voted to acquit, because the vote to convict fell short of two-thirds. Johnson's acquittal came by a single vote. In 1974, Richard Nixon resigned after the House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment against him, before the full House voted. In each of these cases, people argued about the questions Hamilton raised. What counts as an "abuse or violation of some public trust"? Must an impeachable offense be a crime? Did senators vote on the evidence or on party loyalty? Supporters of the impeachment process say it is an essential check on officials who abuse power. Critics worry that it can become just another weapon in party battles. Hamilton saw both sides of that danger in 1788.
:::
