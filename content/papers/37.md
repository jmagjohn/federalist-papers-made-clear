:::summary
Before examining the Constitution piece by piece, Madison asks readers to judge it fairly. No plan made by humans is perfect, and the Convention faced enormous difficulties. Its task was new, with no good model to copy. It had to combine **strong, stable government** with **liberty**. It had to draw a line between **national and state power**, which is hard because even nature and language don't have perfectly clear boundaries. And it had to settle conflicts between **large and small states** and between different regions. Given all this, Madison says, the amazing thing is not that the Constitution has flaws, but that the delegates agreed at all.
:::

:::keypoints
- Big public questions are rarely judged calmly. Some people decided to **condemn** the Constitution before reading it, and others to **praise** it.
- These essays are written for readers who care about their country and are willing to judge **fairly**.
- A **faultless plan** shouldn't be expected, and readers should remember that they can make mistakes too.
- The Convention's task was **new**. Past confederacies offered only warnings about what to avoid.
- It had to mix **energy and stability** with **liberty and republican government**, which pull in different directions.
- Drawing the line between **national and state authority** is extremely hard. Even nature, the human mind, and language lack precise boundaries.
- **Large and small states**, and different **regions**, had competing interests that required compromise.
- The fact that the delegates reached near-unanimous agreement is **astonishing**, perhaps even a sign of divine help.
:::

:::context Background
This essay begins the second half of *The Federalist*, which examines the Constitution in detail. Madison was uniquely qualified to write it. He had attended almost every session of the Constitutional Convention from May to September 1787 and took detailed notes, which were kept secret until after his death in 1836. He knew firsthand how close the Convention came to failing, especially during the fight over representation in July 1787.
:::

## Judging fairly {orig=1-3}

In reviewing the flaws of the current Confederation, and showing that they can't be fixed by a government less energetic than the one proposed, we have already considered several of the Constitution's most important principles. But the final goal of these essays is to judge clearly and fully the merits of the Constitution and whether we should adopt it. So our plan won't be complete without a closer and more thorough look at the Convention's work. We need to examine it from every side, compare all its parts, and estimate its likely effects. To help this remaining task lead to a fair result, some reflections are needed first.

It's an unavoidable misfortune in human affairs that public measures are rarely examined with the calm spirit needed to judge whether they will truly help or hurt the public good. And this spirit tends to shrink, not grow, on exactly the occasions that need it most.

People who have learned this from experience won't be surprised that the Convention's plan has stirred up attitudes, on both sides, that get in the way of fair discussion. The plan recommends so many important changes, can be seen from so many angles, and touches so many passions and interests.

It's clear from some people's own writings that they examined the Constitution not only expecting to criticize it, but having already decided to condemn it. Others' language shows the opposite bias, a decision in advance to support it. That makes their opinions worth little on the question too.

But in treating these two groups as equally unreliable, I don't mean to suggest their intentions are equally good. In fairness to the second group, everyone agrees our situation is critical and that something must be done. So someone who decided in advance to support the plan may have been moved by that concern, not only by bad motives. The person who decided in advance to oppose it can have had no excusable motive at all. The first group's intentions may be honest, or they may be wrong. The second group's can't be honest and must be wrong.

In truth, these essays aren't written for either group. They ask for the attention only of readers who combine a sincere desire for their country's happiness with a willingness to judge fairly how to achieve it.

Readers like that will examine the Convention's plan without looking for faults or exaggerating them. They will see that a perfect plan was never to be expected. And they won't just make allowances for the mistakes the Convention could make as a group of human beings. They will also remember that they themselves are only human, and shouldn't assume they can't be wrong when they judge the fallible opinions of others.

:::vocab
Candor: Honesty and fairness, especially in judging others.
Predetermination: Deciding something in advance, before looking at the evidence.
Fallibility: The ability to make mistakes.
Infallibility: The impossibility of being wrong.
:::

:::commentary
Madison asks for something every reader should practice: judge an argument on its merits, not on what you already believe. He admits that both sides include people who made up their minds in advance.

But notice that he isn't completely even-handed. He says a biased supporter might have good motives, since the country needs *something*, while a biased opponent "can have been governed by no venial motive whatever," meaning no excusable motive. That's a persuasive move disguised as fairness. An Anti-Federalist could easily flip it around.
:::

## A task with no model {orig=4-5}

Readers will also quickly see that, besides these reasons for fairness, we should make many allowances for the difficulties built into the task the Convention faced.

The first thing that strikes us is how new the task was. These essays have shown that the current Confederation is built on false principles. So we must change its foundation, and everything built on top of it. We have also shown that the other confederacies we could look to as examples were spoiled by the same false principles. They can only serve as lighthouses, warning us of the course to avoid, without showing the course to follow.

The most the Convention could do in that situation was to avoid the mistakes that past experience, in other countries and our own, pointed out. And it could provide a convenient way to correct its own mistakes as future experience reveals them.

:::commentary
The "convenient mode of rectifying their own errors" is the **amendment process** in Article V. The framers knew they would get some things wrong, so they built in a way to fix the Constitution without starting over. Under the Articles of Confederation, every single state had to agree to any change, which made reform almost impossible.
:::

## Combining strength with liberty {orig=6-7}

One of the most important difficulties the Convention faced must have been combining the stability and energy that government needs with the careful respect owed to liberty and to the republican form. If the delegates hadn't largely succeeded at this, they would have done a poor job of what they were appointed to do and what the public expected. Yet no one who understands the subject will deny that it was hard.

**Energy** in government is essential to protect against outside and inside dangers, and to carry out the laws promptly and well, which is part of the very definition of good government.

**Stability** in government is essential to the nation's reputation and the advantages that come with it. It is also essential to the peace of mind and confidence of the people, which are among the main blessings of civil society. Unpredictable, constantly changing laws are not only bad in themselves; the people hate them. We can say with confidence that the people of this country, who understand government well and care about its effects, will never be satisfied until something is done about the ups and downs and uncertainty of the state governments.

But compare these valuable ingredients with the vital principles of **liberty**, and you immediately see how hard it is to mix them in the right amounts.

The spirit of republican liberty seems to demand, on one side, not only that all power come from the people, but that those given power stay dependent on the people through short terms of office. Even during those short terms, power should be held not by a few people, but by many.

Stability, on the other hand, requires that the same people hold power for a long time. Frequent elections lead to frequent changes of people, and frequent changes of people lead to frequent changes of policy. And energy in government requires not only that power last a certain time, but that it be carried out by a single person.

How well the Convention succeeded at this will be clearer when we look at its work more closely. From this quick look, it must clearly have been a hard part of the job.

:::commentary
This is one of the clearest statements of the central problem of the Constitution. Liberty seems to call for **short terms** and **power spread among many people**, so officials stay close to the voters. Stability calls for **long terms**. Energy calls for **one person** in charge. These pull in opposite directions.

The Constitution's answer was to mix them. The House has short two-year terms (liberty). The Senate has six-year terms (stability). The presidency is held by one person (energy) for four years. Judges serve for life (stability and independence). Each branch leans toward a different value, and together they balance out.
:::

## Where does national power end and state power begin? {orig=8-11}

Drawing the right line between the authority of the national government and the authority of the state governments must have been just as hard. Everyone will see this difficulty to the degree that they're used to thinking carefully about large and complicated things.

Even the powers of the human mind have never been clearly separated and defined, despite the efforts of the sharpest philosophers. Sense, perception, judgment, desire, will, memory, and imagination blend into each other by such fine shades and small steps that their boundaries have escaped the most careful study. They remain a rich source of clever argument and debate.

The boundaries in nature are another example. The wisest and most hardworking scientists have never been able to trace with certainty the line that separates plant life from non-living matter, or where plant life ends and animal life begins. And there's even more uncertainty in how the things within each of these great areas of nature are sorted and classified.

In nature, all the lines are perfectly exact. They only seem blurry because our eyes are imperfect. But when we move from nature to human institutions, the fuzziness comes both from the thing itself and from our way of seeing it. So we must lower our expectations even further about what human wisdom can achieve.

Experience has taught us that no skill in the science of government has yet managed to separate and define its three great areas, legislative, executive, and judicial, with enough certainty. It hasn't even clearly defined the rights and powers of the two parts of a legislature. Questions come up every day that show how unclear these subjects are, and they puzzle the greatest experts in political science.

The experience of centuries, along with the combined work of the wisest legislatures and legal scholars, has been just as unsuccessful in marking out the subjects and limits of different bodies of law and different courts. Even in Great Britain, which has worked harder at precision than any other country, the exact reach of the common law, statute law, maritime law, church law, corporation law, and other local laws and customs still isn't clearly settled. The authority of its many courts is also a source of frequent and complicated disputes, which shows how unclear their limits are.

All new laws, however skillfully written and carefully debated, are considered somewhat unclear and uncertain until their meaning is worked out through a series of particular discussions and court decisions.

Besides the confusion that comes from complicated subjects and imperfect human minds, the tool we use to share our ideas creates another problem. Words exist to express ideas. Clarity requires not only that ideas be clearly formed, but that they be expressed in words that fit them exactly and only them. But no language has enough words and phrases for every complex idea, and every language has many words that can mean different things.

So however accurately things are separated in themselves, and however carefully the separation is thought through, the definition can still be made inaccurate by the inaccurate words used to express it. This unavoidable inaccuracy will be greater or smaller depending on how complicated and new the things being defined are. When the Almighty himself chooses to speak to humanity in its own language, his meaning, however clear it must be, is made dim and doubtful by the cloudy medium through which it is communicated.

So there are three sources of vague and imprecise definitions: unclear subjects, imperfect minds, and inadequate language. Any one of these produces some confusion. In marking the boundary between national and state authority, the Convention must have felt the full effect of all three.

:::quote
When the Almighty himself condescends to address mankind in their own language, his meaning, luminous as it must be, is rendered dim and doubtful by the cloudy medium through which it is communicated.

Meaning: Even if God spoke to people, human language would make the message unclear. So of course a constitution written by humans will have unclear spots. "Condescends" here means chooses to come down to a lower level.
:::

:::vocab
Liquidated (meaning): Made clear and settled. Madison uses this unusual word to describe how courts and practice gradually fix the meaning of unclear laws.
Perspicuity: Clearness in expression; being easy to understand.
Jurists: Legal scholars and judges.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Madison's most thoughtful passages, and it matters a great deal for how we read the Constitution today. He openly admits that the Constitution's words can't be perfectly precise. Language is limited, the subjects are complicated, and people's minds are imperfect. So the meaning of many parts will only become clear over time, through "a series of particular discussions and adjudications," meaning debates and court decisions.

This idea, sometimes called **"liquidation,"** shows that Madison expected the Constitution's meaning to be settled partly through practice. It's still part of the debate between people who emphasize the original meaning of the words and people who emphasize how the Constitution has been understood and applied over time.
:::

## Large states vs. small states, North vs. South {orig=12-13}

Add to these difficulties the competing claims of the larger and smaller states. We can safely assume the larger states argued for a share in the government fully matching their greater wealth and importance, and the smaller states held on just as hard to the equality they enjoy now. We can also assume neither side would give in completely, so the fight could only end in compromise.

It's also very likely that, once the ratio of representation was settled, the compromise itself sparked a new fight between the same sides. Each would try to shape the government and divide its powers in ways that boosted the branches where it had won the most influence. Some features of the Constitution support both of these guesses. And to the extent either one is true, it shows the Convention had to sacrifice theoretical neatness to outside pressures.

And it wasn't only the large and small states that lined up against each other on various points. Other alliances, based on differences in location and policy, must have created more difficulties. Each state can be divided into different districts, and its citizens into different classes, which produce competing interests and local jealousies. In the same way, the different parts of the United States are distinguished by many circumstances that have the same effect on a larger scale.

For reasons explained in an earlier essay, this variety of interests may have a healthy effect on how the government works once it is formed. But everyone must see that it had the opposite effect on the task of forming it.

:::commentary
Madison is being careful, since the Convention's debates were secret. When he says "we may well suppose," he actually *knew*. The large-versus-small-state fight nearly ended the Convention. The result was the Great Compromise: representation by population in the House and equal votes in the Senate. Afterward, small states pushed to give the Senate more power (over treaties and appointments), which fits Madison's description of a "fresh struggle."

The "different local position and policy" refers mainly to the conflict between Northern and Southern states, especially over slavery and trade. That conflict produced the three-fifths clause, the twenty-year protection of the slave trade, and the fugitive slave clause. Madison's mention of "a former paper" refers to [No. 10](/papers/10/), which argued that a variety of interests would protect against faction.
:::

## A remarkable achievement {orig=14-15}

Under all these pressures, would it be surprising if the Convention had been forced to depart from the artificial structure and neat symmetry that a clever theorist might give a constitution designed alone in his study or in his imagination?

The real wonder is that so many difficulties were overcome, and overcome with an agreement that was almost as unheard of as it was unexpected. No fair-minded person can think about this without sharing in the astonishment. And no religious person can think about it without seeing in it the finger of that Almighty hand which has so often and so clearly reached out to help us at the critical moments of the Revolution.

In an earlier essay, we noted the repeated failed attempts in the United Netherlands to reform the harmful and notorious flaws of their constitution. The history of almost every great gathering people have held to reconcile their disagreements, calm their jealousies, and adjust their interests is a history of factions, fights, and disappointments. It ranks among the darkest pictures of human weakness and wickedness. The few bright exceptions only remind us of the general rule, and by their brightness make the surrounding gloom look even darker.

When we think about the causes of these exceptions and apply them to our case, we are led to two important conclusions. First, the Convention must have been unusually free from the poisonous influence of party hostility, the disease that most often strikes and corrupts groups that meet to make decisions. Second, all the state delegations at the Convention were either satisfied with the final result, or were persuaded to accept it by a deep belief that private opinions and narrow interests had to be sacrificed to the public good, and by despair of seeing this need reduced by delays or new experiments.

:::quote
It is impossible for the man of pious reflection not to perceive in it a finger of that Almighty hand which has been so frequently and signally extended to our relief in the critical stages of the revolution.

Meaning: Madison suggests the Convention's success was so unlikely that religious people would see God's help in it, as they had during the Revolution.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's claim of near-unanimous agreement is a bit generous. Of the 55 delegates who attended the Convention, only 39 signed the Constitution. Some left early, and three who stayed to the end refused to sign: George Mason and Edmund Randolph of Virginia and Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts. Two of New York's three delegates walked out. But the vote was by state, and every state delegation present voted to approve the final document, which is what Madison means.

Benjamin Franklin made a similar point on the Convention's last day. He said that he didn't approve of every part of the Constitution, but he doubted he could expect a better one from any assembly of people with "all their prejudices, their passions, their errors of opinion, their local interests, and their selfish views." He urged every delegate to doubt "a little of his own infallibility" and sign.
:::

:::today
Madison's point about the limits of language still shapes every major constitutional debate. Phrases like "necessary and proper," "unreasonable searches," "cruel and unusual punishments," and "equal protection" don't define themselves. Courts, Congress, presidents, and the public have argued over their meaning for more than two centuries. Madison's warning about how hard it is to draw a clear line between national and state power has also proved true. Americans are still arguing about exactly where that line falls.
:::
