:::summary
Madison begins a careful review of the powers the Constitution gives the national government. He first sorts them into six groups, then starts with the most basic one: security against foreign danger. A nation must be able to declare war, raise armies, build a navy, and raise money. Since no constitution can limit how strong a nation's enemies become, it can't safely limit the forces a nation may use to defend itself. Madison admits that permanent armies are dangerous to liberty, but he says the Union itself is the best protection against them, along with the rule that Congress must vote on army funding at least every two years. He ends by rejecting the claim that the phrase "general welfare" gives Congress unlimited power.
:::

:::keypoints
- The Constitution can be judged two ways: by **how much power** it gives, and by **how that power is divided** among the branches. Nos. 41–46 look at the first.
- Every useful power can be abused. The real questions are whether a power is **necessary**, and how well it is **guarded** against abuse.
- Madison sorts the national powers into **six classes**. This paper covers the first: **security against foreign danger**.
- A government can't limit its own defenses when it can't limit the strength of its enemies. Rules that ignore **self-preservation** will just be broken.
- **Standing armies** are dangerous to liberty but may be necessary. A united America, protected by distance like Britain is by the sea, would rarely need a large one. A divided America would need many.
- Congress can fund the army for **no more than two years** at a time, and House members face election every two years.
- A **navy** is needed, especially for exposed places like New York, and it can't be turned against the people's liberties.
- The words **"general welfare"** don't give Congress unlimited power. The list of specific powers that follows explains and limits them.
:::

:::context Background
This is the first of six essays (Nos. 41–46) in which Madison reviews the powers the Constitution gives the national government. In the essays just before this, he discussed how hard the Convention's job was ([No. 37](/papers/37/)) and whether the plan was truly republican ([No. 39](/papers/39/)). Now he turns to the question opponents cared about most: was the new government simply being given too much power?

Hamilton had already defended the war and taxing powers at length in Nos. 23–36. So Madison often refers back to those essays and focuses on new objections. One of the strongest fears in 1788 was of a **standing army**. Many Americans believed, from British and European history, that a permanent army in peacetime was the classic tool of tyrants.
:::

## Two ways to judge the Constitution {orig=1-3}

The Constitution proposed by the Convention can be judged from two general points of view. The *first* concerns the total amount of power it gives the government, including the limits it places on the states. The *second* concerns how the government is built, and how this power is divided among its branches.

The first point of view raises two important questions:

1. Is any of the power given to the national government unnecessary or improper?
2. Taken all together, are these powers a danger to the authority left to the states?

Is the total power of the national government greater than it ought to be? That is the *first* question.

:::commentary
Madison is laying out a plan for the next several papers. Nos. 41–44 ask whether each power is necessary. Nos. 45–46 ask whether all these powers together threaten the states. Then Nos. 47–51 turn to the "second point of view," the structure of the government and the separation of powers.
:::

## Every power can be abused {orig=4}

Anyone who has fairly followed the arguments against the government's broad powers will have noticed something. The critics have thought very little about whether these powers are necessary means to a necessary goal. Instead they have dwelled on the drawbacks that always come mixed in with political benefits. They have dwelled on the possible abuses that come with every power or trust that can also be used for good.

This way of handling the subject can't fool the good sense of the American people. It may show off the writer's cleverness. It may open an endless field for speeches and fine phrases. It may inflame people who don't think and confirm the prejudices of people who think badly.

But calm, fair-minded people will see three things right away. Even the purest human blessings have some impurity mixed in. We must always choose, if not the lesser evil, then at least the *greater* good, not the *perfect* good. And in every political institution, a power to advance the public happiness includes some freedom of judgment that can be misused and abused.

So they will see that whenever a power is to be given, the first question is whether the power is necessary to the public good. If the answer is yes, the next question is how to guard as well as possible against turning that power against the public.

:::quote
...the choice must always be made, if not of the lesser evil, at least of the GREATER, not the PERFECT, good...

Meaning: No plan is perfect. The real choice is between better and worse options, not between a flawless plan and a flawed one.
:::

:::vocab
Alloy: A cheaper metal mixed into gold or silver. Madison uses it to mean that every good thing has some bad mixed in.
Discretion: The freedom to decide what to do in a situation.
:::

## Six kinds of powers {orig=5-6}

To judge this subject correctly, we should review the different powers given to the government of the Union. To make this easier, we can sort them into classes according to the goals they serve:

1. Security against foreign danger.
2. Regulating dealings with foreign nations.
3. Keeping harmony and proper dealings among the states.
4. Certain other goals that are useful to everyone.
5. Keeping the states from certain harmful acts.
6. Provisions that give all these powers their proper effect.

The powers in the *first* class are:

- declaring war and granting letters of marque,
- providing armies and fleets,
- regulating the militia and calling it into service,
- raising and borrowing money.

:::vocab
Letters of marque: Government licenses that allowed private ship owners, called privateers, to attack and capture enemy ships in wartime.
Militia: Ordinary citizens who are trained to serve as soldiers when needed, rather than full-time professional soldiers.
:::

:::commentary
These six classes organize the next four papers. No. 41 covers class 1. [No. 42](/papers/42/) covers classes 2 and 3. [No. 43](/papers/43/) covers class 4. [No. 44](/papers/44/) covers classes 5 and 6. It helps to keep this list in mind as you read them.
:::

## War, armies, and the logic of self-defense {orig=7-11}

Security against foreign danger is one of the most basic goals of any society. It is an openly stated and essential goal of the American Union. The powers needed to achieve it must be fully trusted to the national government.

Is the power to declare war necessary? No one will say no. So there is no need to prove that it is. The present Confederation already gives this power in the fullest form.

Is the power to raise armies and equip fleets necessary? It is included in the power to declare war. It is included in the power of self-defense.

But was it necessary to give an *unlimited* power to raise *troops*, as well as provide fleets? And to keep both in *peace* as well as in *war*?

These questions have already been answered too fully in earlier papers to need a long discussion here. Indeed, the answer seems so obvious and conclusive that it hardly needs discussion anywhere. How could anyone sensibly limit the force needed for *defense*, when he cannot limit the force that may be used to *attack*? If a federal constitution could chain the ambition of every other nation, or limit how hard they push, then it might wisely chain its own government's judgment and limit what it may do for its own safety.

:::commentary
The "earlier papers" are Hamilton's Nos. 23–29. In [No. 23](/papers/23/), Hamilton made the same point: because no one can predict the size of future dangers, there should be no fixed limit on the power to meet them.

Notice one important difference from the Articles of Confederation. Under the Articles, Congress could declare war but could only *ask* the states for soldiers and money. The Constitution lets Congress raise armies and taxes directly.
:::

## A danger that may also be a necessity {orig=12-13}

How could we safely forbid preparing for war in peacetime, unless we could also forbid every hostile nation from preparing? The means of security can only be measured by the means and the danger of attack. In fact, they will always be decided by these rules and no others.

It is useless to put constitutional barriers in the way of the drive for self-preservation. It is worse than useless. It plants in the Constitution itself grabs of power that will become necessary. And every one of those grabs becomes a precedent that grows into more, and less necessary, repetitions.

If one nation keeps a trained army at all times, ready to serve its ambition or revenge, it forces even the most peaceful nations within its reach to take similar precautions. The 1400s were the unhappy age when peacetime armies began. King Charles VII of France introduced them. All of Europe has followed his example, or been forced into it. If the other nations had not followed it, all of Europe would long ago have been chained under one universal king. If every nation except France disbanded its peacetime army today, the same thing might happen. The veteran legions of Rome were more than a match for the untrained courage of all other nations. That made Rome the ruler of the world.

But it is just as true that Rome's liberties were the final victims of its military victories. And Europe's liberties, as far as they ever existed, have with few exceptions been the price of its armies. So a standing army is dangerous, even though it may also be necessary. On the smallest scale, it causes problems. On a large scale, its effects may be deadly. On any scale, it deserves careful watching and precaution.

A wise nation will weigh all of this. It will not foolishly shut itself off from any resource that may become essential to its safety. But it will use all its wisdom to reduce both the *need* for, and the *danger* of, a resource that may threaten its liberties.

:::quote
It is in vain to oppose constitutional barriers to the impulse of self-preservation.

Meaning: If a constitution forbids a nation from defending itself properly, the rule will simply be broken when danger comes. And once broken, it sets a bad example for breaking other rules.
:::

:::vocab
Standing army: A permanent, professional army kept even in peacetime.
Precedent: An earlier action or decision that is used as an example or rule for later ones.
Legion: A large unit of soldiers in the ancient Roman army.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's point about Rome was familiar to every educated reader in 1788. Rome's armies conquered the Mediterranean world, but ambitious generals like Julius Caesar later used those armies to overthrow the Roman Republic. Many Americans also remembered British troops stationed in Boston before the Revolution. The Declaration of Independence complained that the king had kept "standing armies" among the colonists "without the consent of our legislatures."

Madison's argument here is subtle. A rule that a nation must break in an emergency is worse than no rule at all, because breaking it teaches the government that rules can be ignored.
:::

## The Union is the best protection against armies {orig=14-15}

The proposed Constitution shows the clearest signs of this wisdom. The Union itself, which the Constitution holds together and protects, removes every excuse for a dangerous army. A united America, with a handful of troops or without a single soldier, looks more threatening to foreign ambition than a divided America with a hundred thousand battle-ready veterans.

It was noted in an earlier paper that the lack of such an excuse has saved the liberties of one European nation. Great Britain is an island with a strong navy, so the armies of its neighbors can't reach it. Because of that, its rulers have never been able to use real or invented dangers to trick the public into accepting a large peacetime army. The distance of the United States from the world's powerful nations gives us the same lucky security. A dangerous army can never be necessary, or even seem believable, as long as we stay a united people.

But never forget, even for a moment, that we owe this advantage to the Union alone. The day the Union breaks apart will begin a new order of things. The fears of the weaker states or confederacies, or the ambition of the stronger ones, will set the same example in the New World that Charles VII set in the Old. Others will follow it here for the same reasons they followed it there.

Instead of enjoying the precious advantage Britain gets from its position, America will become a copy of the European continent. It will show liberty crushed everywhere between standing armies and endless taxes. In fact, a divided America would suffer even worse than Europe. Europe's troubles come only from within its own borders. No stronger powers from another part of the world plot among Europe's rival nations, stir up their hatreds, and use them as tools of foreign ambition, jealousy, and revenge. In America, the misery caused by our own jealousies, quarrels, and wars would be only part of our fate. Many more evils would come from Europe's relationship to our part of the world, a relationship no other part of the world has with Europe.

This picture of what disunion would bring can't be painted too vividly or shown too often. Every man who loves peace, every man who loves his country, every man who loves liberty, should keep it always before his eyes. Then he will feel a proper attachment to the Union of America, and know how much to value the means of preserving it.

:::commentary
This is one of Madison's strongest arguments, and it repeats a theme from Hamilton's [No. 8](/papers/8/). If the states split apart, they would arm against each other, the way European nations did. Then every state would have its own standing army, and liberty would suffer everywhere. On this view, the Union isn't a threat to liberty. It is liberty's best defense.

His point about Europe also proved accurate in one way. In the decades after 1788, European powers like Britain, France, and Spain still held territory around the United States, and the young country was pulled into their conflicts, most seriously in the War of 1812.
:::

## The two-year limit on army funding {orig=16-18}

Next to the Union itself, the best possible protection against standing armies is a limit on how long money can be set aside to support them. The Constitution wisely includes this protection. I won't repeat the points made in earlier papers, which I hope have explained this subject fairly and fully.

But I should mention one argument against this part of the Constitution that critics draw from British practice. They say that in Britain, keeping an army requires a vote of the legislature every year, while the American Constitution stretches this critical period to two years. That is how the comparison is usually presented to the public. But is it fair? Does the British constitution limit Parliament to one year? Does the American Constitution *require* Congress to fund the army for two years? Just the opposite. The people making this argument must know that the British constitution puts *no limit at all* on Parliament. And the American Constitution ties Congress to two years as the *longest* term allowed.

If the British example were stated honestly, it would go like this. The British constitution puts no limit on how long Parliament may fund the army. Yet in practice, Parliament has chosen to limit it to one year at a time. Consider what that means. In Britain, the House of Commons is elected for seven years. A large share of its members are elected by a tiny share of the people. The voters are corrupted by their representatives, and the representatives are corrupted by the Crown. Yet even there, the representatives can fund the army for an unlimited time, and they don't want to, or don't dare, go beyond one year.

So shouldn't suspicion itself blush at claiming that American representatives can't be trusted with this decision? They will be elected *freely*, by the *whole body* of the people, every *second year*. And their power will be expressly limited to the short period of *two years*.

A bad cause almost always gives itself away. The opposition to the national government is a constant example of this truth. Of all their blunders, none stands out more than their attempt to recruit the people's wise distrust of standing armies to their side. That attempt has fully awakened public attention to the subject. And the investigations it has started must end in a complete and general conviction of two things.

First, the Constitution provides the most effective guards against danger from armies. Second, only a Constitution fully able to defend the nation and preserve the Union can save America from having as many standing armies as the number of states or confederacies it splits into. Without it, each of those armies would keep growing. In time they would be heavier on the people's property and more threatening to their liberties than any army a united and effective government might need. Under such a government, any army that became necessary would be bearable for the people's property and safe for their liberties.

:::vocab
Appropriation: A decision by a legislature to set aside money for a specific use.
House of Commons: The elected house of the British Parliament.
The Crown: The British king and his government.
:::

:::commentary
Article 1, section 8 says that no money for the army "shall be for a longer term than two years." This means every newly elected Congress must decide again whether to keep paying for the army. The limit doesn't apply to the navy, which was seen as less of a threat to liberty at home.

Madison's facts about Britain were accurate for his time. Since 1716, British law had allowed the House of Commons to sit for up to seven years between elections. Many seats belonged to tiny districts, sometimes called "rotten boroughs," with only a handful of voters. And Parliament had made a habit of approving the army one year at a time.
:::

## Why America needs a navy {orig=19-20}

The need for a power to build and keep a navy is so obvious that this part of the Constitution has escaped the criticism that has spared few other parts. It must be counted among America's greatest blessings that the Union will be the only source of its strength at sea, and that strength at sea will be a main source of its security against foreign danger. In this way, our situation is again like Britain's advantage as an island. Luckily, the defenses best able to drive off foreign attacks are ones that a treacherous government can never turn against our liberties.

The people who live along the Atlantic coast all have a deep stake in this naval protection. So far, they have been allowed to sleep quietly in their beds. Their property has stayed safe from lawless raiders. Their seaport towns have not yet had to pay ransom to bold, sudden invaders to avoid being burned. But this good luck is not thanks to the present government's ability to protect the people who owe it loyalty. It comes from causes that are temporary and can't be trusted.

Except perhaps for Virginia and Maryland, whose eastern coasts are especially exposed, no part of the Union should feel more anxious about this than New York. New York's coastline is long. A very important part of the state is an island. A large river that ships can sail runs into the state for more than fifty leagues, about 150 miles. The great center of its trade, the great storehouse of its wealth, lies at the mercy of events at every moment. It could almost be seen as a hostage, forcing the state to give in shamefully to the orders of a foreign enemy, or even to the greedy demands of pirates and barbarians.

If the unsteady state of affairs in Europe leads to war, all the wild passions of war will be let loose on the ocean. Then it will truly be a miracle if we escape insults and raids, both at sea and on the land along it. Right now, the states most exposed to these disasters have nothing to hope for from the ghost of a national government that now exists. And even if each state had enough resources to fortify itself, the cost of protecting what it has would almost eat up the thing being protected.

:::vocab
Navigable: Deep and wide enough for ships to travel on.
League: An old measure of distance, about three miles.
Emporium: A major center of trade.
:::

:::commentary
The "important district" that is an island is Manhattan, and the river is the Hudson. Madison is speaking directly to his New York readers, reminding them that New York City, their main source of wealth, could be attacked from the sea.

His mention of "pirates and barbarians" had a real basis. In the 1780s, pirates from the Barbary States of North Africa were capturing American merchant ships and holding their crews for ransom, and the Confederation had no navy to stop them. Under the Constitution, Congress passed a law in 1794 to build new warships, the start of the modern U.S. Navy.
:::

## The militia and the power to raise money {orig=21-22}

The power to regulate the militia and call it into service has already been fully defended and explained.

The power to raise and borrow money is the muscle behind the power of national defense, so it belongs in the same class. This power has also been carefully examined already. I hope it has been clearly shown to be necessary, in the extent and the form the Constitution gives it.

I will add just one more thought, for those who argue that the power should have been limited to *external* taxation, by which they mean taxes on goods imported from other countries. No doubt this will always be a valuable source of money. For a long time it must be a main source, and right now it is an essential one. But we could form very wrong ideas if we forget two things. The amount of money raised from foreign trade will change as the amount and kind of imports change. And those changes won't match the growth of the population, which is the general measure of what the public needs.

As long as farming is the only kind of work, imports of manufactured goods will grow as the number of consumers grows. But once people who aren't needed on farms start making goods at home, imports of manufactured goods will shrink even as the population grows. At a later stage, imports may consist largely of raw materials, which will be made into goods for export. Those imports should be encouraged with bounties, not burdened with taxes that discourage them. A system of government meant to last ought to foresee these changes and be able to adjust to them.

:::vocab
Bounty: A payment from the government to encourage some activity, such as producing a certain good.
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton defended the militia power in [No. 29](/papers/29/) and the taxing power in Nos. 30–36, including [No. 33](/papers/33/).

Madison's prediction about trade is a good example of long-range thinking. He saw that America might one day become a manufacturing nation, and that taxes on imports alone might not pay the government's bills. For most of the 1800s, taxes on imports were in fact the national government's main source of money. That changed after the 16th Amendment (1913) allowed a national income tax.
:::

## Does "general welfare" mean unlimited power? {orig=23-26}

Some critics who admit that the taxing power is necessary have launched a fierce attack on the words used to describe it. They have repeated again and again that the power "to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts, and excises, to pay the debts, and provide for the common defense and general welfare of the United States" amounts to unlimited permission to use any power that someone claims is needed for the common defense or general welfare. Nothing shows more clearly how desperate these writers are for objections than their stooping to such a twisted reading.

If the Constitution listed and defined no other powers of Congress except these general words, the critics might have some excuse. Even then, it would be hard to explain why anyone would describe a power to make laws on every possible subject in such an awkward way. A power to destroy freedom of the press or trial by jury, or even to control how property is inherited or sold, would be very strangely described by the words "to raise money for the general welfare."

But what excuse can the objection have when a list of the specific goals these general words refer to follows right after them, separated by nothing more than a semicolon? When we explain the different parts of the same document, we should give meaning to every part that can bear it. So should one part of the same sentence be cut out of the meaning entirely? Should the vaguer, more uncertain words keep their widest possible meaning, while the clear and precise words are given no meaning at all? Why would anyone include a list of particular powers, if those and all others were already meant to be included in the general power before them?

Nothing is more natural or common than to use a general phrase first, and then explain and narrow it with a list of particulars. But the idea of a list that neither explains nor narrows the general meaning, and can only confuse and mislead, is absurd. We are left to blame this absurdity either on the critics or on the authors of the Constitution. We will take the liberty of assuming it didn't come from the authors.

The objection is even stranger because the Convention's wording is copied from the Articles of Confederation. Article 3 describes the goals of the union among the states as "their common defense, security of their liberties, and mutual and general welfare." Article 8 is even closer: "All charges of war and all other expenses that shall be incurred for the common defense or general welfare, and allowed by the United States in Congress, shall be defrayed out of a common treasury." Similar words appear again in Article 9.

Read either of these articles by the rules the critics apply to the new Constitution, and they give the present Congress a power to make laws in all cases whatsoever. But what would people have thought of the Congress if it had seized on these general phrases, ignored the specific lists that define and limit them, and used an unlimited power to provide for the common defense and general welfare? I ask the critics themselves: would they have used the same reasoning to defend Congress that they now use against the Convention? How hard it is for error to avoid condemning itself!

:::vocab
General welfare clause: The opening words of Article 1, section 8, which give Congress the power to tax "to pay the debts and provide for the common defense and general welfare of the United States."
Enumerated powers: The specific powers listed in the Constitution, mainly in Article 1, section 8.
Defrayed: Paid for.
:::

:::quote
Nothing is more natural nor common than first to use a general phrase, and then to explain and qualify it by a recital of particulars.

Meaning: The general words "common defense and general welfare" are explained by the specific list of powers that follows. They don't add new powers of their own.
:::

:::commentary
This passage started one of the longest-running debates in American law. Madison's view is that "general welfare" is just a heading for the list of specific powers that follows. Congress can tax and spend only to carry out those listed powers.

Hamilton later took a broader view. As secretary of the treasury, he argued in the early 1790s that Congress could tax and spend for *anything* that served the general welfare, even if it wasn't on the list, as long as it was truly national and not local. In *United States v. Butler* (1936), the Supreme Court sided with Hamilton's view of the spending power. That ruling is a big reason the national government today spends money on so many programs not named in the Constitution, from highways to school lunches.

Note that the Court did *not* adopt the critics' reading that Madison attacks here. "General welfare" limits what Congress can spend money on. It is not a separate power to pass any law at all. Supporters of the broad spending power say it lets the nation meet needs the founders couldn't foresee. Critics say it lets Congress use money to control matters the Constitution left to the states.
:::

:::today
Two questions from this paper are still debated. The first is about war. The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war, but the United States has kept a large standing army since World War II, and presidents have sent troops into many conflicts without a formal declaration. Some people say this shows Madison's point that rules can't hold back the demands of national security. Others say it shows why his warnings about standing armies still matter. The second is about the "general welfare." Every time people argue about whether a national program is something Congress should be paying for, they are carrying on the debate Madison and Hamilton began.
:::

:::questions
- Madison says we must choose "the GREATER, not the PERFECT, good." Is that a fair way to judge a constitution, or is it an excuse for accepting flaws?
- Why did Madison think a united America would need a *smaller* army than a divided one? Do you find the argument convincing?
- The Constitution limits army funding to two years at a time. Does a limit like that still protect liberty today? Why or why not?
- Should "general welfare" be read narrowly, as Madison argues here, or broadly, as Hamilton later argued? What are the risks of each?
