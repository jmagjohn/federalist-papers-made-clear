:::summary
Opponents warned that the president would be a king in disguise. Hamilton answers by comparing the president's powers, point by point, with those of the British king and the governor of New York. The president is elected for four years, while the king rules for life and passes the crown to his heirs. The president can be impeached and prosecuted, while the king is above the law. The president's veto can be overridden, while the king's can't. The president commands the military but can't declare war or raise armies. He shares treaty-making and appointments with the Senate. Hamilton concludes that the president has about as much power as New York's governor, and far less than a king.
:::

:::keypoints
- One person holding executive power doesn't make the president a king. So do New York's governor and many others.
- **Term:** The president serves four years and must be re-elected. The king inherits the crown for life.
- **Accountability:** The president can be **impeached, removed, and prosecuted**. The king's person is "sacred and inviolable."
- **Veto:** The president's veto can be **overridden** by two-thirds of Congress. The king's is absolute.
- **Military:** The president commands the armed forces, but only **Congress** can declare war and raise armies. The king can do all of these.
- **Pardons:** The president can't pardon in impeachment cases. New York's governor's pardon power is in some ways broader.
- **Treaties and appointments:** The president needs the **Senate**. The king acts alone.
- The king can also grant titles of nobility, regulate trade and money, and heads the national church. The president can do **none** of these.
:::

:::context Background
Hamilton's earlier essay, [No. 67](/papers/67/), complained that opponents had painted the presidency in frightening colors, describing the president as a monarch surrounded by guards and royal splendor. One Anti-Federalist writer, "Cato" (probably New York's own governor, George Clinton), argued that the president would become a king. Since Americans had just fought a war against King George III, this was a powerful fear. In this essay, Hamilton goes through the Constitution's actual provisions on the presidency one by one.
:::

## The president is one person, but so are many others {orig=1-2}

I'll now trace the real features of the proposed executive, as the Convention's plan describes them. This will clearly show how unfair the descriptions of it have been.

The first thing we notice is that executive authority, with few exceptions, will be given to a single official. But this hardly provides grounds for any comparison. If it makes him resemble the king of Great Britain, it makes him just as much like the Grand Seignior, the khan of Tartary, the Man of the Seven Mountains, or the governor of New York.

:::vocab
Grand Seignior: A European name for the sultan of the Ottoman Empire, centered in modern Turkey.
Khan of Tartary: The ruler of the Tatar peoples of Central Asia.
Man of the Seven Mountains: The pope, since Rome is built on seven hills.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is being playful. Saying the president resembles a king because both are one person is like saying a school principal resembles an emperor because both are in charge. Lots of very different leaders hold power alone. The real question is how much power they have and how they can be checked.
:::

## Elected for four years, not born to rule {orig=3}

The president will be elected for four years. He can be re-elected as often as the people of the United States think he deserves their trust. In this, he is completely different from the king of Great Britain, who is a hereditary monarch. The king owns the crown as family property, to be passed down to his heirs forever.

But the president closely resembles the governor of New York, who is elected for three years and can be re-elected without limit or break. Consider how much less time it would take to build up a dangerous influence in a single state than across the whole United States. Then we must conclude that a four-year term for the head of the Union is far less to be feared than a three-year term for the same office in a single state.

:::commentary
The original Constitution put no limit on how many times a president could be re-elected. George Washington set the custom of serving only two terms, and every president followed it until Franklin D. Roosevelt won four elections (1932–1944). After that, the **22nd Amendment** (1951) limited presidents to two terms. Hamilton defends unlimited re-election in [No. 72](/papers/72/).
:::

## The president can be removed and punished {orig=4}

The President of the United States could be impeached, tried, and, if convicted of treason, bribery, or other high crimes or misdemeanors, removed from office. After that, he could be prosecuted and punished in the ordinary courts.

The person of the king of Great Britain is sacred and untouchable. There is no constitutional court he must answer to, and no punishment he can face without setting off a national revolution.

On this delicate and important point of personal responsibility, the president would stand on no better ground than the governor of New York. And he would stand on worse ground than the governors of Maryland and Delaware.

:::quote
The person of the king of Great Britain is sacred and inviolable; there is no constitutional tribunal to which he is amenable...

Meaning: In Britain, no court could put the king on trial. The president, by contrast, can be impeached and then prosecuted like anyone else.
:::

:::vocab
Impeach: To formally charge a public official with serious misconduct. The House impeaches; the Senate holds the trial.
High crimes and misdemeanors: The Constitution's phrase for impeachable offenses besides treason and bribery. Its exact meaning is still debated, but it generally means serious abuse of public office.
Inviolable: Protected from any attack or punishment.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton says a president removed by impeachment "would afterwards be liable to prosecution and punishment in the ordinary course of law." Whether a *sitting* president can be criminally prosecuted, and how far a former president is protected from prosecution for official acts, have become major legal questions. The Justice Department has long held that a sitting president can't be indicted. In *Trump v. United States* (2024), the Supreme Court ruled that former presidents have some immunity from prosecution for official acts. People continue to debate how that fits with Hamilton's description here.
:::

## A veto that can be overridden {orig=5}

The president will have power to send back a bill that both houses of Congress have passed, for reconsideration. The returned bill becomes law anyway if, after reconsidering it, two-thirds of both houses approve it.

The king of Great Britain, on the other hand, has an absolute veto over acts of Parliament. He hasn't used it for a long time, but that doesn't mean it doesn't exist. The reason he hasn't used it is entirely that the crown has found a way to use influence instead of authority. It has learned the art of winning a majority in one house or the other, so it rarely needs to use a power that couldn't be used without risking some national unrest.

The president's limited veto is very different from the British king's absolute veto. It matches exactly the power of review held by New York's council of revision, of which the governor is a member. In this respect, the president's power would be greater than New York's governor's, since the president would hold alone what the governor shares with the chancellor and judges. But it would be exactly the same as the power of the governor of Massachusetts, whose constitution seems to have been the model the Convention copied.

:::commentary
The last time a British monarch vetoed an act of Parliament was in 1708, under Queen Anne. Hamilton's point is that the king didn't need the veto, because he could control Parliament by handing out jobs, pensions, and favors to its members. Americans called this "corruption."

New York's **council of revision** was unusual. The governor, the chancellor, and the judges of the state supreme court together reviewed every bill and could send it back. The U.S. Constitution gave this power to the president alone.
:::

## Commander in chief, but Congress declares war {orig=6}

The Constitution gives the president these powers:

- to be "commander-in-chief of the army and navy of the United States, and of the militia of the several States, when called into the actual service of the United States";
- to grant reprieves and pardons for offenses against the United States, except in cases of impeachment;
- to recommend to Congress whatever measures he thinks necessary and useful;
- to call both houses of Congress, or either one, into session on special occasions, and to adjourn them if they disagree about when to adjourn;
- to make sure the laws are faithfully carried out;
- to commission all officers of the United States.

In most of these, the president's power will resemble the British king's and the New York governor's equally. The most important differences are these.

**First, the militia.** The president will only command the militia occasionally, and only the part of the nation's militia that Congress calls into actual national service. The king of Great Britain and the governor of New York always have full command of all the militia within their territory. So in this, the president's power would be less than either the king's or the governor's.

**Second, the army and navy.** The president will be commander in chief of the army and navy of the United States. In name, this is the same as the king's authority. But in substance, it is far less. It means nothing more than the supreme command and direction of the military and naval forces, as the Confederacy's top general and admiral. The British king's power extends to declaring war and to raising and regulating navies and armies. Under this Constitution, all of those powers would belong to Congress.

New York's governor, on the other hand, only commands the state's militia and navy. But several state constitutions explicitly name their governors commanders in chief of the army as well as the navy. It's fair to ask whether the constitutions of New Hampshire and Massachusetts, in particular, give their governors more power here than a president of the United States could claim.

**Third, pardons.** The president's pardon power would cover all cases except impeachments. The governor of New York can pardon in all cases, even impeachments, except for treason and murder.

Isn't the governor's power here, considering its political effects, greater than the president's? Any conspiracy or plot against the government that hasn't yet become actual treason could be shielded from every kind of punishment by a pardon. So if a governor of New York led such a conspiracy, he could guarantee his accomplices and followers complete freedom from punishment until the plot became open warfare.

A president, by contrast, could pardon even treason when prosecuted in the ordinary courts. But he couldn't shield anyone from the effects of impeachment and conviction.

Wouldn't the hope of complete protection for all the early steps of a plot be a greater temptation to start and continue an attack on public liberty than the mere hope of escaping death and the loss of property if the final armed attempt failed? And would that second hope matter at all, once plotters considered that the person who would grant the pardon might himself be caught up in the plot, and might be unable to protect them? To judge this, remember that the Constitution limits treason to "levying war upon the United States, and adhering to their enemies, giving them aid and comfort." New York's laws define it in a similar way.

**Fourth, adjourning the legislature.** The president can only adjourn Congress in one case: when the two houses disagree about when to adjourn. The British monarch can suspend Parliament or even dissolve it entirely. The governor of New York can also suspend the state legislature for a limited time, a power that can be used for very important purposes in certain situations.

:::quote
It would amount to nothing more than the supreme command and direction of the military and naval forces, as first General and admiral of the Confederacy; while that of the British king extends to the declaring of war and to the raising and regulating of fleets and armies--all which, by the Constitution under consideration, would appertain to the legislature.

Meaning: The president runs the military once it exists, but only Congress can declare war and create armies and navies.
:::

:::vocab
Commander in chief: The top commander of the armed forces. Under the Constitution, this is the president.
Reprieve: A delay in carrying out a punishment.
Pardon: An official act forgiving a crime and canceling its punishment.
Prorogue: To end or suspend a session of a legislature without dissolving it.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's description of the commander-in-chief power is much narrower than how it has worked in modern times. Congress has formally declared war only eleven times, in five wars, the last time in World War II. Since then, presidents have sent troops into major conflicts, including Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, without a declaration of war, sometimes with congressional authorization and sometimes without it. In 1973, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution to limit this, but presidents of both parties have argued it is unconstitutional. Many scholars cite this passage to argue that the modern presidency has gone far beyond what Hamilton promised.

Hamilton's argument about pardons is subtle. He worries that a governor who can pardon everyone involved in a plot *before* it becomes treason could protect his own co-conspirators. The president can't stop impeachment, so he couldn't fully protect officials who abuse power with him. Whether a president can pardon himself has never been tested in court.
:::

## Treaties need the Senate {orig=7}

The president will have power, with the advice and consent of the Senate, to make treaties, as long as two-thirds of the senators present agree.

The king of Great Britain is the sole and absolute representative of the nation in all foreign affairs. He can, on his own, make treaties of peace, trade, alliance, and every other kind. Some have hinted that his authority here isn't final, and that his agreements with foreign powers must be reviewed and approved by Parliament. But I believe no one ever heard of this idea until it was invented for this debate. Every legal scholar in that kingdom, and everyone who knows its constitution, knows it as an established fact that the power to make treaties belongs completely to the crown. Agreements made by royal authority are fully legal and complete without anyone else's approval.

It's true that Parliament is sometimes seen changing existing laws to fit the terms of a new treaty. This may have given rise to the idea that its cooperation was needed for the treaty to be binding. But Parliament steps in for a different reason. It has to adjust a very artificial and complicated system of tax and trade laws to the changes the treaty makes, and add new provisions to fit the new situation, to keep the machine from breaking down.

So in this respect, there is no comparison between the president's intended power and the British king's actual power. The king can do alone what the president can do only with the agreement of one branch of the legislature.

It must be admitted that here, the national executive's power would be greater than any state executive's. But this comes naturally from the sovereign power over treaties. If the Union broke apart, it would become a question whether each state's executive alone should hold that delicate and important power.

:::vocab
Advice and consent: The Senate's role in approving treaties (by a two-thirds vote) and appointments (by a majority).
Treaty: A formal written agreement between nations.
:::

## Receiving ambassadors {orig=8}

The president will also be authorized to receive ambassadors and other foreign diplomats. Opponents have made a lot of noise about this, but it is more a matter of dignity than of real power. It won't matter much in how the government is run. And it was far more convenient to arrange it this way than to require Congress, or one of its houses, to meet every time a foreign diplomat arrived, even if only to replace one who had left.

:::commentary
Hamilton called this power a mere formality, but it became important. Receiving an ambassador means recognizing a foreign government as legitimate. Presidents have used this to decide which governments the U.S. recognizes. Washington used it in 1793 to receive the new French Republic's minister. In *Zivotofsky v. Kerry* (2015), the Supreme Court ruled that the power to recognize foreign governments belongs to the president alone.
:::

## Appointments need the Senate too {orig=9}

The president will nominate, and with the advice and consent of the Senate, appoint ambassadors and other diplomats, Supreme Court justices, and in general all officers of the United States created by law whose appointments the Constitution doesn't otherwise provide for.

The king of Great Britain is truly called "the fountain of honor." He not only appoints people to every office, but can create offices. He can grant titles of nobility whenever he likes, and he controls an enormous number of church positions.

The president's power here is clearly far less than the British king's. It isn't even equal to the governor of New York's, if we judge the meaning of the state constitution by how it has actually worked. In New York, the power of appointment belongs to a council made up of the governor and four state senators chosen by the Assembly. The governor claims, and has often used, the right to nominate. He also gets the tie-breaking vote on appointments. If he really has the right to nominate, his power here equals the president's, and his tie-breaking vote makes it greater. In the national government, if the Senate were split evenly, no appointment could be made. In New York, if the council were split, the governor could tip the balance and confirm his own nominee.

Compare the publicity that must come with appointments made by the president and an entire house of Congress, with the privacy of appointments made by New York's governor, shut up in a secret room with four people at most, and often only two. Consider also how much easier it is to influence the few members of a council of appointment than the larger number in the national Senate. Then we can't hesitate to say that, in practice, New York's governor has far more power over appointments than the president will have.

:::note Hamilton's footnote
Hamilton added an honest footnote here. He admitted he didn't think the New York governor's claim to the right of nomination was well founded. But he said it's fair to judge a government by how it actually operates, until that practice is officially challenged.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton was taking a shot at New York's Governor George Clinton, the leading opponent of the Constitution in the state. New York's council of appointment was widely seen as a tool for handing out jobs to political allies. By pointing out that Clinton had *more* appointment power than the president would, Hamilton turned the critics' argument against them.
:::

## Summing up: a president is not a king {orig=10-11}

So, except for the president's shared power over treaties, it's hard to say whether the president would have more or less power overall than the governor of New York. And it's even clearer that there's no basis for comparing him to the king of Great Britain. To make the contrast even sharper, let's put the main differences side by side.

- The president would be elected by the people for four years. The king of Great Britain is a permanent, hereditary prince.
- The president could be personally punished and disgraced. The king's person is sacred and untouchable.
- The president would have a limited veto over Congress. The king has an absolute veto.
- The president would have the right to command the nation's army and navy. The king has that right and also the power to declare war, and to raise and regulate navies and armies on his own authority.
- The president would share the power to make treaties with one house of Congress. The king alone has the power to make treaties.
- The president would share the power of appointment in the same way. The king alone makes all appointments.
- The president can grant no special privileges at all. The king can make foreigners citizens, make commoners into nobles, and create corporations with all their legal rights.
- The president can make no rules about the nation's trade or money. The king is in several ways the master of trade. He can set up markets and fairs, regulate weights and measures, stop trade with other countries for a limited time, coin money, and allow or forbid the use of foreign coins.
- The president has no religious authority at all. The king is the supreme head and governor of the national church!

What answer should we give to those who try to convince us that things so different are alike? The same answer we should give to those who tell us that a government whose entire power would be held by elected, temporary servants of the people is an aristocracy, a monarchy, and a tyranny.

:::quote
The one has no particle of spiritual jurisdiction; the other is the supreme head and governor of the national church!

Meaning: The president has no religious authority. The British monarch is the head of the Church of England, which is still true today.
:::

:::commentary
This side-by-side list is one of the most effective passages in the series, and it's a useful study guide to the presidency. Notice how many of the king's powers the Constitution gives to Congress instead: declaring war, raising armies, regulating trade, and coining money.

Historians still debate how much the modern presidency resembles Hamilton's description. Supporters of a strong presidency point to Hamilton's own call for "energy in the executive" in [No. 70](/papers/70/). Critics point to this essay, arguing that presidents today use powers, like starting wars or making major policy through executive orders, that Hamilton told New Yorkers the president would never have.
:::

:::today
This essay is often cited whenever Americans worry that a president is acting too much like a king. Arguments over presidents starting military actions without Congress, issuing sweeping executive orders, using pardons for political allies, or claiming immunity from prosecution all come back to the question Hamilton raised: how different is the president from a monarch? His answer was that the president is elected, limited, and accountable. Whether that is still true depends on whether Congress, the courts, and voters actually use the checks the Constitution gives them.
:::
