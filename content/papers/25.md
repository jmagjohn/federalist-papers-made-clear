:::summary
Hamilton continues his defense of the national military power. Some people suggest that the states, rather than the Union, should provide the frontier forts and troops. Hamilton says that would be unfair to exposed states like New York and dangerous to everyone, because state armies would breed rivalry and could be turned against the Union. He then shows that a constitutional ban on peacetime armies couldn't work. A ban on *keeping* armies would be vague and easy to dodge, and a ban on *raising* them would leave the country unable to prepare until it was invaded. The militia alone can't defeat trained armies, as the Revolution proved. Finally, he points to Pennsylvania and Massachusetts, which raised troops in peacetime despite their own rules, to show that "parchment" limits give way to real necessity.
:::

:::keypoints
- Leaving defense to the separate states would **reverse the basic principle** of the Union. It would be unfair to some states, dangerous to all, and harmful to the Union.
- Britain, Spain, and Native nations **surround the whole Union**, so defense should be paid for from a **common treasury**.
- State armies would fuel **rivalry among the states** and could be used to **destroy national authority**.
- An army is safer in the hands of the government the people **watch most closely**.
- A ban on *keeping up* armies in peacetime would be **vague and easy to evade**.
- A ban on *raising* armies in peacetime would mean the country **must be attacked before it can prepare**.
- The **militia alone** could not have won the Revolution. "War, like most other things, is a science."
- **Pennsylvania** and **Massachusetts** raised peacetime troops anyway, showing how weak "parchment provisions" are against necessity.
- Breaking a constitution, even out of need, **weakens respect** for it and sets a bad precedent.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues directly from [No. 24](/papers/24/), where Hamilton argued that the country needs small forts on the frontier and a protected navy. Here he answers two follow-up ideas: that the states could handle those needs, and that the Constitution should simply ban peacetime armies.

Hamilton's readers had recent events in mind. During the Revolution, Washington's Continental Army had to rely heavily on short-term militia who came and went. And in the winter of 1786–87, Massachusetts had faced an armed uprising of farmers, known as Shays' Rebellion, while Congress was too poor to help.
:::

## Defense can't be handed to the states {orig=1-2}

Someone may argue that the needs listed in my last essay should be met by the state governments, under the direction of the Union. But that would actually turn the first principle of our political union upside down. In practice, it would move the job of common defense from the national government to the individual states. Such a plan would be oppressive to some states, dangerous to all of them, and harmful to the Union.

The territories of Britain, Spain, and the Indian nations near us don't border just a few particular states. They surround the Union from Maine to Georgia. So the danger, though greater in some places than others, is shared by all. For the same reason, the means of guarding against it should be decided by common councils and paid for from a common treasury.

Some states, because of where they are, are more directly exposed than others. New York is one of these. Under a plan of separate state defense, New York would have to carry the whole weight of the forces needed for its own immediate safety, and also for the indirect or eventual protection of its neighbors. That would be neither fair to New York nor safe for the other states.

Such a system would bring other problems as well:

- For a long time to come, the states that had to support these forces would be as unable as they would be unwilling to bear the cost of adequate defense. The safety of all would then depend on the stinginess, poor planning, or poverty of a few.
- Suppose those few states grew richer and expanded their forces to match. The other states would quickly become alarmed at seeing the Union's whole military force in the hands of two or three states, probably among the most powerful ones. Each would want some force to balance it, and excuses could easily be found.
- In this situation, military forces fed by mutual jealousy would tend to swell beyond their natural or proper size. And since each state would control its own, they would become tools for weakening or destroying the national authority.

:::commentary
Hamilton is writing to New Yorkers, so he stresses New York's position. In 1788 the state bordered British Canada to the north, and Britain still held forts on New York soil, such as Niagara and Oswego, which it had promised to give up in 1783. The Iroquois nations also held much of western New York. Under a system of state defense, New York's taxpayers would pay for forts that protected New England and New Jersey too.
:::

:::vocab
Federal head: Hamilton's term for the national government, the "head" of the union of states.
Counterpoise: A force that balances another force.
:::

## State armies would threaten both the Union and liberty {orig=3-4}

I have already given reasons to expect that the state governments will naturally tend to compete with the national government, out of love of power. I have also shown that in any contest between the national government and one of the states, the people will most likely side with their local government. Add to this huge advantage the separate control of their own military forces, and the states would face too strong a temptation, and have too easy a means, to attack and finally overthrow the Union's constitutional authority.

The people's liberty would also be less safe in this situation than if the national forces stayed in the hands of the national government. To the extent that an army is a dangerous weapon of power, it is better placed in the hands the people are most likely to watch with suspicion than in the hands they are least likely to suspect. The experience of the ages proves this truth: the people are always in the most danger when the means of harming their rights belong to those they suspect least.

The authors of the present Confederation fully understood the danger to the Union of letting the states have separate military forces. So they plainly forbade the states to keep either warships or troops without the consent of Congress. The truth is that a national government and military forces under state control fit together no better than a properly funded national treasury and the system of quotas and requests.

:::quote
...the people are always most in danger when the means of injuring their rights are in the possession of those of whom they entertain the least suspicion.

Meaning: Power is most dangerous when it's held by someone people trust too much, because no one is watching closely. Hamilton argues people will watch the national government more carefully than their own states.
:::

:::commentary
This is a clever reversal. Anti-Federalists trusted their state governments and feared a distant national government. Hamilton says that is exactly why the national government is the *safer* place for an army: people will keep a suspicious eye on it.

The Constitution keeps the rule Hamilton praises in the Articles. Article I, Section 10 says no state shall, without the consent of Congress, "keep Troops, or Ships of War in time of Peace." The states keep their militias, which Hamilton discusses in [No. 29](/papers/29/).
:::

## A ban on "keeping up" armies wouldn't work {orig=5-6}

There are other ways to see why it would be wrong to limit the national legislature's judgment on this subject. The objection I mentioned aims to rule out standing armies in time of peace. But we have never been told how far the ban is supposed to go. Would it forbid *raising* armies in peacetime, or only KEEPING THEM UP once peace has come?

If it only covers keeping them up, it will have no exact meaning, and it won't achieve its purpose. Once armies have been raised, what would count as "keeping them up" in violation of the Constitution? How much time would have to pass before there was a violation? A week? A month? A year?

Or shall we say that armies may be kept as long as the danger that caused them to be raised continues? That would admit that they might be kept IN TIME OF PEACE, against threatening or approaching danger. It would leave the plain words of the ban behind and open the door to very loose interpretation.

And who would judge whether the danger continued? Clearly this would have to be left to the national government. So the matter would come down to this: to guard against a danger it feared, the national government could first raise troops, and then keep them as long as it believed the peace or safety of the community was in any way at risk. It's easy to see that such broad judgment would leave plenty of room to escape the ban.

The only reason anyone could think such a rule useful is the chance that the executive and the legislature might join together in some plan to seize power. If that ever happened, how easy it would be to invent excuses about coming danger! Indian attacks, stirred up by Spain or Britain, would always be available. A foreign power might even be provoked on purpose, to create the needed appearance of danger, and then calmed again by well-timed concessions. If we can reasonably suppose that such a plot had been formed, with a good chance of success, then the army, once raised for whatever reason or excuse, could be used to carry it out.

:::vocab
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Latitude of construction: Freedom to interpret a rule loosely.
:::

## A ban on raising armies would be even worse {orig=7}

To prevent this, suppose we extended the ban to the RAISING of armies in time of peace. The United States would then present the strangest sight the world has ever seen: a nation whose Constitution makes it unable to prepare for defense until it has actually been invaded.

The custom of formally declaring war before fighting has lately gone out of use. So the government would have to wait for an enemy to appear inside our territory as its legal permission to begin enlisting men to protect the country. We would have to take the blow before we could even prepare to strike back. Every policy by which nations foresee distant danger and meet the gathering storm would be forbidden, as if it went against the true principles of free government.

We would have to leave our property and liberty at the mercy of foreign invaders. Our weakness would invite them to seize a helpless prize. And why? Because we are afraid that rulers we chose ourselves, who depend on our will, might endanger our liberty by abusing the very means needed to protect it.

:::quote
We must receive the blow, before we could even prepare to return it.

Meaning: If the Constitution banned raising armies in peacetime, the country couldn't build a defense until an enemy had already attacked.
:::

## The militia alone is not enough {orig=8}

Here, I expect, we will be told that the country's militia is its natural defense and would always be equal to the task of national defense. In substance, this belief nearly cost us our independence. It cost the United States millions of dollars that could have been saved. The facts from our own experience that forbid relying on it are too recent for us to be fooled by such a suggestion.

Steady military operations against a regular, disciplined army can only succeed with a force of the same kind. Concerns about cost, as well as about steadiness and strength, confirm this. The American militia, through their courage on many occasions in the last war, built lasting monuments to their fame. But the bravest of them know that the liberty of their country could not have been won by their efforts alone, however great and valuable those efforts were. War, like most other things, is a science. It must be learned and perfected through hard work, persistence, time, and practice.

:::quote
War, like most other things, is a science to be acquired and perfected by diligence, by perseverance, by time, and by practice.

Meaning: Winning wars takes training and experience, not just courage. That's why Hamilton thinks the country can't rely on part-time citizen soldiers alone.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton speaks here as a veteran. Militia did fight bravely at times during the Revolution, for example at Lexington and Concord and at Bunker Hill. But they often signed up for only a few months, left when their terms ran out, and sometimes broke and ran when facing trained British troops in open battle. George Washington repeatedly complained to Congress about having to depend on them, and pushed for a professional Continental Army with longer enlistments.

This passage cuts against a popular belief of the time, that a free country should rely on citizen-soldiers instead of professionals. Many Anti-Federalists held that view strongly, and it shaped the debates that led to the Second Amendment. Hamilton returns to the militia in [No. 29](/papers/29/).
:::

## Pennsylvania and Massachusetts prove the point {orig=9}

All extreme policies defeat themselves, because they go against the natural and proven course of human affairs. Pennsylvania right now gives an example of this truth. That state's Bill of Rights declares that standing armies are dangerous to liberty and ought not to be kept up in time of peace. Yet Pennsylvania, in a time of complete peace, because of local disorders in one or two of its counties, has decided to raise a body of troops. In all likelihood, it will keep them as long as there appears to be any danger to the public peace.

The conduct of Massachusetts teaches a lesson on the same subject, though in a different way. That state was forced to raise troops to put down an uprising at home, without waiting for the approval of Congress that the Articles of Confederation require. It still keeps a unit of paid soldiers to prevent the spirit of revolt from returning.

The Massachusetts constitution put no obstacle in the way of this. But the example still teaches us several things:

- Cases are likely to arise under our government, as under those of other nations, when a military force in peacetime is essential to the safety of society. So it would be wrong to restrict the legislature's judgment on this point.
- Applied to the United States, it shows how little even its own citizens are likely to respect the rights of a weak government.
- And above all, it shows how helpless "parchment provisions," rules written on paper, are in a struggle with public necessity.

:::commentary
The Massachusetts uprising was **Shays' Rebellion** (1786–87). Farmers in western Massachusetts, many of them Revolutionary War veterans, were crushed by debts and heavy state taxes. Courts were seizing their farms. Armed groups, including one led by Captain Daniel Shays, closed courts to stop the foreclosures. In January 1787, they marched on the national arsenal at Springfield. Congress had voted to raise troops, officially to fight Native nations on the frontier, but it couldn't pay for them. So Massachusetts raised its own militia army, paid for largely by loans from wealthy Boston merchants, and the rebels were scattered.

The rebellion shocked many leaders and helped convince them that the Articles needed replacing. Hamilton's point is that Massachusetts broke the Articles' rule about troops because it had to. Rules that ignore real needs, he argues, simply get broken.
:::

:::vocab
Insurrection: A violent uprising against the government; a rebellion.
Parchment: A writing material made from animal skin, used for important documents. "Parchment provisions" means rules that exist only on paper.
:::

## A lesson from ancient Sparta {orig=10}

The Lacedaemonians, or Spartans, had a basic rule that no one could serve as admiral twice. Their allies in the Peloponnesian League once suffered a severe defeat at sea by the Athenians. They then demanded that Lysander, who had served successfully as admiral before, command the combined fleets. To please their allies while still appearing to follow their ancient laws, the Spartans used a flimsy trick. They gave Lysander the real power of admiral under the official title of vice-admiral.

I have chosen this example from among a great many that could be given. Like our own examples, it confirms the truth I have stated: nations pay little attention to rules and principles that, by their very nature, go against the needs of society.

Wise statesmen will be careful not to tie down the government with limits that can't be obeyed. They know that every violation of a nation's basic laws, even one forced by necessity, weakens the sacred respect that rulers ought to feel for their country's constitution. And it sets a precedent for other violations, where the same excuse of necessity doesn't exist at all or is much less urgent and obvious.

:::commentary
Lysander was the Spartan commander who won the Peloponnesian War against Athens, destroying the Athenian fleet at Aegospotami in 405 BC. Hamilton's story is accurate in outline: Sparta's rules barred him from a second term as admiral, so another man held the title while Lysander held the real command.

Hamilton's final point is one of the most important in this essay. A constitution that can't be obeyed in an emergency teaches leaders that it's acceptable to break the constitution when necessary. And once that habit forms, leaders will claim "necessity" when there isn't any. So, he argues, it is safer to give the government the powers it will truly need, and then hold it to the rules.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's warning that broken rules weaken respect for a constitution still comes up in debates over emergency powers. In wars, economic crises, and public health emergencies, governments have often claimed that necessity justified stretching the law. Some argue, as Hamilton did, that the best protection is to give the government clear and adequate powers in advance, so it never has an excuse to break the rules. Others worry that broad powers granted in advance are exactly what invite abuse. His debate with the Anti-Federalists over citizen-soldiers versus professional armies also continues, in arguments about the size of the military and the role of the National Guard.
:::
