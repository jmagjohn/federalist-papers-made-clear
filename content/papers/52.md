:::summary
Madison begins a detailed look at the House of Representatives. He explains that voters for the House will be the same people who vote for the larger house of their own state legislature, a rule that respects each state's standards while keeping the House dependent on the people. Members must be 25, citizens for seven years, and residents of their state, but no wealth or religion is required. Then he defends two-year terms. He looks to experience: Britain's Parliament, Ireland's parliament, and the American colonies all kept some liberty with far longer terms. So two years is surely safe. And because Congress will have limited powers and be watched by the state legislatures, its members will have less to abuse and more people watching them.
:::

:::keypoints
- Voters for the House will be whoever can vote for the **larger branch of their state legislature**. This avoids leaving the right to vote to Congress or to state lawmakers.
- A representative must be **25 years old**, a citizen for **seven years**, and a resident of the state, and may not hold another federal office.
- The door is open to **"merit of every description,"** native or immigrant, young or old, rich or poor, of any religion.
- The House must have **"an immediate dependence on, and an intimate sympathy with, the people."** Frequent elections are the only way to secure this.
- Britain's House of Commons was elected only every **seven years**, yet the British kept much of their liberty.
- Ireland's parliament once lasted a whole king's reign, and the colonies had terms of **one to seven years**. Virginia, which led the way to independence, had elections only every seven years.
- Congress will have **limited powers** and will be watched by the **state legislatures**, so two-year terms are even safer.
:::

:::context Background
With this paper, Madison starts a series of essays (Nos. 52–58) on the House of Representatives, the part of the new government closest to the people. Before this, the series had covered general principles, ending with the separation of powers in Nos. 47–51.

Many Americans in 1788 believed in a slogan: "where annual elections end, tyranny begins." Most state legislatures were elected every year, and some, like Rhode Island and Connecticut, every six months. To them, a two-year term for the House looked dangerously long. Madison begins his answer here and continues it in [No. 53](/papers/53/).
:::

## Turning to the House {orig=1}

In the last four papers I looked at broad questions about the government. Now I turn to a closer examination of its separate parts. I will begin with the House of Representatives.

## Who can vote for the House {orig=2}

The first point to examine about this part of the government is the qualifications of the voters and of the people they elect. The voters will have the same qualifications as the voters for the most numerous branch of each state legislature.

The definition of the right to vote is rightly seen as a basic part of republican government. So the Convention had a duty to define and establish this right in the Constitution. It would have been wrong to leave it open for Congress to change from time to time, for the reason I just gave. It would have been wrong to leave it to the state legislatures to decide, for the same reason. There was an extra reason, too. It would have made the House, the branch of the federal government that ought to depend on the people alone, too dependent on the state governments. And trying to turn the different rules in the different states into one single rule would probably have been as unpopular with some states as it would have been hard for the Convention.

So the Convention's choice seems to have been the best one available. It should satisfy every state, because it matches the standard the state itself has already set, or may set in the future. It will be safe for the United States, because the standard is fixed by the state constitutions. This means the state governments can't change it on their own. And there is no reason to fear that the people of the states will change this part of their own constitutions in a way that takes away the rights the federal Constitution secures for them.

:::vocab
Most numerous branch: The larger house of a state legislature, such as a state assembly or house of delegates.
:::

:::commentary
This was a practical compromise. In 1788 the states had very different voting rules. Most required voters to own some property or pay taxes, but the details varied. A single national rule would have pleased no one. So the Constitution simply borrowed each state's own rule (Article I, Section 2).

Madison's prediction that the people would never narrow these rights turned out to be too hopeful. Some states did take away voting rights. New Jersey stopped women from voting in 1807. North Carolina in 1835 and Pennsylvania in 1838 took the vote away from free Black men. After the Civil War, Southern states used poll taxes, literacy tests, and violence to keep Black citizens from voting. Over time, the nation responded with amendments, including the 15th, 19th, 24th, and 26th, and with laws like the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Today, the right to vote in federal elections is shaped far more by national rules than Madison expected.
:::

## Who can serve in the House {orig=3}

The qualifications of the people elected were defined less carefully and properly by the state constitutions. They were also easier to make uniform. So the Convention was right to consider and set them. A representative of the United States:

- must be at least twenty-five years old;
- must have been a citizen of the United States for seven years;
- must live in the state he represents at the time he is elected; and
- may not hold any other office under the United States while he serves.

Within these reasonable limits, the door to this part of the federal government is open to merit of every kind, whether native-born or immigrant, young or old, and without regard to poverty or wealth, or to any particular religious faith.

:::quote
Under these reasonable limitations, the door of this part of the federal government is open to merit of every description, whether native or adoptive, whether young or old, and without regard to poverty or wealth, or to any particular profession of religious faith.

Meaning: Apart from a few basic rules about age, citizenship, and residence, anyone with talent can serve in the House.
:::


:::commentary
These qualifications are still the same today. The rule against holding another federal office comes from Article I, Section 6, and it is a key part of the separation of powers. A member of Congress can't also work in the president's cabinet, for example. Britain works differently: its prime minister and cabinet ministers sit in Parliament.

Notice what is *not* on the list. There is no property requirement and no religious test. Many state constitutions in 1788 had both. Madison makes the same point in [No. 57](/papers/57/).
:::

## Are two-year terms safe? {orig=4-5}

A second point to examine about this branch is how long representatives will serve. To decide whether this part of the plan is right, we must answer two questions. First, will elections every two years be safe in this case? Second, are they necessary or useful?

**First**, is it safe? It is essential to liberty that the government as a whole should share a common interest with the people. So it is especially essential that this branch of the government should depend directly on the people and have a close sympathy with them. Frequent elections are without question the only way to make sure of this dependence and sympathy.

But exactly how frequent they must be can't be calculated precisely. It must depend on many different circumstances. So let us consult experience, the guide that should always be followed whenever it can be found.

:::quote
...it is particularly essential that the branch of it under consideration should have an immediate dependence on, and an intimate sympathy with, the people.

Meaning: The House, above all, must be closely tied to the people, both by depending on their votes and by sharing their feelings.
:::


## Lessons from the British Parliament {orig=6}

Representation, in place of citizens meeting in person, was at most very poorly understood by ancient governments. So we can only expect useful examples from modern times. And even there, to avoid a search that is too vague and scattered, it will be best to stick to the few best-known examples that are most like our own case.

The first example is the House of Commons in Great Britain. The history of this branch of the English constitution before Magna Carta is too unclear to teach us anything. Scholars of ancient history have even questioned whether it existed then. The earliest records after that time show that parliaments were to *meet* every year, not that they were to be *elected* every year. And even these yearly meetings were left so much up to the king that, using various excuses, ambitious kings often arranged very long and dangerous gaps between them.

To fix this problem, a law passed during the reign of Charles II said that the gaps could not last more than three years. When William III came to the throne, after a revolution in the government, the issue was taken up even more seriously. It was declared to be one of the basic rights of the people that parliaments should be held FREQUENTLY. A few years later, during the same reign, another law gave the word "frequently" an exact meaning. It said that a new parliament must be called within three years after the last one ended. The most recent change, from three years to seven, is well known to have been made fairly early in this century. It came during a time of alarm over whether the Hanoverian family would keep the throne.

These facts show that the most frequent elections that kingdom has ever thought necessary to bind representatives to the people is once every three years. Even with seven-year terms, and all the other bad features of their Parliament, the British have kept a good deal of liberty. So if the term were cut from seven years to three, along with the other needed reforms, the people's influence over their representatives would grow a great deal. That should satisfy us that two-year elections, under the federal system, cannot possibly be dangerous to the House's proper dependence on its voters.

:::vocab
Magna Carta: The 1215 agreement in which English nobles forced King John to accept limits on his power. Later generations saw it as a foundation of English liberty.
Triennial: Happening every three years.
Septennial: Happening every seven years.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's history is accurate in its main points. The **Glorious Revolution** of 1688 replaced King James II with William III and Mary II. The English Bill of Rights of 1689 declared that "Parliaments ought to be held frequently," and the Triennial Act of 1694 required a new election at least every three years. Then, in 1716, Parliament passed the **Septennial Act**, stretching the limit to seven years. The ruling party feared that a quick election might help supporters of the exiled Stuart family, who had just led a rebellion in 1715 against the new Hanoverian king, George I.

The seven-year limit lasted until 1911, when Parliament cut it to five years. Today, British general elections must be held at least every five years.
:::

## Lessons from Ireland {orig=7}

Until recently, elections in Ireland were controlled entirely by the king's choice, and they were seldom held except when a new king took the throne or some other chance event occurred. The parliament that began with George II lasted through his whole reign, about thirty-five years. The representatives depended on the people in only two ways. The people could fill occasional empty seats by electing new members. And there was a chance that some event might bring about a general new election.

The Irish parliament's ability to defend the rights of its voters, even when it wanted to, was also tightly chained by the king's control over what it could discuss. Recently, if I am not mistaken, these chains have been broken, and parliaments that last eight years have also been established. What effect this partial reform will have must be left to future experience.

So the example of Ireland, seen this way, can teach us little. As far as we can draw any conclusion from it, it is this: if the people of that country have been able to keep any liberty at all under all these disadvantages, then two-year elections would secure every degree of liberty that depends on a proper connection between representatives and the people.

:::commentary
Madison's facts about Ireland are generally right. George II reigned from 1727 to 1760, a little over thirty years, so Madison's "about thirty-five years" is a slight overestimate. Ireland's Octennial Act of 1768 required elections every eight years. In 1782, reforms reduced the British government's control over the Irish parliament's lawmaking. That independence did not last long: the Act of Union of 1800 abolished the Irish parliament and merged Ireland into the United Kingdom.

Ireland's parliament also had a deep blind spot. Catholics, the large majority of the Irish people, were barred from serving in it and, for most of the 1700s, from voting.
:::

## Lessons from the colonies {orig=8}

Let us bring our study closer to home. The example of these states when they were British colonies deserves special attention, though it is so well known that little needs to be said about it. In all of them, representation was established in at least one branch of the legislature. But the length of terms varied, from one to seven years.

Do we have any reason to think, from the spirit and conduct of the people's representatives before the Revolution, that two-year elections would have been dangerous to public liberty? The spirit shown everywhere at the start of the struggle, which overcame the obstacles to independence, is the best proof of something. It shows that enough liberty had been enjoyed everywhere to make people value it and eager to expand it properly. This is true of the colonies with the least frequent elections as well as of those with the most frequent ones.

Virginia was the first colony to resist Parliament's attempts to seize power over America. It was also the first to support independence by an official public act. Yet in Virginia, if I have not been misinformed, elections under the old government were held only every seven years. I mention this example not to prove that Virginia had any special merit, since being first in these cases was probably an accident. Still less do I mention it to show any advantage in seven-year elections, which are unacceptable compared with more frequent ones. I mention it only as proof, and I think a very solid proof, that the liberties of the people can be in no danger from elections every two years.

:::commentary
Madison was a Virginian, so he knew his own state's history well, though he politely hedges ("if I have not been misinformed"). Virginia's House of Burgesses was the oldest elected assembly in the colonies, first meeting in 1619. In 1765 it passed Patrick Henry's resolutions against the Stamp Act. In May 1776, Virginia's convention instructed its delegates in Congress to propose independence. On June 7, 1776, Richard Henry Lee of Virginia did so.

Madison's argument here is a classic "even so" argument. If people could stay free and even launch a revolution with seven-year terms, then two-year terms can't be a threat to liberty.
:::

## Three more reasons two years is safe {orig=9}

The conclusion drawn from these examples is made even stronger by three facts.

**First**, Congress will have only part of the supreme lawmaking power that the British Parliament holds completely. The colonial assemblies and the Irish legislature also used that full power, with a few exceptions. It is an accepted and well-founded rule that, when nothing else affects the case, the greater a power is, the shorter its term should be. And the smaller the power, the more safely its term can be extended.

**Second**, I have shown in another paper that Congress will be restrained not only by its dependence on the people, as other legislatures are. It will also be watched and controlled by the state legislatures alongside it, which other legislatures are not.

**Third**, the more permanent branches of the federal government will have far fewer ways to lure the House of Representatives away from its duty to the people, if they wanted to, than the other branches of the governments I mentioned have over their popular branches.

So with less power to abuse, the federal representatives will be less tempted on one side, and doubly watched on the other.

:::commentary
The "more permanent branches" are the Senate and the president. Madison's comparison is to Britain, where the king could reward members of Parliament with jobs, pensions, and titles. Americans called this "corruption," and it was one of their main complaints against British government. The Constitution's ban on members of Congress holding other federal offices was meant to block that kind of influence.

The idea that the states would "watch" Congress is developed in [No. 46](/papers/46/), where Madison argues that state governments would quickly sound the alarm if the federal government overstepped its powers.
:::

:::today
Two-year House terms still spark debate. Because members face reelection so often, critics say they spend much of their time raising money and campaigning, and some have proposed amending the Constitution to give House members four-year terms. Supporters of the current system answer that frequent elections are exactly what keep the House, in Madison's words, in "intimate sympathy with the people." The question of who gets to vote, which Madison left to the states, has also grown into one of the most important and contested areas of American law.
:::
