:::summary
Hamilton explains why the president needs a four-year term. A leader who knows he'll be out of office soon won't risk taking an unpopular stand. Serving the people doesn't mean obeying "every sudden breeze of passion." The people usually *intend* the public good, but they don't always reason correctly about how to reach it. Sometimes leaders must resist a passing mood long enough for people to think it over. The president also needs to be independent of Congress, which tends to think it *is* the people. Four years isn't a perfect length, but it's long enough to give the president firmness, and short enough that it poses no threat to liberty.
:::

:::keypoints
- **Duration**, the length of the term, is the second ingredient of an energetic executive (after unity).
- People care more about what they hold securely. A president with a very short term won't risk **unpopular but necessary** actions.
- The republican principle means the **"deliberate sense of the community"** should govern, not every sudden passion.
- "The people commonly **INTEND** the **PUBLIC GOOD**," but they don't always **REASON RIGHT** about how to achieve it.
- Leaders should sometimes **resist a temporary delusion** to give people time for "cool and sedate reflection."
- The executive must also be **independent of Congress**, which often acts as if it were the people themselves.
- Four years gives the president a long enough stretch to show the wisdom of his choices before facing voters.
- Four years is **not long enough to threaten liberty**. If the British House of Commons could tame a king, a four-year elected president is no danger.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 70](/papers/70/), Hamilton listed four ingredients of "energy in the executive": unity, duration, adequate support, and competent powers. This essay covers the second. At the time, most state governors served only one year. Many Americans believed that short terms and frequent elections were the best protection against tyranny. A popular saying was "where annual elections end, tyranny begins." Hamilton had argued at the Constitutional Convention for an executive who would serve for life during good behavior, an idea the Convention rejected.
:::

## Why a longer term gives firmness {orig=1}

Duration in office has been named as the second requirement for an energetic executive. It matters for two things: the president's personal firmness in using his constitutional powers, and the stability of the policies adopted under his leadership.

As for the first, clearly, the longer the term, the more likely we are to get this important advantage. It's a general rule of human nature that a person cares about what he has in proportion to how secure his hold on it is. He will be less attached to something he holds for a moment or uncertainly than to something he holds for a long time or securely. And so he'll be willing to risk more for the second than the first. This applies to a political office, honor, or trust just as much as to any ordinary property.

The conclusion is this. A president who knows he MUST leave office very soon will tend to care too little about the office to risk any serious criticism or trouble. He won't use his powers independently, or face down bad moods, however brief, that may sweep through a large part of the public, or even through a ruling faction in Congress.

And if he only MIGHT have to leave office unless re-elected, and he wants to be re-elected, his hopes and his fears will combine even more strongly to corrupt his honesty or weaken his courage. Either way, weakness and indecision will be the marks of the office.

:::commentary
Hamilton's point is about incentives. If you know you'll be gone in a year, why make enemies? And if you're always running for re-election, you'll be tempted to do whatever is popular right now. A longer term gives a president room to make hard choices and defend them.

There's a trade-off here that Hamilton recognizes. The fear of losing re-election also keeps a president accountable. He defends re-election in [No. 72](/papers/72/), so he's not against accountability. He's against a term so short that the president never has time to lead.
:::

## Serving the people vs. following every passion {orig=2}

Some people would consider it the executive's best quality to bend slavishly to whatever current is running strongest, either among the public or in Congress. But such people have very crude ideas about the purposes of government, and about the true ways to promote the public happiness.

The republican principle requires that the deliberate sense of the community should guide the conduct of those it trusts to manage its affairs. But it doesn't require unconditional obedience to every sudden breeze of passion, or to every passing impulse the people may get from the tricks of men who flatter their prejudices in order to betray their interests.

It's rightly said that the people usually INTEND the PUBLIC GOOD. This is often true even of their mistakes. But their good sense would despise a flatterer who pretended that they always REASON RIGHT about the MEANS of promoting it. They know from experience that they sometimes make mistakes. The wonder is that they make so few, surrounded as they always are by:

- the tricks of flatterers and suck-ups;
- the traps of the ambitious, the greedy, and the desperate;
- the schemes of people who have more of their trust than they deserve;
- and of people who try to gain their trust rather than to deserve it.

When the people's interests conflict with what they want at the moment, it is the duty of those they have appointed as guardians of those interests to resist the temporary delusion. That gives the people time and opportunity for cooler, calmer reflection. Examples could be given where conduct like this has saved the people from very serious consequences of their own mistakes. And the people have later built lasting monuments of gratitude to the leaders who had the courage and generosity to serve them at the risk of their displeasure.

:::quote
The republican principle demands that the deliberate sense of the community should govern the conduct of those to whom they intrust the management of their affairs; but it does not require an unqualified complaisance to every sudden breeze of passion...

Meaning: In a republic, leaders should follow what the people want after careful thought, not every sudden mood. "Complaisance" means eagerness to please.
:::

:::quote
It is a just observation, that the people commonly INTEND the PUBLIC GOOD. This often applies to their very errors. But their good sense would despise the adulator who should pretend that they always REASON RIGHT about the MEANS of promoting it.

Meaning: The people usually want what's best for the country, but that doesn't mean they always know the best way to get there. An "adulator" is a flatterer.
:::

:::vocab
Deliberate sense of the community: What the people want after careful, unhurried thought, as opposed to a quick emotional reaction.
Servile pliancy: Bending like a servant to whatever others want.
Sycophant: Someone who flatters powerful people to gain favor; a suck-up.
:::

:::commentary
This passage is one of the clearest statements of the founders' view of representative government. A representative isn't just a mirror of public opinion. He or she is a "guardian" of the people's interests, who sometimes must stand against what's popular. Madison makes a similar argument about the Senate in [No. 63](/papers/63/).

This idea raises a hard question that democracies still struggle with: who decides when the public is suffering from a "temporary delusion," and when it's simply disagreeing with its leaders? A leader who resists the public may be wise and brave, or just stubborn and out of touch. History remembers some leaders as heroes for standing against public opinion, and others as arrogant for ignoring it. Hamilton's answer is that the voters get the final say, at the next election.
:::

## Independence from Congress {orig=3-4}

But however much we might want the executive to obey the wishes of the people without limit, there is no good reason to want him to obey the moods of the legislature in the same way. Sometimes Congress may oppose the people. Other times the people may not care either way. In either case, it is certainly desirable that the executive be in a position to dare to act on his own judgment with energy and decision.

The same rule that teaches us to divide power among the branches also teaches us that the division should be arranged so that each branch is independent of the others. What's the point of separating the executive or the judiciary from the legislature if both are set up to be completely at the legislature's mercy? That kind of separation would exist only in name. It couldn't achieve the goals it was designed for.

It's one thing to be subject to the laws, and another to depend on the legislature. The first fits the basic principles of good government. The second violates them, and whatever the Constitution's forms say, it unites all power in the same hands.

Earlier essays have fully shown, with examples, the legislature's tendency to absorb every other power. In purely republican governments, this tendency is almost impossible to resist. The people's representatives in a popular assembly sometimes seem to imagine that they are the people themselves. They show strong signs of impatience and disgust at the slightest opposition from anyone else, as if the executive or judiciary using its own rights were an attack on their privileges and an insult to their dignity. They often seem eager to exercise a bossy control over the other branches. And since they usually have the people on their side, they act with such force that it is very difficult for the other parts of government to keep the Constitution in balance.

:::quote
The representatives of the people, in a popular assembly, seem sometimes to fancy that they are the people themselves...

Meaning: Legislators sometimes act as if they *are* the people, so any disagreement with them seems like an attack on the people.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is repeating Madison's warning from [No. 48](/papers/48/): in a republic, the legislature is the branch most likely to grab power. Notice the important line: "It is one thing to be subordinate to the laws, and another to be dependent on the legislative body." The president must obey the laws Congress passes. But Congress can't control the president's job or pay. That independence is what makes separation of powers real.
:::

## How a short term would weaken the president {orig=5}

Someone might ask how the length of the term could affect the executive's independence from Congress, unless Congress had the power to appoint or remove him. One answer comes from the principle already mentioned: people take little interest in a short-lived advantage, and have little reason to risk any serious trouble or danger for it.

Another answer, maybe more obvious though not more convincing, comes from Congress's influence over the people. Congress could use that influence to block the re-election of a president who, by honestly resisting some bad plan of theirs, had made them angry.

## Is four years long enough? {orig=6-7}

Someone may also ask whether four years would achieve the goal. And if it wouldn't, wouldn't a shorter term be better, since it would at least be safer against ambitious plans, rather than a longer term that is still too short to give the president the desired firmness and independence?

We can't claim that four years, or any other limited term, would completely achieve the goal. But it would help enough to have an important effect on the spirit and character of the government.

Between the start and the end of such a term, there would always be a long stretch when the end would be far enough away that it wouldn't improperly affect a man with a reasonable amount of courage. During that time, he could reasonably expect that there would be enough time before the next election to convince the public that his policies were right.

As he got closer to the moment when the public would judge his conduct in a new election, his confidence, and with it his firmness, would probably weaken. But both would get support from the chances his time in office had given him to earn the respect and goodwill of the people. He could then safely take risks, in proportion to how much wisdom and honesty he had shown, and how much respect and loyalty he had earned from his fellow citizens.

So on one hand, a four-year term will add enough to the executive's firmness to be a very valuable ingredient. On the other hand, it isn't long enough to justify any alarm for public liberty.

Consider the British House of Commons. It began with very weak powers, little more than the power to agree or disagree to a new tax. Yet it moved rapidly to shrink the powers of the crown and the privileges of the nobility to the limits it thought fit for free government, while raising itself to an equal branch of the legislature. In one case, it even managed to abolish both the monarchy and the aristocracy, and to overturn all the old institutions of church and state. And recently, it made the king tremble at a reform it attempted.

If the Commons could do all that, what is there to fear from an elected official with a four-year term and the limited powers of a President of the United States? Only that he might not be strong enough for the job the Constitution gives him. I'll just add this: if his term is short enough to raise doubts about his firmness, those doubts are inconsistent with fears that he will grab power.

:::vocab
House of Commons: The elected lower house of the British Parliament.
Prerogatives: Special powers or rights that belong to an office, such as the king's.
Aristocracy: A class of nobles who inherit their rank and privileges.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's historical examples:

- The House of Commons started with little more than the power to approve taxes. Over centuries, it used its control of money to take power from the king. This shows how a legislature with the "power of the purse" can grow.
- "Abolish both the royalty and the aristocracy" refers to the English Civil War. In 1649, Parliament executed King Charles I and abolished the monarchy and the House of Lords for about eleven years.
- The "recent occasion" (explained in Hamilton's footnote) was Charles James Fox's India Bill of 1783. It would have shifted control of British India from the East India Company to commissioners chosen by Parliament. King George III saw it as a threat to his power and pressured the House of Lords to defeat it.

His final line is clever: critics can't claim *both* that four years is too short for the president to be firm *and* that the president is dangerously powerful. Those fears contradict each other.
:::

:::today
Americans still argue about how closely leaders should follow public opinion. Polls now measure the public's mood almost daily, and politicians face constant pressure from social media. Some people admire leaders who follow the polls as truly democratic. Others admire leaders who take unpopular stands they believe are right. Hamilton's distinction between the "deliberate sense of the community" and "every sudden breeze of passion" is one way to think about that tension. His four-year term has stayed the same since 1789, but since 1951 the 22nd Amendment has limited presidents to two terms.
:::
