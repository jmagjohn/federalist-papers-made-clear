:::summary
Hamilton argues that limiting the *legislature's* power over defense is a mistake born of "a zeal for liberty more ardent than enlightened." He traces the American fear of standing armies back to England, where the danger came from kings who kept armies without Parliament's consent. The English answer, in the Bill of Rights of 1689, was to require Parliament's approval, not to tie Parliament's hands. Americans, he says, wrongly carried their distrust of kings over to their own elected representatives. The Constitution's real safeguard is better: Congress can't fund an army for more than two years, so it must openly vote on the question again and again, while rival parties, the state legislatures, and the people watch. A plot to build a dangerous army would take years, would need a long chain of traitors, and would be impossible to hide.
:::

:::keypoints
- In a revolution, people rarely find the right balance between **power** and **private rights**.
- Restraining the legislature on defense comes from "a zeal for liberty more ardent than enlightened." **Trust must be placed somewhere.**
- The fear of standing armies came from **English history**, where kings kept armies on their own authority.
- England's **Bill of Rights (1689)** banned peacetime armies only *without Parliament's consent*. It didn't limit Parliament itself.
- Americans wrongly applied their distrust of kings to **their own elected representatives**.
- Even Pennsylvania's and North Carolina's rules say armies "**ought not**" be kept, not "shall not." Such rules are ignored when needed.
- The **two-year limit** on army funding forces Congress to vote openly on the army, in front of voters, at least every two years.
- Opposition parties and **state legislatures** will sound the alarm if Congress goes too far.
- A plot against liberty would **take years**, require many traitors, and **couldn't be hidden**.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues the argument of [No. 24](/papers/24/) and [No. 25](/papers/25/). Hamilton has shown that a ban on peacetime armies would be unworkable. Now he explains where the fear of standing armies came from and why the Constitution's own safeguard is better.

Most Americans in 1787 knew English history well. They saw the Glorious Revolution of 1688, when Parliament replaced King James II with William of Orange and his wife Mary, as a great victory for liberty. The English Bill of Rights that followed was a model for American thinking about rights.
:::

## Finding the balance between power and rights {orig=1-2}

It was hardly to be expected that, in a popular revolution, people's minds would stop at the happy middle point. That point marks the healthy boundary between POWER and PRIVILEGE. It combines the energy of government with the security of private rights. Our failure to find this delicate and important balance is the main source of the troubles we now face. If we aren't careful to avoid repeating the error as we try to correct and improve our system, we may go from one fantastic scheme to another. We may try change after change, but we will probably never make any real change for the better.

The idea of restraining the legislature in the means of providing for national defense is one of those clever notions that come from a zeal for liberty more passionate than wise. As we have seen, it has not spread far. Even in this country, where it first appeared, Pennsylvania and North Carolina are the only two states that have supported it at all. All the others have refused to give it the slightest support. They wisely judged that trust must be placed somewhere. The need to trust is part of the very act of handing over power. And it is better to risk the abuse of that trust than to hamper the government and endanger public safety with unwise limits on the legislature.

On this point, the Constitution's opponents are fighting the general decision of America. Experience should have taught them to correct the extremes we may have fallen into before. Instead, they seem eager to lead us into new extremes that are even more dangerous and absurd. It is as if our government had been found too strong or too rigid. The ideas they teach would weaken or loosen it, by methods that have been condemned or avoided on other occasions.

It can be said without unfair insult that if the principles they preach on various points became the popular belief, they would make the people of this country unfit for any kind of government at all. But there is no need to fear this. The citizens of America have too much good judgment to be argued into anarchy. And unless I am badly mistaken, experience has created a deep and serious belief in the public mind. People now see that a more energetic government is essential to the welfare and prosperity of the community.

:::quote
The idea of restraining the legislative authority, in the means of providing for the national defense, is one of those refinements which owe their origin to a zeal for liberty more ardent than enlightened.

Meaning: People who want to limit Congress's defense powers love liberty, but their passion is greater than their understanding of what liberty actually requires.
:::

:::vocab
Energy: In the Federalist Papers, this means strength, effectiveness, and the ability to act firmly and quickly.
Anarchy: A state of disorder with no effective government.
Privilege: Here, the rights and freedoms of individuals that government may not take away.
:::

## Where the fear came from: English history {orig=3-5}

It may be useful here to briefly describe where the idea of banning peacetime military forces came from and how it grew. In thoughtful people, it may come from studying the nature and tendencies of armies, along with events in other times and countries. But as a national feeling, it must be traced to the habits of thought we inherited from the nation most of our people came from.

In England, for a long time after the Norman Conquest, the king's power was almost unlimited. Step by step, his powers were cut back in favor of liberty, first by the nobles and later by the people, until most of his most threatening claims had disappeared. But English liberty did not completely triumph until the revolution of 1688, which put the Prince of Orange on the throne.

The king had an accepted, undefined power to make war. Using it, Charles II had kept 5,000 regular troops in peacetime on his own authority. James II raised that number to 30,000, paid out of the king's own allowance. At the revolution, to end such a dangerous power, the new Bill of Rights declared that "the raising or keeping a standing army within the kingdom in time of peace, UNLESS WITH THE CONSENT OF PARLIAMENT, was against law."

In that kingdom, even when the love of liberty was at its height, no protection against standing armies was thought necessary beyond forbidding the executive alone to raise or keep them. The patriots who led that famous revolution were too moderate and too well informed to think of limiting the legislature's judgment. They knew several things:

- A certain number of troops for guards and garrisons were essential.
- No exact limits could be set on the nation's emergencies.
- A power equal to every possible situation had to exist somewhere in the government.
- By leaving that power to the judgment of the legislature, they had gone as far as caution could go while still keeping the community safe.

:::commentary
The Norman Conquest was the invasion of England in 1066 by William of Normandy. The "barons" who first limited the king's power include the nobles who forced King John to accept **Magna Carta** in 1215. Parliament gained power over the centuries after that, especially in the struggles of the 1600s.

James II was a Catholic king whose growing army alarmed many English Protestants. In 1688, leading politicians invited his Protestant son-in-law, William of Orange, to come from the Netherlands. James fled, and Parliament made William and Mary joint rulers. Hamilton's key point is accurate: the English Bill of Rights limited the *king*, not Parliament. Parliament could approve an army whenever it wished.
:::

:::vocab
Prerogative: A special power that belongs to a king or executive by right of office.
Civil list: The money set aside to pay a king's personal and household expenses.
:::

## America inherited the fear, and overdid it {orig=6-7}

From the same source, the people of America inherited a sense that standing armies in peacetime are dangerous to liberty. The events of a revolution made the public extra sensitive on every point connected with the people's rights. In some cases, it heated our zeal beyond what was healthy for the body politic.

The attempts of two states to restrict their legislatures on military forces are examples of this. The principles that had taught us to distrust the power of a hereditary king were, in an unwise excess, extended to the people's own representatives in their elected assemblies.

Even in some states that didn't make this mistake, we find needless declarations that standing armies ought not to be kept in peacetime WITHOUT THE CONSENT OF THE LEGISLATURE. I call them needless because the reason behind the similar rule in the English Bill of Rights doesn't apply to any state constitution. Under those constitutions, the power to raise armies at all can't possibly belong to anyone but the legislatures themselves. So it was unnecessary, if not absurd, to declare that something must not be done without the consent of the only body that had the power to do it. That is why some of these constitutions say nothing at all on the subject. Among them is the constitution of our own state of New York, which has been rightly praised in both Europe and America as one of the best governments established in this country.

It is worth noticing that even the two states that seem to have considered banning peacetime forces used words of caution, not prohibition. They don't say standing armies SHALL NOT BE kept up in peacetime, but that they OUGHT NOT to be. This unclear wording seems to have come from a conflict between suspicion and good sense. On one side was the desire to keep out such forces no matter what. On the other was the belief that a total ban would be unwise and unsafe.

:::commentary
Hamilton's central claim is that Americans confused two different dangers. In England, the threat came from a king who could raise an army without the people's representatives. In America, the people's representatives themselves would control the army. Hamilton thinks distrusting your own elected legislature the way you'd distrust a king is a mistake.

Anti-Federalists disagreed. They argued that elected officials could also become corrupt, especially in a distant national government with few representatives for each citizen. Their concern about the military shaped later debates. The Second Amendment, adopted in 1791, begins by declaring a "well regulated Militia" to be "necessary to the security of a free State," a phrase that reflects the era's preference for citizen-soldiers.
:::

:::vocab
Body politic: The people of a nation or state, thought of as one organized group.
Hereditary: Passed down from parent to child, like a crown.
:::

## Weak rules versus the two-year limit {orig=8-9}

Can anyone doubt what would happen to such a rule? Whenever public affairs seemed to require departing from it, the legislature would treat it as mere advice. It would be made to give way to the needs, or supposed needs, of the state. The example of Pennsylvania I mentioned before settles the question. So what use is such a rule (someone might ask) if it stops working the moment someone wants to ignore it?

Let's compare how well that kind of rule works with the rule in the new Constitution, which limits money for military purposes to two years at a time. The first, by aiming at too much, will achieve nothing. The second avoids an unwise extreme and fits perfectly with proper provision for the nation's needs. It will have a healthy and powerful effect.

## How the two-year rule protects us {orig=10}

This rule will FORCE the national legislature, at least once every two years, to think about whether to keep a military force. It must make a new decision on the question and state its view by a formal vote, in full view of the people it represents. It is NOT FREE to give the executive permanent funds to support an army, even if it were careless enough to want to place such improper trust in the executive.

Party spirit, to some degree, must be expected to infect every political body. So there will surely be members of Congress quite willing to attack the majority's actions and accuse it of bad motives. Paying for a military force will always be a favorite subject for speeches. Every time the question comes up, the opposition party will rouse public attention to it. If the majority is really inclined to go beyond proper limits, the community will be warned of the danger and will have a chance to take steps against it.

Apart from parties in Congress itself, there are the state legislatures. They will always be watchful, and even suspicious and jealous, guardians of citizens' rights against invasions by the national government. Every time the question is debated, they will keep a close eye on the conduct of the national leaders. If anything improper appears, they will be quick to sound the alarm to the people. They will be not only the VOICE but, if necessary, the ARM of the people's discontent.

:::quote
...the State legislatures, who will always be not only vigilant but suspicious and jealous guardians of the rights of the citizens against encroachments from the federal government...

Meaning: The states will watch the national government closely and warn the people if it starts to overstep. Hamilton even says they could become the "arm," not just the "voice," of the people's resistance.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is describing what we might call "accountability through repetition." Because army funding expires, Congress can't approve an army once and forget about it. Each new vote is public, so voters can hold members responsible. It also gives the minority party a regular chance to criticize.

Notice the striking phrase "the ARM of their discontent." Hamilton, the strongest nationalist among the founders, suggests the states might lead the people in active, even forceful, resistance to the national government. He develops this idea in [No. 28](/papers/28/), and Madison makes a similar argument in [No. 46](/papers/46/). Interestingly, the two-year limit applies only to the *army*. The Constitution's power "to provide and maintain a Navy" has no such limit, because a navy was seen as much less of a threat to liberty at home.
:::

:::vocab
Constituents: The people a government or representative serves.
Encroachment: Gradually taking over someone else's power or territory.
:::

## A plot against liberty would take time and be discovered {orig=11-12}

Plans to destroy the liberties of a great community REQUIRE TIME to prepare. An army large enough to seriously threaten those liberties could only be built up step by step. That would require not just a short-term alliance between the legislature and the executive, but a conspiracy that lasted for years.

Is it likely that such an alliance would exist at all? Is it likely that it would be kept up and passed along through all the changes in membership that elections every two years would bring to both houses? Can we assume that every man, the moment he took his seat in the Senate or the House, would become a traitor to the people who elected him and to his country? Can we suppose that not one man would be sharp enough to detect such a terrible conspiracy, or brave and honest enough to warn the people? If we can fairly assume such things, then all trust in representatives should end at once. The people should take back all the powers they have handed over. They should split into as many states as there are counties, so that they can manage their own affairs in person.

Even if such things could reasonably be assumed, the plan could not be hidden for long. Expanding the army so greatly in a time of deep peace would announce it. What believable reason could be given, in a country placed as ours is, for such huge increases in military force? The people could not be fooled for long. And once the plan was discovered, the destruction of the plan, and of the plotters, would quickly follow.

:::commentary
This argument depends on two things: regular elections and public information. A plot would have to survive many elections, and a free press would report a sudden military buildup. Hamilton is confident that at least one honest member of Congress would blow the whistle.

Critics might answer that a government doesn't need a giant secret plot to slowly expand its military. Each increase can be justified by a new danger, real or exaggerated. Hamilton himself admitted in [No. 25](/papers/25/) that leaders could "fabricate pretenses of approaching danger." His reply here is that, under the Constitution, every one of those increases would have to be openly voted on and paid for.
:::

## What if a large army is raised during a war? {orig=13-14}

Some say the two-year limit on army funding would be useless. Once the executive had a force big enough to frighten the people into submission, they argue, he could use that force itself to get the resources he needed, without any funding from the legislature.

But the question comes up again: on what excuse could he be given a force that large in peacetime? Suppose it was created because of a rebellion at home or a foreign war. Then the case falls outside the objection, which is aimed only at the power of keeping troops *in time of peace*. Few people will be so unrealistic as to argue seriously that armies should not be raised to put down a rebellion or resist an invasion.

And suppose defending the community in such a crisis required an army so large that it put liberty at risk. That is one of those disasters for which there is neither prevention nor cure. No form of government could guard against it. It could even happen under a simple military alliance, if the allies ever had to form an army for their common defense.

But this evil is far less likely to strike us if we are united than if we are divided. In fact, it can safely be said that it is very unlikely to strike a united America at all. It's hard to imagine dangers so great attacking the whole Union that they would require a force large enough to put our liberties at the slightest risk. This is especially true when we count the help of the militia, which should always be relied on as a valuable and powerful support for the army. But in a divided America, as I have fully shown elsewhere, the opposite would be not only likely but almost unavoidable.

:::note
In Hamilton's text, the sentence says the evil is unlikely to attend us "in the latter situation," meaning a divided America. From the rest of the paragraph, he clearly means the *former* situation, a united America, so we have adapted it that way.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton admits something important: a very large wartime army *could* threaten liberty, and no constitution can fully prevent that. His answer is that a united America would rarely need such an army, while separate, rival confederacies would constantly need them. This is the argument he made in [No. 8](/papers/8/), where he warned that disunion would turn America into a land of armed camps, like Europe.

History tested this argument. During the Civil War, more than two million men served in the Union army, and President Lincoln suspended the writ of *habeas corpus* in some areas. Yet after the war ended, the army was quickly cut back. Supporters see this as proof that Hamilton was right that free institutions can survive a large wartime army. Critics point to the lasting growth of the military after World War II as a sign that "temporary" forces can become permanent.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's two-year rule is still part of the Constitution, and Congress follows it. Military funding is voted on every year, and the debate over the defense budget is one of the biggest fights in Congress. Members regularly argue over how large the military should be, where troops should be stationed, and how much presidents can do with the forces Congress pays for. Hamilton's idea, that the power of the purse is the people's best control over the army, remains a key part of how Americans think about civilian control of the military.
:::
