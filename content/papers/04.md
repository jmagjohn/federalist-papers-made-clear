:::summary
In No. 3, Jay showed that a united America would give other nations fewer *just* reasons for war. Here he warns that nations also start wars for *pretended* reasons: greed, glory, or rivalry. America competes with European powers in fishing, shipping, and trade with Asia, and Spain and Britain block its rivers. Those rivals won't be happy to see America grow. The best protection is to be strong enough that no one dares attack. One national government can use the best talent from every state, organize one militia and one navy, and defend any part of the country with the strength of the whole. Jay points to Great Britain: if England, Scotland, Wales, and Ireland each had their own government and navy, they would all be weak.
:::

:::keypoints
- Nations start wars for **pretended** reasons as well as **just** ones, whenever they think they can gain something.
- Kings often go to war for **personal** reasons: glory, revenge, ambition, or family interests.
- America is a **rival** of European nations in fishing, shipping, and the China and India trade, and in trade with their colonies.
- **Spain** blocks the Mississippi and **Britain** blocks the St. Lawrence River.
- The best defense is to be in a **situation that discourages attack**: strong government, arms, and resources.
- One national government can draw on the **ablest people**, follow **one policy**, organize **one militia**, and defend **any part** with the strength of the whole.
- **Britain's** power came from uniting England, Scotland, Wales, and Ireland under one government.
- Divided states might not help each other, and couldn't agree on who commands or how peace is made.
- Foreign nations will see America **as it really is**, and treat it with respect or contempt accordingly.
:::

:::context Background
This is the third of John Jay's essays on dangers from foreign nations. In the 1780s, America's trade was struggling. Britain had closed its West Indies colonies to American ships after the Revolution. Spain had closed the Mississippi River to American shipping in 1784, cutting off western farmers from their main route to market. In 1786, Jay himself had negotiated with Spain's representative, Diego de Gardoqui, and proposed giving up use of the Mississippi for 25 years in exchange for a trade treaty. Southern and western Americans were furious, and the deal fell through. Jay knew firsthand how weak America's bargaining position was.
:::

## Pretended causes of war {orig=1-3}

My last essay gave several reasons why the people's safety would be best protected by union against the danger of giving other nations JUST causes of war. Those reasons show that a national government would give such causes less often, and would settle them more easily, than the state governments or the proposed little confederacies.

But the safety of the American people against FOREIGN force depends not only on avoiding JUST causes of war. It also depends on putting themselves, and keeping themselves, in a position that doesn't INVITE attack or insult. After all, there are PRETENDED causes of war as well as just ones.

It's sadly true, however shameful it is to human nature, that nations in general will go to war whenever they think they can get something out of it. In fact, absolute kings will often go to war when their nations have nothing to gain, for purely personal reasons. These include a thirst for military glory, revenge for personal insults, ambition, or private deals to enrich or support their own families or followers. These and many other motives, which exist only in the ruler's own mind, often lead him into wars that aren't justified by justice or by the wishes and interests of his people.

But besides these motives for war, which are more common in absolute monarchies but still deserve our attention, there are others that affect nations as often as kings. Some of them, on examination, come from our own situation and circumstances.

:::vocab
Pretended causes of war: Excuses for war that are not truly just, used to cover greed or ambition.
Absolute monarch: A king or queen whose power has no legal limits.
:::

## Where America competes with Europe {orig=4-9}

Consider these rivalries:

- **Fishing.** We compete with France and Britain in the fisheries. We can supply their markets more cheaply than they can supply themselves, despite their efforts to stop us by paying bonuses to their own fishermen or taxing foreign fish.
- **Shipping.** We compete with them, and with most other European nations, in shipping and carrying goods for others. We'd be fooling ourselves if we thought any of them would be happy to see our shipping grow. Since our carrying trade can't grow without shrinking theirs somewhat, it's in their interest, and will be their policy, to hold it back rather than help it.
- **Trade with China and India.** In this trade we get in the way of more than one nation. It lets us share in profits they had almost to themselves, and it lets us buy directly the goods we used to buy from them.
- **Trade with their colonies.** No nation with territory on or near this continent will be pleased to see our own trade grow in our own ships. Our goods are cheap and excellent, we're close by, and our merchants and sailors are bold and skillful. All this will give us a bigger share of the benefits of those territories than their rulers want.
- **Rivers.** Spain finds it useful to close the Mississippi to us on one side, and Britain shuts us out of the St. Lawrence on the other. Neither will let the other waters between them and us become routes for trade and contact.

From these and similar points, which could be explained in more detail if it were wise to do so, it's easy to see how jealousy and unease may gradually creep into the minds and governments of other nations. We shouldn't expect them to watch calmly and without concern as we grow in unity, power, and importance on land and at sea.

:::vocab
Fisheries: Fishing grounds and the fishing industry. The waters off Newfoundland were among the richest in the world.
Carrying trade: The business of shipping other people's goods for a fee.
Bounty: A payment a government gives to encourage an industry, such as paying fishermen for each catch.
Duty: A tax on imported goods.
:::

:::commentary
Jay's examples were very real. The Newfoundland fisheries had been a major issue in the 1783 peace negotiations, and Jay helped win American fishing rights there. American merchants began trading directly with China in 1784, when the ship *Empress of China* sailed from New York to Canton. And the closing of the Mississippi was explosive in the West, where settlers in Kentucky and Tennessee talked openly of breaking away from the Union, or even joining Spain, if the river wasn't opened.

Notice Jay's phrase "if consistent with prudence." As Secretary for Foreign Affairs, he couldn't say everything he knew in a newspaper essay read by foreign diplomats.
:::

## Strength discourages attack {orig=10-11}

The American people know that reasons for war may come from these circumstances, and from others not yet obvious. They know that whenever such reasons find the right time and opportunity, there will be no shortage of excuses to justify them.

So they wisely see union and a good national government as necessary to put them, and keep them, in A POSITION that discourages war instead of INVITING it. That position means being as well defended as possible. And that depends on the country's government, weapons, and resources.

Since the safety of the whole is in the interest of the whole, and can't be provided without government, whether one government or several or many, let's ask whether one good government isn't better suited to this purpose than any other number.

:::quote
...union and a good national government as necessary to put and keep them in SUCH A SITUATION as, instead of INVITING war, will tend to repress and discourage it.

Meaning: A strong, united country is less likely to be attacked, because enemies will think twice. This idea is sometimes called "deterrence" or "peace through strength."
:::

## What one government can do {orig=12}

One government can gather and use the talents and experience of the ablest people, wherever in the Union they live. It can follow the same principles of policy everywhere. It can bring the different parts of the country into harmony, protect them, and give each the benefit of its foresight and precautions. In making treaties, it will consider the interests of the whole, and the interests of each part as connected with the whole.

It can use the resources and power of the whole country to defend any particular part, and do so more easily and quickly than state governments or separate confederacies, which lack coordination and a unified system. It can put the militia under one plan of training. And by placing the militia's officers properly under the president, it can, in effect, combine them into one body, making them more effective than if they were divided into thirteen, or three or four, separate and independent forces.

:::vocab
Militia: Citizen soldiers who train part-time and can be called up in emergencies.
Chief Magistrate: The president.
:::

## The example of Great Britain {orig=13-14}

What would Britain's militia be like if the English militia obeyed the government of England, the Scottish militia obeyed the government of Scotland, and the Welsh militia obeyed the government of Wales? Suppose there were an invasion. Could those three governments, if they agreed at all, fight the enemy with all their forces as effectively as the single government of Great Britain?

We've heard a lot about Britain's fleets, and if we're wise, the time may come when America's fleets also get attention. But Britain's navy would never have become famous for its skill and power if one national government hadn't regulated Britain's shipping to make it a training ground for sailors, and hadn't called on all the nation's means and materials to build fleets.

Let England have its own shipping and fleet. Let Scotland have its own shipping and fleet. Let Wales have its own, and let Ireland have its own. Let those four parts of the British empire be under four independent governments. It's easy to see how quickly each would shrink into comparative unimportance.

:::commentary
Jay chose a comparison his readers knew well. England and Scotland had shared a king since 1603, but they had separate parliaments until the Act of Union in 1707 joined them into the Kingdom of Great Britain. That union helped make Britain the world's leading naval and commercial power. Jay's point: the same could happen for America.

His example has one irony. In 1787, Ireland still had its own separate parliament under the British crown, and many Irish people resented British rule. Ireland was formally united with Great Britain in 1801, and most of it became independent in the 1920s.
:::

:::vocab
Nursery for seamen: A place where sailors are trained. A large merchant shipping industry trained sailors who could later serve in the navy.
:::

## What divided states couldn't do {orig=15-16}

Now apply these facts to our own case. Leave America divided into thirteen, or if you like, three or four independent governments. What armies could they raise and pay? What fleets could they ever hope to have?

If one were attacked, would the others rush to help it, and spend their blood and money defending it? Wouldn't there be a danger that the enemy's tempting promises would persuade them to stay neutral? Or that too great a love of peace would lead them to refuse to risk their own calm and safety for neighbors they may have been jealous of, and whose weakening they were happy to see? Such behavior wouldn't be wise, but it would be natural. The history of the states of Greece, and of other countries, is full of examples. And it's quite likely that what has happened so often would happen again in similar circumstances.

But suppose they were willing to help the invaded state or confederacy. How, when, and in what amounts would they provide soldiers and money? Who would command the allied armies, and which government would give him orders? Who would decide the terms of peace? And if they disagreed, what referee would decide between them and make them accept the decision?

Many difficulties and problems would be unavoidable in such a situation. But one government, watching over the general and shared interests, and combining and directing the powers and resources of the whole, would be free of all these problems. It would do far more for the people's safety.

:::commentary
Jay's questions about who would command and who would make peace weren't hypothetical. During the Revolution, Congress struggled constantly to get states to send their share of troops and money. Each state wanted its own militia used to defend its own territory first. Washington complained bitterly about this. Jay is reminding readers that even with a common enemy, coordinating separate governments was a nightmare.
:::

## Foreign nations will see us as we are {orig=17}

Whatever our situation, whether firmly united under one national government or split into several confederacies, foreign nations will know and see it exactly as it is. And they will act toward us accordingly.

If they see that our national government is effective and well run, our trade wisely regulated, our militia well organized and trained, our resources and finances carefully managed, our credit restored, and our people free, content, and united, they will be much more inclined to seek our friendship than to provoke our anger.

But suppose they find us either without an effective government, with each state doing right or wrong as its leaders see fit, or split into three or four independent and probably quarreling republics or confederacies. One might lean toward Britain, another toward France, and a third toward Spain, and the three powers might play them against each other. What a poor, pitiful figure America would make in their eyes! How exposed America would be, not only to their contempt but to their attacks. And how soon painful experience would teach us that when a people or a family divides, it always ends up working against itself.

:::quote
...when a people or family so divide, it never fails to be against themselves.

Meaning: A divided people or family hurts only itself. This echoes the Bible's warning that "a house divided against itself cannot stand," which Abraham Lincoln later made famous.
:::

:::today
Jay's argument that strength discourages attack is still a basic idea in foreign policy, often called **deterrence**. It shaped America's alliances like NATO, which commits members to defend each other, solving exactly the problem Jay describes of separate nations deciding whether to help a neighbor. Debates continue over how much military strength is enough, and whether strength prevents wars or sometimes provokes them. Jay's warning about foreign powers playing divided states against each other also still applies to divided nations around the world.
:::
