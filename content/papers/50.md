:::summary
In No. 49, Madison rejected the idea of calling on the people whenever the branches clashed. Now he considers a different version: reviewing the government at **regular, scheduled times**. He finds it hardly better. If reviews come often, they will be just as heated as occasional appeals. If they come rarely, the distant threat won't stop lawmakers today, and the damage will be done or deeply rooted by the time anyone looks. Madison then examines the one real test of the idea, Pennsylvania's **Council of Censors** of 1783–84. Its leading members were partisans who had helped make the very decisions they were judging. The council split into two fixed parties, may have misread the constitution itself, and had no real effect. It proved the disease existed, Madison concludes, but also that the remedy didn't work.
:::

:::keypoints
- Madison is asking only whether regular reviews can **enforce** the Constitution, not whether they are a good way to **change** it.
- **Frequent** reviews would be distorted by the same passions as occasional appeals.
- **Rare** reviews would be too distant to restrain lawmakers, too late to undo the harm, and too late to uproot old abuses.
- Pennsylvania's **Council of Censors** actually tried the idea, and its record confirms Madison's reasoning.
- Its leaders had been **party leaders and officials** who had helped make or fight the measures they were reviewing.
- It split into **two fixed parties** that voted the same way on every question, proof that "PASSION, not REASON," was in charge.
- Its rulings were questionable and were **ignored** in practice.
- Parties will always exist in a free country. Their disappearance would mean either a national emergency or "an absolute extinction of liberty."
:::

:::context Background
Pennsylvania's 1776 constitution was the most democratic in America. It had a one-house legislature, no single governor, and a unique watchdog: a **Council of Censors**, elected by the people every seven years. The council's job was to review whether the government had obeyed the constitution. It met for the first and only time in 1783–84. Madison already used its findings in [No. 48](/papers/48/) to show that Pennsylvania's legislature had broken the rules. Here he uses its record to show that the watchdog itself failed.

This is the fourth of Madison's five essays on separation of powers (Nos. 47–51), and the shortest.
:::

## What about regular reviews? {orig=1-2}

Some may argue that *occasional* appeals to the people have the problems I described, but that *periodic* appeals, made at fixed times, are the right way to prevent and correct violations of the constitution.

Please note that I am looking at these ideas only as ways to *enforce* the constitution, by keeping each branch within its proper limits. I am not considering them as ways to *change* the constitution itself.

From the first point of view, appeals to the people at fixed times seem nearly as unsuitable as appeals made whenever a problem comes up. If the reviews are close together, the measures being reviewed will be recent. They will be tied up with all the circumstances that tend to spoil and twist the results of occasional reviews.

If the reviews are far apart, the same will still be true of all the recent measures. Older measures might be reviewed more calmly because of the passage of time. But that advantage comes with drawbacks that seem to cancel it out:

- **A distant threat is a weak restraint.** A far-off chance of public blame would do little to hold back power when present motives push it to excess. Imagine a legislature of one or two hundred members, eager to reach some favorite goal and breaking through the constitution's limits to get it. Would they really be stopped by the thought that their conduct might be reviewed ten, fifteen, or twenty years from now?
- **The harm would already be done.** The abuses would often have finished their damage before the remedy was applied.
- **Old abuses are hard to remove.** Even where that wasn't so, the abuses would be long-standing. They would have taken deep root and would not be easy to pull up.

:::commentary
Madison sets up a dilemma, a choice between two bad options. Short gaps between reviews mean the reviewers will be judging fresh, hot political fights. Long gaps mean the reviews come too late to matter. Either way, the plan fails.

Notice his distinction between *enforcing* and *altering* a constitution. Madison isn't against changing the Constitution through a proper process. The Constitution's Article V provides one, and Madison himself later led the effort in the first Congress that produced the Bill of Rights. What he doubts is that the people, voting at set times, can act as an everyday referee between the branches.
:::

## Pennsylvania's experiment {orig=3}

The plan of reviewing the constitution at set times, to correct recent violations as well as for other purposes, has actually been tried in one of the states. As we have seen, one of the goals of the Council of Censors, which met in Pennsylvania in 1783 and 1784, was to inquire "whether the constitution had been violated, and whether the legislative and executive departments had encroached upon each other."

This important and new political experiment deserves close attention for several reasons. In some ways, since it was a single experiment made under rather unusual circumstances, it may not be completely conclusive. But for the question we are considering, it involves certain facts that I believe fully and clearly illustrate my reasoning.

:::vocab
Council of Censors: A body created by Pennsylvania's 1776 constitution. Every seven years it was supposed to review whether the government had obeyed the constitution.
Censorial: Having to do with reviewing and judging conduct, like a censor. In ancient Rome, censors were officials who kept the list of citizens and judged public morals.
:::

## What the council's record shows {orig=4-8}

**First,** the names of the men on the council show that at least some of its most active members had also been active leaders in the parties that already existed in the state.

**Second,** these same active leaders of the council had been active and influential members of the legislature and the executive during the very period being reviewed. Some had even supported or opposed the very measures they were now testing against the constitution. Two members had been vice-presidents of the state, and several others had served on the executive council, within the previous seven years. One had been speaker of the legislative assembly, and several others had been leading members of it during the same period.

**Third,** every page of the council's records shows how all this affected the mood of its debates. For as long as the council met, it was split into two fixed and bitter parties. The members themselves admitted this and regretted it. But even if they hadn't, the records themselves give equally clear proof. On every question, no matter how unimportant or unrelated to the others, the same names always appear lined up against each other on opposite sides.

Any fair-minded observer can safely conclude, without meaning to criticize either party or anyone in it, that unfortunately PASSION, not REASON, must have guided their decisions. When people use their reason calmly and freely on many separate questions, they are bound to disagree on some of them. When they are ruled by a shared passion, their opinions, if we can even call them that, will always be the same.

**Fourth,** it is at least doubtful whether the council's decisions, in several cases, got the limits of the legislature and the executive wrong, instead of holding those branches within their proper places.

**Fifth,** I have never heard that the council's decisions on constitutional questions, whether right or wrong, changed the way the legislature actually interpreted the constitution. In fact, if I'm not mistaken, in one case the legislature of the time rejected the council's interpretation and actually won the dispute.

:::commentary
Madison's third point is a sharp test of whether a group is thinking or just taking sides. If the same members always vote together on every question, even unrelated ones, they are not weighing each issue on its merits. They are voting by team. Political scientists today still look at voting patterns this way, and many observers note that votes in modern legislatures often split along party lines in just this fashion.

The council was divided between the supporters of Pennsylvania's 1776 constitution, known as the Constitutionalists, and its critics, who wanted to replace it with a more balanced government. The Council of Censors never met again. In 1790, Pennsylvania adopted a new constitution with a two-house legislature and a single governor, and it dropped the council.
:::

## The disease and the failed cure {orig=9}

So this review body proves two things at once. Its investigations prove that the disease exists. And its own example proves that the remedy doesn't work.

:::quote
This censorial body, therefore, proves at the same time, by its researches, the existence of the disease, and by its example, the inefficacy of the remedy.

Meaning: The Council of Censors found real violations of the constitution, which proves the problem is real. But it couldn't fix them, which proves that this kind of review is not the solution. "Inefficacy" means failure to produce the desired effect.
:::

## Parties will always exist {orig=10}

Someone might object that Pennsylvania, at that moment and for a long time before, was overheated and torn apart by party conflict. But that doesn't weaken my conclusion. Can we assume that at any future seven-year review, the same state will be free of parties? Can we assume that any other state, at that time or any other, will be free of them?

We should neither assume nor wish for such a thing. The disappearance of parties would have to mean one of two things: either the whole country is united by fear for its safety, or liberty has been completely destroyed.

:::quote
...an extinction of parties necessarily implies either a universal alarm for the public safety, or an absolute extinction of liberty.

Meaning: In a free country, people will always divide into parties. The only times they don't are during a crisis that unites everyone, or under a tyranny that doesn't allow disagreement.
:::

:::commentary
This line connects to Madison's famous argument in [No. 10](/papers/10/): "Liberty is to faction what air is to fire." Parties and factions are the price of freedom. You can't get rid of them without getting rid of liberty itself. So any plan that only works when people are free of party spirit is a plan that won't work in a free country.

It also echoes No. 49's point that the first state constitutions were written during the Revolution, when a common danger held down the usual party divisions.
:::

## Why barring officials won't help {orig=11}

Suppose we took a precaution. We could bar anyone who had served in the government during the period under review from the assemblies the people elect to review it. That still wouldn't solve the problem.

This important job would probably fall to men who were less capable and, in other ways, hardly better qualified. They might not have personally served in the government, and so might not have directly carried out the measures being examined. But they would probably have been involved in the parties connected with those measures, and they would have been elected with those parties' support.

:::connection
In [No. 49](/papers/49/), Madison rejected occasional appeals to the people. Having now rejected scheduled ones as well, he gives his own answer in [No. 51](/papers/51/): build the government so that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition." For the Council of Censors' findings about Pennsylvania's legislature, see [No. 48](/papers/48/).
:::

:::today
Pennsylvania's Council of Censors was copied by Vermont, which kept its own council until 1870, but the idea has otherwise disappeared from American government. Today, disputes about whether a branch has overstepped its constitutional limits are mostly settled by the courts, along with elections, impeachment, and the branches' own checks on each other. Madison's warning about party spirit is still widely discussed. Some observers see today's party-line votes in Congress as proof of his point that people "governed by a common passion" all vote the same way. Others argue that parties help voters make sense of politics and hold leaders accountable.
:::
