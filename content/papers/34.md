:::summary
Hamilton argues that sharing the power to tax between the nation and the states is both workable and wise. Critics said two governments taxing the same people couldn't work in theory, but ancient Rome had two rival lawmaking assemblies for centuries. Then Hamilton compares what each government will need to spend. The states' costs will be small and predictable, mostly the salaries of their officials. The nation's costs, mainly for war and defense, have no limit, and a constitution must plan for centuries, not just for today. Europe's debts came almost entirely from wars, and no one can promise America will stay at peace. So it would make no sense to set aside a large source of revenue for the states alone. Shared, or **concurrent**, taxing power is the only arrangement that gives the Union unlimited means while letting the states meet their own needs.
:::

:::keypoints
- Under the Constitution, the states would have **equal** taxing power with the Union except for import duties, which leaves them most of the country's resources.
- Arguing that two taxing powers **can't** exist is theory against fact. Rome had two independent lawmaking assemblies, each able to undo the other's laws, for ages.
- America's case is easier: **neither government can cancel the other's taxes**.
- A constitution must be built for **"the probable exigencies of ages,"** not just today's needs. Future dangers can't be measured, so the power to meet them can't safely be limited.
- War is likely. Trouble is brewing in Europe, and peace or war **won't always be our choice**.
- **Wars and rebellions** cause most government spending and debt. Ordinary state costs are small by comparison.
- Once their war debts are paid, the states will need **less than 200,000 pounds** a year each, while the Union's needs have no limit.
- Any fixed split of tax sources would give the states **too much or too little**. Concurrent power is the only sensible solution.
:::

:::context Background
In Nos. 32 and 33, Hamilton showed that under the Constitution the states keep their own power to tax everything except imports and exports. Here he answers a different objection: that this arrangement wouldn't work, and that each government should instead get its own separate sources of tax money. Many Anti-Federalists wanted the national government limited to import duties, leaving all "internal" taxes to the states.

In early 1788, when this essay appeared, Americans had just come through an eight-year war with Britain. The national government owed large debts from that war, and so did many of the states.
:::

## The states will have plenty to work with {orig=1}

I believe my last paper clearly showed that, under the proposed Constitution, the individual states would have EQUAL authority with the Union over revenue, except for duties on imports. This leaves the states by far the greatest part of the community's resources. So there is no basis at all for saying they wouldn't have as much money as they could want for their own needs, free from all outside control. That this field is wide enough will become even clearer when we look at how small a share of public expenses the state governments will have to pay.

:::vocab
Revenue: Money a government collects, mainly through taxes, to pay for its work.
Coequal: Equal in rank or power.
:::

## Theory against fact: the example of Rome {orig=2-3}

Some argue from abstract principles that this shared authority cannot exist. To do so is to set guesses and theory against fact and reality. Reasoning like that might show that something OUGHT NOT TO EXIST. But it must be completely rejected when it is used to prove that something does not exist, against the clear evidence that it does.

It is well known that in the Roman republic, final lawmaking power belonged for ages to two different political bodies. They were not two branches of the same legislature. They were separate and independent legislatures, and opposite interests ruled in each. In one, the patricians, or nobles, had the upper hand. In the other, the plebeians, or common people, did.

Many arguments could have been made to prove that two such seemingly contradictory bodies, each with power to CANCEL or REPEAL the other's acts, were unworkable. But anyone in Rome who tried to prove they didn't exist would have been thought mad.

I am, of course, referring to the COMITIA CENTURIATA and the COMITIA TRIBUTA. In the first, the people voted by "centuries," or voting groups, arranged to give the advantage to the patricians. In the second, numbers won, and the plebeians were fully in control. Yet these two legislatures existed side by side for ages, and the Roman republic rose to the greatest heights of human achievement.

In the case we are considering, there is no contradiction like the one in this example. Neither government has any power to cancel the acts of the other. And in practice, there is little reason to fear any trouble. Before long, the states' needs will naturally shrink into A VERY NARROW RANGE. In the meantime, the United States will most likely find it convenient to stay away entirely from the sources of tax money that the states prefer to use.

:::vocab
Patrician: A member of ancient Rome's noble families.
Plebeian: A common citizen of ancient Rome.
Comitia: Latin for "assemblies." The Roman republic had several assemblies where citizens voted on laws and chose officials.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's opponents argued that two governments taxing the same people was a contradiction, like two captains steering the same ship. Hamilton's answer is a favorite one in these essays: experience beats theory. If something has actually worked, it's foolish to argue that it can't.

Rome's system was more complicated than Hamilton's short summary suggests, and historians still debate exactly how its assemblies were organized and what powers each had. But his basic point holds: Rome had more than one popular assembly that could pass laws, and the republic lasted for centuries. Hamilton also says the American case is easier, since neither government can cancel the other's taxes. They can only both tax.
:::

## A constitution must plan for centuries {orig=4}

To judge this question more precisely, we should compare the things the national government will have to pay for with the things the states will have to pay for. We will find that the national needs are completely unlimited, while the state needs stay within quite moderate limits.

As we look into this, we must remember not to focus only on the present. We must look far into the future. Constitutions should not be designed just to meet today's needs. They should be designed for today's needs combined with the likely needs of future ages, based on the natural and tested course of human affairs.

So nothing could be more misleading than to judge how much power the national government should have from an estimate of what it needs right now. There should be a CAPACITY to meet future emergencies as they happen. And since these emergencies have no limits by nature, it isn't safe to limit that capacity.

It may be true that someone could estimate, accurately enough, how much revenue would pay off the Union's current debts and keep up the services that would be enough in peacetime for some time to come. But would it be wise to stop there? Wouldn't it be the height of foolishness to leave the government responsible for national defense completely unable to protect the community from future threats to the peace, whether foreign war or rebellion at home?

If, instead, we should go beyond this point, where can we stop, short of an unlimited power to meet emergencies as they arise? It's easy to say, in general terms, that we can make a reasonable judgment about how to prepare for likely dangers. But we can safely challenge anyone who says so to show us their facts. We can confidently say those facts would be as vague and uncertain as any that might be offered to predict how long the world will last.

Looking only at the chance of attacks inside our borders tells us little, and even that can't be calculated in any satisfying way. But if we mean to be a trading people, our policy must include being able, one day, to defend that trade. Supporting a navy and fighting wars at sea would involve so many unknowns that they would defeat every effort of political arithmetic.

:::quote
Constitutions of civil government are not to be framed upon a calculation of existing exigencies, but upon a combination of these with the probable exigencies of ages, according to the natural and tried course of human affairs.

Meaning: A constitution shouldn't be written only for today's problems. It must be ready for the problems of future generations, too.
:::

:::vocab
Exigency: An urgent need or emergency.
Contingency: Something that might happen in the future but can't be predicted for sure.
Political arithmetic: An old term for using numbers and statistics to study a nation's population, wealth, and finances.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Hamilton's most important ideas about constitutions. A law can be changed next year, but a constitution is meant to last for generations. So it shouldn't be tied to the conditions of one moment. His prediction about a navy came true quickly. Congress began building a navy in the 1790s, and within a few years American warships were fighting at sea against France (the Quasi-War of 1798–1800) and against the Barbary states of North Africa, whose ships had been seizing American merchant ships.
:::

## We can't count on peace {orig=5}

Suppose we decide to try the new and absurd experiment of forbidding the government to wage an offensive war for reasons of state. Even so, we certainly shouldn't make it unable to protect the community against the ambition or hostility of other nations.

For some time, a cloud has been hanging over Europe. If it breaks into a storm, who can promise that some of its fury won't fall on us? No reasonable person would quickly declare that we are completely out of its reach. Or suppose the explosive materials now gathering scatter without catching fire, or a fire starts but doesn't spread to us. What guarantee do we have that our peace will long stay undisturbed by some other cause, from some other direction?

Let us remember that peace or war won't always be our choice. However moderate and unambitious we may be, we can't count on the moderation of others, or hope to put out their ambition. At the end of the last war, who could have imagined that France and Britain, tired and drained as they both were, would so soon be glaring at each other with hostility?

To judge from human history, we have to conclude that the fiery and destructive passions of war rule the human heart much more powerfully than the gentle and kind feelings of peace. To build our political systems on hopes of lasting peace is to rely on the weaker parts of human character.

:::quote
...to model our political systems upon speculations of lasting tranquillity, is to calculate on the weaker springs of the human character.

Meaning: Planning a government on the hope that there will always be peace is a mistake. Human nature makes war more likely than lasting peace.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's warning about Europe was soon proven right. In 1787, Britain and France came close to war over a crisis in the Netherlands. A few years later, the French Revolution began in 1789, and from 1792 to 1815 Europe was almost constantly at war. America was pulled in, as Hamilton feared. French and British ships both seized American merchant vessels. The United States fought an undeclared naval war with France and then the War of 1812 with Britain, in which British troops burned Washington, D.C.

Notice that Hamilton calls a ban on offensive war a "novel and absurd experiment." Some later constitutions in other countries did renounce war, such as Japan's after World War II. Whether such limits work, or are wise, is still debated.
:::

## Wars are what cost money {orig=6-7}

What are the main sources of expense in every government? What has caused the enormous debts that weigh down several European nations? The answer is plainly wars and rebellions, and the upkeep of the institutions needed to guard the nation against these two deadliest diseases of society.

Now consider the costs of a state's ordinary internal government: its police and public order, its legislative, executive, and judicial branches with their offices, and the encouragement of farming and manufacturing. These cover almost everything the states will spend money on. Yet they are tiny compared with the costs of national defense.

In Great Britain, the nation must pay for all the showy display of a monarchy. Even so, no more than about a fifteenth of the nation's yearly income goes to these ordinary costs. The other fourteen-fifteenths go to paying interest on debts from the wars Britain has fought, and to keeping up its fleets and armies.

Someone might say that the costs of a monarchy's ambitious adventures and vain pursuits are not a fair guide to what a republic would need. But we should also remember that the waste and luxury of a rich kingdom's home government should be just as much greater than the thrift that suits the modest simplicity of a republic. If we fairly reduce both sides, the proportion may still hold.

:::vocab
Body politic: A nation or people thought of as a single living body.
Police: In Hamilton's time, a broad word for a government's ordinary internal rules and order, not only law enforcement officers.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's figure for Britain shows how much war dominated government budgets in the 1700s. Britain had fought a long series of costly wars with France, and borrowed heavily to pay for them. The interest on that debt was one of its largest expenses.

This was largely true of the United States for a long time, too. For most of its first century and a half, the national government's biggest costs were the military, interest on war debts, and pensions for veterans. That changed in the 1900s. Today, programs like Social Security and Medicare make up a much larger share of national spending than the military.
:::

## The states' needs are small; the nation's are unlimited {orig=8-9}

Now think about the large debt we ourselves took on in a single war. Assume only an ordinary share of the events that disturb the peace of nations. We will see at once, without any detailed explanation, that national spending must always be far, far greater than state spending.

It's true that several states, on their own, are weighed down with large debts left over from the last war. But this can't happen again if the proposed system is adopted. Once these debts are paid off, the only important need for revenue the state governments will still have is to support their own civil list, meaning their officials' salaries. Adding in every other possible cost, the total for each state should come to well under two hundred thousand pounds.

In designing a government for future generations as well as ourselves, the parts meant to be permanent should be based on permanent, not temporary, causes of expense. If this principle is right, we would need to provide the state governments with about two hundred thousand pounds a year, while the Union's needs would have no limit, even in imagination.

Seen this way, by what logic can anyone argue that the local governments should control, forever, an EXCLUSIVE source of revenue for any amount beyond two hundred thousand pounds? To give the states more than that, and to SHUT OUT the Union, would take the community's resources out of the hands that need them for the public welfare. It would put them in other hands that would have no fair or proper use for them.

:::vocab
Civil list: The salaries and expenses of a government's non-military officials.
Posterity: Future generations.
Pound: The British unit of money. In 1788, most states still kept their accounts in pounds. The national government began minting its own dollar coins in the 1790s.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Hamilton's predictions that turned out badly wrong. He expected state budgets to stay tiny. Today, state and local governments spend enormous sums on things Hamilton's generation barely imagined as government jobs, such as public schools, highways, universities, prisons, and health care. In fact, state and local governments together employ far more people than the national government does.

His other prediction, that the states wouldn't pile up war debts again, did come true, partly because of his own work. As Treasury Secretary, he persuaded Congress in 1790 to have the national government take over the states' Revolutionary War debts. From then on, wars were paid for mainly by the nation.
:::

## Any fixed split would be too much or too little {orig=10}

Suppose, then, the Convention had wanted to divide the sources of revenue between the Union and the states in PROPORTION to what each needed. What particular source could have been chosen for the states that would not be either too much or too little? It would be too little for their present needs, and too much for their future needs.

Consider the line between external and internal taxes. At a rough estimate, it would give the states control of two-thirds of the community's resources to pay from a tenth to a twentieth of its expenses. It would give the Union one-third of the community's resources to pay from nine-tenths to nineteen-twentieths of its expenses.

Suppose we drop that line and give the states an exclusive power to tax only houses and land. There would still be a great mismatch between the MEANS and the END. The states would have one-third of the community's resources to meet, at most, one-tenth of its needs.

And if a source could have been chosen that exactly matched the states' needs and no more, it would not have been enough to pay off the states' existing debts. That would have left them dependent on the Union to pay them.

:::commentary
Here Hamilton puts numbers on his argument. His figures are rough estimates, as he admits. But they make the point vivid. Under the critics' plan, the states would get most of the money while the Union would face most of the costs. That is backward, by Hamilton's rule that the means should match the end.
:::

## Why sharing is the only answer {orig=11}

These observations support the position I have stated elsewhere: that "A CONCURRENT JURISDICTION in the article of taxation was the only admissible substitute for an entire subordination, in respect to this branch of power, of State authority to that of the Union." In other words, shared taxing power was the only acceptable alternative to putting the states completely under the Union in matters of taxation.

Any division of the sources of revenue that could have been chosen would have sacrificed the great INTERESTS of the Union to the POWER of the individual states. The Convention thought shared power was better than putting the states completely under the Union. And this arrangement at least has the merit of combining an unlimited constitutional power of taxation in the national government with an adequate and independent power in the states to provide for their own needs.

There are a few other aspects of this important subject of taxation that still need to be considered.

:::vocab
Concurrent jurisdiction: Authority shared by two governments at the same time. Both the nation and the states can tax most of the same things.
Subordination: Being placed under the control of a higher authority.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's idea that a constitution should be built for "the probable exigencies of ages" comes up again and again in American debates. Some people say the Constitution's broad language was meant to let the government meet new challenges the founders couldn't foresee. Others say that's exactly why the Constitution's limits must be kept firm, so that "emergencies" don't become an excuse for endless growth in power. His warning that peace "will not always be left to our option" also still shapes debates over military spending. Supporters of a strong military say it prevents wars by discouraging attackers. Critics say large military budgets take money from other needs and can make war more tempting.
:::
