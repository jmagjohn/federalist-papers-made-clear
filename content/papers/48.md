:::summary
In No. 47, Madison showed that separation of powers doesn't require the branches to be completely unconnected. Now he goes further: the branches **must** be connected enough for each to check the others, or separation won't last. Simply writing boundaries into a constitution, which Madison calls **"parchment barriers,"** doesn't work. Americans have spent so much energy fearing kings that they've overlooked the real danger in a republic: the **legislature**, which is "drawing all power into its impetuous vortex." Its powers are broad and vague, and it controls the money. Madison proves his point with two examples: Thomas Jefferson's account of Virginia, and a review of Pennsylvania's government by its Council of Censors.
:::

:::keypoints
- Unless the branches are **connected enough to check each other**, the separation of powers can't be kept in practice.
- Power naturally **expands**, so each branch needs real protection against the others.
- Writing boundaries on paper, **"parchment barriers,"** has proved far too weak.
- **"The legislative department is everywhere extending the sphere of its activity, and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex."**
- Americans focused on the danger of kings and **forgot the danger of legislatures**.
- In a republic, the legislature is the most dangerous branch: it's confident, passionate, and big enough to feel strong emotions but small enough to act on them.
- Its powers are **broad and hard to define**, so it can disguise power grabs. It also controls **money**, including the salaries of other officials.
- **Virginia:** Jefferson wrote that all power had flowed to the legislature, creating an **"elective despotism."**
- **Pennsylvania:** Its Council of Censors found the legislature had repeatedly violated the state constitution.
:::

:::context Background
This is the second of Madison's five essays on separation of powers (Nos. 47–51). After the Revolution, Americans wrote state constitutions designed above all to prevent tyranny by a king or governor. Most made governors weak and gave legislatures sweeping power. By 1787, many leaders, including Madison, had concluded that this was a mistake. State legislatures had passed laws canceling debts, printing paper money, overturning court decisions, and interfering with the executive. Madison had served in the Virginia legislature and seen it firsthand.
:::

## The branches must be connected {orig=1-2}

The last essay showed that the political rule we examined there does not require the legislative, executive, and judicial branches to be completely unconnected. Next, I will show that unless the branches are connected and blended enough to give each one a constitutional check on the others, the degree of separation that the rule requires for free government can never really be maintained in practice.

Everyone agrees that the powers that properly belong to one branch shouldn't be directly and completely exercised by either of the other branches. It's equally clear that no branch should have, directly or indirectly, an overpowering influence over the others in using their own powers. No one will deny that power tends to expand, and that it must be effectively kept from going past its assigned limits.

So once we have separated the kinds of power in theory, as legislative, executive, or judicial, the next and hardest task is to give each branch some practical protection against invasion by the others. What that protection should be is the great problem to solve.

:::quote
It will not be denied, that power is of an encroaching nature, and that it ought to be effectually restrained from passing the limits assigned to it.

Meaning: Power always tends to spread and take over more ground, so it has to be actively held back.
:::

:::vocab
Apothegm: A short, wise saying. Madison means the rule that the branches of government should be separate.
Encroaching: Gradually pushing into someone else's territory or powers.
:::

## "Parchment barriers" don't work {orig=3}

Is it enough to mark the boundaries of these branches precisely in the constitution, and trust these parchment barriers to stop power's tendency to spread? This is the protection that the authors of most American constitutions seem to have mainly relied on. But experience shows that this protection has been greatly overrated. Some stronger defense is absolutely necessary for the weaker branches against the more powerful one.

The legislative branch everywhere is expanding its activity and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex.

:::quote
The legislative department is everywhere extending the sphere of its activity, and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex.

Meaning: Legislatures keep expanding their power, pulling everything toward them like a whirlpool. "Impetuous" means forceful and rash, and a "vortex" is a whirlpool or tornado.
:::

:::vocab
Parchment barriers: Limits that exist only in writing. Parchment is the treated animal skin that important documents were written on.
:::

:::commentary
"Parchment barriers" is one of Madison's most famous phrases. His point is that writing "the branches shall be separate" in a constitution means nothing if the branches don't have the tools to defend themselves. Many countries have had constitutions with beautiful promises that were ignored in practice. Madison wants something stronger than words: a structure where each branch can actually fight back. He gives his answer in [No. 51](/papers/51/).
:::

## Americans feared the wrong danger {orig=4-5}

The founders of our state governments deserve so much credit for their wisdom that no task is less pleasant than pointing out their mistakes. Still, respect for the truth requires us to say this. They seem never, for a moment, to have looked away from the danger to liberty posed by a hereditary king with overgrown, grasping powers, supported by a hereditary branch of the legislature. They seem never to have remembered the danger of power grabs by the legislature, which, by gathering all power into the same hands, must lead to the same tyranny as power grabs by an executive.

In a government where many broad powers are placed in the hands of a hereditary king, the executive branch is rightly seen as the source of danger. It should be watched with all the suspicion that a love of liberty inspires.

In a democracy, a crowd of people exercise legislative power in person. Because they can't deliberate in an orderly way or plan together, they are constantly exposed to the ambitious schemes of their executive officials. So there, too, tyranny may well rise from the executive at some convenient moment.

But a representative republic is different. There, the executive is carefully limited in both the extent and the length of its power. The legislative power is held by an assembly that feels confident and bold in its own strength, because it believes it has influence over the people. It is large enough to feel all the passions that drive a crowd, but not so large that it can't pursue those passions with the methods reason suggests. It is against the bold ambition of this branch that the people should direct all their suspicion and use all their precautions.

:::commentary
Madison makes a sharp point about the American Revolution. Americans had just fought a king, so they designed governments to prevent another one. But in a republic, the king is gone and the executive is weak. The new danger is a legislature that believes it speaks for "the people" and so can do anything. Madison describes the legislature as just the right size to be dangerous: big enough to get swept up in passions like a mob, but small enough to organize and act on them.

This is the opposite of what many Americans worry about today. Many people now see the president as the branch most likely to overreach. Madison's warning was shaped by the 1780s, when state legislatures were all-powerful and governors had very little power.
:::

## Why the legislature has the advantage {orig=6}

Other circumstances also give the legislature an advantage in our governments. Its constitutional powers are broader and harder to limit precisely. So it can more easily disguise its attacks on the other branches behind complicated and indirect measures. Lawmakers often face a genuinely difficult question about whether a particular measure goes beyond the legislature's proper area.

On the other hand, executive power is confined to a narrower range and is simpler in nature. And the judiciary's boundaries are even more clearly marked. So any attempt by either of these branches to grab power would immediately expose and defeat itself.

That's not all. The legislature alone can reach into the people's pockets. Under some constitutions it has full control, and under all of them it has a dominant influence, over the pay of the people in the other branches. This makes the other branches dependent on the legislature, which makes its power grabs even easier.

:::commentary
Madison names two big advantages the legislature has:

- **Vague powers.** "Making laws" can cover almost anything, so a legislature can dress up a power grab as an ordinary law. By contrast, if a judge or governor overstepped, it would be obvious.
- **Control of money.** The legislature sets taxes and budgets, including the salaries of governors and judges. If it can cut your pay, it can pressure you.

The U.S. Constitution addresses the second problem directly. Congress can't change the president's salary during a term (Article II) or reduce judges' salaries at all (Article III).
:::

## Proof from Virginia: Jefferson's warning {orig=7-8}

I have appealed to our own experience to prove what I'm saying. If specific proof were needed, it could be multiplied endlessly. Every citizen who has taken part in or paid attention to public affairs could serve as a witness. I could collect plenty of evidence from the records of every state in the Union. But for a shorter and equally convincing proof, I'll point to the example of two states, confirmed by two reliable authorities.

The first example is Virginia. As we've seen, its constitution explicitly declares that the three great branches should not be mixed. The authority is Mr. Jefferson. Among his other advantages for observing how that government works, he was himself its governor. To fully convey what his experience taught him on this subject, I'll quote a fairly long passage from his very interesting *Notes on the State of Virginia*:

> "All the powers of government, legislative, executive, and judiciary, result to the legislative body. The concentrating these in the same hands, is precisely the definition of despotic government. It will be no alleviation, that these powers will be exercised by a plurality of hands, and not by a single one. One hundred and seventy-three despots would surely be as oppressive as one. Let those who doubt it, turn their eyes on the republic of Venice. As little will it avail us, that they are chosen by ourselves. An ELECTIVE DESPOTISM was not the government we fought for; but one which should not only be founded on free principles, but in which the powers of government should be so divided and balanced among several bodies of magistracy, as that no one could transcend their legal limits, without being effectually checked and restrained by the others..."

Jefferson goes on to explain that Virginia's constitution declared the branches separate, "BUT NO BARRIER WAS PROVIDED BETWEEN THESE SEVERAL POWERS." The judges and executive officials depended on the legislature for their pay, and some for keeping their jobs. So if the legislature took over executive or judicial powers, no one was likely to resist, and resistance wouldn't work anyway. The legislature could simply pass its decisions as laws, which bound the other branches. As a result, Jefferson wrote, it had "IN MANY instances, DECIDED RIGHTS which should have been left to JUDICIARY CONTROVERSY," and directing the executive branch during its sessions was "BECOMING HABITUAL AND FAMILIAR."

:::quote
One hundred and seventy-three despots would surely be as oppressive as one... An ELECTIVE DESPOTISM was not the government we fought for...

Meaning: Jefferson says it doesn't matter whether tyranny comes from one king or from 173 elected lawmakers. Being elected doesn't make unchecked power any less dangerous. (Virginia's legislature had 173 members.)
:::

:::vocab
Despotism: Rule by someone with unlimited power; tyranny.
Elective despotism: Tyranny by elected officials, Jefferson's phrase for a legislature with unchecked power.
Notes on the State of Virginia: Jefferson's only full-length book (1785), describing Virginia's geography, society, laws, and government.
:::

:::commentary
"Elective despotism" is a powerful idea: people can lose their liberty to officials they themselves elected. Elections alone don't guarantee freedom. The powers of government also have to be divided and balanced.

Madison and Jefferson were close friends and allies. Jefferson was in Paris as America's minister to France during the Constitutional Convention, but Madison kept him informed by letter. Citing Jefferson here also helped persuade readers, since Jefferson was famous as the author of the Declaration of Independence.
:::

## Proof from Pennsylvania: the Council of Censors {orig=9-15}

The other state I'll use as an example is Pennsylvania. The other authority is its Council of Censors, which met in 1783 and 1784. Part of this body's duty, under the state constitution, was "to inquire whether the constitution had been preserved inviolate in every part; and whether the legislative and executive branches of government had performed their duty as guardians of the people, or assumed to themselves, or exercised, other or greater powers than they are entitled to by the constitution."

To carry out this duty, the council had to compare the actions of both the legislature and the executive with their constitutional powers. Based on the facts it listed, most of which both sides on the council agreed were true, the legislature had openly violated the constitution in many important ways:

- **It passed a great number of laws without first printing them for the people to consider,** with no apparent need, even though the constitution required this. That rule was one of the main protections the constitution relied on against bad laws.
- **It violated the constitutional right to trial by jury,** and took powers the constitution had not given it.
- **It took over executive powers.**
- **It changed judges' salaries** from time to time, even though the constitution clearly required them to be fixed. And it often took over cases that belonged to the courts and decided them itself.

Anyone who wants the details under each of these headings can read the council's records, which have been published. Some of the violations can be blamed on special circumstances of the war. But most of them can be seen as the natural growth of a badly designed government.

The council also found that the executive branch had often broken the constitution. But three points should be made about this. First, many of these cases were caused directly by the needs of the war, or were recommended by Congress or the commander in chief. Second, in most of the other cases, the executive followed the stated or known wishes of the legislature. Third, Pennsylvania's executive is unusual among the states because of how many members it has. In this respect, it is as much like a legislative assembly as like an executive council. Since its members weren't held individually responsible for the group's actions, and since they drew confidence from each other's example and combined influence, they were naturally more willing to risk unauthorized actions than a single executive, or a small group, would be.

:::vocab
Council of Censors: A body created by Pennsylvania's 1776 constitution. Every seven years it was supposed to review whether the government had obeyed the constitution.
Usurp: To take power that doesn't rightfully belong to you.
:::

:::commentary
Pennsylvania's 1776 constitution was the most democratic of its time. It had a one-house legislature, no governor (instead, a twelve-member Executive Council), and the Council of Censors to check violations. Many people admired it, but Madison saw it as proof that a powerful legislature without real checks would break the rules. He discusses the Council of Censors again in [No. 50](/papers/50/), arguing that even this watchdog failed to stop the violations.

Notice Madison's point about Pennsylvania's large executive council. It echoes Hamilton's argument in [No. 70](/papers/70/): when power is shared among many people, no one feels personally responsible, so they take more risks.
:::

## Conclusion {orig=16}

The conclusion I'm entitled to draw from these observations is this: simply marking the constitutional limits of each branch on parchment is not enough to guard against the attacks that lead to the tyrannical concentration of all government power in the same hands.

:::today
Madison's warning about "parchment barriers" is often repeated by people who study constitutions around the world. Many countries have written constitutions with strong separation of powers that collapsed in practice, because one branch, often the executive, simply ignored the limits. In the United States, the debate is often reversed from Madison's day. Many people now worry about presidents taking power from Congress through executive orders and emergency declarations. Others worry about Congress handing its lawmaking power to federal agencies. Either way, Madison's central lesson still applies: limits on power only work if each branch has the tools and the will to defend its own role.
:::
