:::summary
Critics charged that the House of Representatives would be filled with wealthy men who had little sympathy for ordinary people. Madison calls this the strangest objection of all, because it attacks the basic idea of a republic: that the people can be trusted to choose their own leaders. The voters for the House will be the great body of the people, rich and poor alike. Anyone with merit can be elected, with no requirement of wealth, birth, religion, or profession. Representatives will be tied to the people by honor, gratitude, ambition, frequent elections, and the fact that every law they pass applies to them too. Finally, he shows that large election districts in Britain and in several American states have not produced rulers who betray the many.
:::

:::keypoints
- The aim of every constitution is to choose rulers with **wisdom and virtue**, and then to **keep them virtuous** while in office. The best tool for the second goal is short terms.
- The voters for the House are **"the great body of the people,"** the same people who vote for the larger house of their state legislature.
- **No qualification** of wealth, birth, religion, or profession limits who can be elected.
- Representatives are bound to the people by five things: their **merit**, **gratitude**, **ambition**, **frequent elections**, and having to **live under their own laws**.
- If Americans ever accept a law that binds the people but not the lawmakers, "the people will be prepared to tolerate any thing but liberty."
- The only real difference from state elections is that federal districts are **larger**. That makes good choices more likely, not less.
- Large districts in Britain and in New Hampshire, Massachusetts, New York, Pennsylvania, and Connecticut have **not** produced rulers who oppress the many.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 55](/papers/55/), Madison listed four objections to the House of Representatives. He answered the first (it is too small to be trusted) in No. 55 and the second (its members won't know local conditions) in [No. 56](/papers/56/). This paper answers the third: that the House will come from the wealthy, well-known class and will sacrifice "the many" to "the few." He answers the fourth in [No. 58](/papers/58/).

Anti-Federalist writers, such as the New York essayist "Brutus," argued that with so few members and such large districts, only famous and wealthy men could win. Ordinary farmers and workers, they said, would have no one like themselves in Congress. This was one of the most serious democratic criticisms of the Constitution.
:::

## A charge that strikes at the root of republics {orig=1-3}

The **third** charge against the House of Representatives is that its members will come from the class of citizens with the least sympathy for the mass of the people. Critics say such members will be most likely to sacrifice the many to raise up the few.

Of all the objections made against the federal Constitution, this may be the most extraordinary. It is aimed at an imagined **oligarchy**, or rule by a few. But the principle behind it strikes at the very root of republican government.

The aim of every political constitution is, or should be, first to choose as rulers the people with the most wisdom to see, and the most virtue to pursue, the common good of society. Next, it should take the most effective precautions to keep them virtuous for as long as they hold their public trust. Choosing rulers by election is the defining method of republican government. This form of government relies on many different ways to keep its rulers from becoming corrupt. The most effective one is limiting the length of their terms, so that they stay properly responsible to the people.

:::vocab
Oligarchy: Government by a small group of people, usually the rich or powerful.
Republic: A government in which the people elect representatives to make decisions for them, instead of deciding everything directly.
:::

:::quote
The aim of every political constitution is, or ought to be, first to obtain for rulers men who possess most wisdom to discern, and most virtue to pursue, the common good of the society; and in the next place, to take the most effectual precautions for keeping them virtuous whilst they continue to hold their public trust.

Meaning: A good constitution does two jobs. It helps the people pick wise, good leaders, and then it keeps those leaders honest once they have power.
:::

## Who votes, and who can serve {orig=4-6}

So let me ask: what feature of the House of Representatives violates the principles of republican government, or favors raising up the few on the ruins of the many? On the contrary, isn't every feature strictly in line with these principles, and carefully fair to the rights and claims of every class and kind of citizen?

Who will elect the federal representatives? Not the rich more than the poor. Not the educated more than the uneducated. Not the proud heirs of famous family names more than the humble children of unknown and unlucky families. The voters will be the great body of the people of the United States. They will be the same people who, in each state, have the right to elect the matching branch of the state legislature.

Who can the people choose? Every citizen whose merit may earn him the respect and trust of his country. No requirement of wealth, of birth, of religious faith, or of profession is allowed to tie the people's judgment or block their wishes.

:::quote
Who are to be the electors of the federal representatives? Not the rich, more than the poor; not the learned, more than the ignorant; not the haughty heirs of distinguished names, more than the humble sons of obscurity and unpropitious fortune.

Meaning: Rich and poor, educated and uneducated, famous and unknown will all have the same vote for the House.
:::


:::commentary
Madison is describing Article I, Section 2, which says voters for the House "shall have the Qualifications requisite for Electors of the most numerous Branch of the State Legislature." In other words, the Constitution let each state decide who could vote. That sounds fair to all classes, but it also meant the national electorate was only as broad as the states allowed.

In 1788, that was quite limited. Most states required voters to own property or pay taxes. Women could not vote, with a short-lived exception in New Jersey, where some property-owning women voted until 1807. Enslaved people could not vote, and free Black men could vote in only some states. So "the great body of the people" really meant mostly white men, and in many states only those with some property. Over time, amendments widened the right to vote, including the 15th (race, 1870), 19th (sex, 1920), 24th (poll taxes in federal elections, 1964), and 26th (age 18, 1971).

Madison's point about candidates was accurate, though. The Constitution set no property requirement for members of Congress, and Article VI bans any religious test for federal office. Many state constitutions at the time did require officeholders to own property.
:::

## What ties representatives to the people {orig=7-10}

Now consider the situation of the men whom their fellow citizens may freely choose as representatives. We will find it includes every protection that could be designed or wished for to keep them faithful to their constituents.

**First**, since they will have been chosen over others by their fellow citizens, we may assume that they will generally stand out for the qualities that earned them that choice. These qualities also promise a sincere and careful respect for the duties they have taken on.

**Second**, they will enter public service under conditions that are sure to create at least a temporary affection for their constituents. Every human heart is sensitive to signs of honor, favor, respect, and trust. Apart from any thought of self-interest, this feeling is some guarantee that they will repay that trust with gratitude and goodwill. Ingratitude is a common complaint against human nature. And it must be admitted that examples of it are all too frequent and shocking, both in public and in private life. But the widespread and intense anger that ingratitude causes is itself proof of how strong and common the opposite feeling is.

**Third**, the ties that bind the representative to his constituents are strengthened by more selfish motives. His pride and vanity attach him to a form of government that supports his ambitions and gives him a share of its honors. A few highly ambitious men may have other hopes or plans. But in general, most men who rise because of their influence with the people will have more to gain by keeping the people's favor than by changing the government to undermine the people's authority.

:::vocab
Constituents: The people a representative serves.
Fidelity: Faithfulness or loyalty.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's list of ties is realistic. He doesn't rely only on good character. He also counts on gratitude and even on selfish ambition. A politician who owes his career to the voters has a personal reason to keep them happy. This is the same "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" thinking he uses in [No. 51](/papers/51/): build a system where self-interest pushes people toward good behavior.
:::

## The restraint of frequent elections {orig=11}

All these protections, however, would be far from enough without the restraint of frequent elections. So, **fourth**, the House of Representatives is designed to keep its members always aware that they depend on the people. Using power might wear away the feelings they had when the people first raised them up. But before that can happen, they will be forced to look ahead to the moment when their power ends. Then their use of it will be reviewed, and they must step down to the level from which they were raised. They will stay there forever, unless they have earned another term by faithfully doing their duty.

:::commentary
House members serve two-year terms, the shortest of any federal office. Madison explains the choice of two years in [No. 52](/papers/52/) and [No. 53](/papers/53/). Today, critics point out that most House members win reelection easily, partly because of how district lines are drawn. Others answer that the short term still keeps members constantly aware of the voters, just as Madison intended.
:::

## Lawmakers must live under their own laws {orig=12-13}

I will add a **fifth** feature of the House that will keep its members from passing oppressive measures. They can make no law that will not apply fully to themselves and their friends, as well as to the great mass of society. This has always been considered one of the strongest bonds that human wisdom can create between rulers and the people. It creates a shared interest and shared feelings between them. Few governments have provided examples of this, but without it every government decays into tyranny.

Someone might ask: what will stop the House of Representatives from making laws that favor themselves and a particular class of society? I answer: the character of the whole system; the nature of just and constitutional laws; and above all, the watchful and courageous spirit that drives the people of America. It is a spirit that feeds freedom and in turn is fed by it.

If this spirit ever sinks so low that it accepts a law that binds the people but not the lawmakers, the people will be ready to accept anything but liberty.

:::quote
If this spirit shall ever be so far debased as to tolerate a law not obligatory on the legislature, as well as on the people, the people will be prepared to tolerate any thing but liberty.

Meaning: If Americans ever let lawmakers exempt themselves from the laws everyone else must follow, they will have already lost the spirit that keeps them free.
:::

:::commentary
This principle has had a real history. For many years, Congress did exempt itself from several workplace laws that applied to private businesses, such as rules on discrimination and workplace safety. Critics used Madison's argument against that practice. In 1995, Congress passed the **Congressional Accountability Act**, which applied many of those laws to itself.

Madison also worried about lawmakers voting themselves pay raises. In 1789 he proposed an amendment saying that a pay change for Congress could not take effect until after the next election. It was not ratified at the time. Two centuries later, in 1992, it was finally ratified as the **27th Amendment**.
:::

## Duty, gratitude, interest, and ambition {orig=14}

That is how the House of Representatives will be related to its constituents. Duty, gratitude, self-interest, and ambition itself are the cords that will bind members to faithfulness and sympathy with the great mass of the people. It is possible that all of these may still fail to control the whims and wickedness of human beings. But aren't they all that government allows and that human wisdom can design? Aren't they the true and natural means by which a republican government provides for the people's liberty and happiness? Aren't they the very same means that every state government in the Union relies on to reach these important goals?

What, then, are we to make of the objection this paper has been answering? What can we say to men who claim the most burning devotion to republican government, yet boldly attack its basic principle? They pretend to champion the people's right and ability to choose their own rulers. Yet they insist the people will choose only those who will immediately and certainly betray the trust given to them.

:::quote
Duty, gratitude, interest, ambition itself, are the chords by which they will be bound to fidelity and sympathy with the great mass of the people.

Meaning: Representatives will stay loyal to the people for many reasons at once, some noble (duty and gratitude) and some selfish (interest and ambition).
:::

:::commentary
Here Madison turns the critics' argument back on them. If the people can't be trusted to pick decent representatives, then the problem isn't the Constitution. It's republican government itself. This is a strong debating move, but it doesn't fully answer the critics. Their worry wasn't that voters are foolish. It was that in very large districts, only rich and famous candidates could become well enough known to win.
:::

## Are bigger districts the real problem? {orig=15-17}

Suppose someone read this objection without having seen how the Constitution says representatives will be chosen. He would assume that some unreasonable property requirement was attached to the right to vote. Or that only people from certain families or with certain fortunes could be elected. Or at least that the Constitution departed very sharply in some way from the method used in the state constitutions. We have seen how wrong the first two assumptions would be. The last would be just as wrong.

The only difference between the two cases is this. Each representative of the United States will be elected by five or six thousand citizens, while in the individual states a representative is elected by about five or six hundred. Will anyone claim that this difference is enough to justify loving the state governments and hating the federal government? If this is what the objection depends on, it deserves to be examined.

Is it supported by **reason**? No one can say so without claiming that five or six thousand citizens are less able to choose a fit representative, or more easily bribed to choose an unfit one, than five or six hundred. Reason tells us the opposite. In so large a number, a fit representative is more likely to be found. And the choice is less likely to be steered away from him by the schemes of the ambitious or the bribes of the rich.

Is the **consequence** of this idea acceptable? Suppose we say that five or six hundred citizens are the most who can vote together for one person. Then wouldn't we have to take away the people's direct choice of their public servants whenever the government doesn't need as many officials as one for every five or six hundred citizens?

:::commentary
Madison's "five or six thousand citizens" refers to *voters*, not total population. The Constitution set a minimum of 30,000 people per representative, and in 1788 only a fraction of them could vote.

Today a House district contains about 760,000 people. That is more than 25 times the Constitution's minimum of 30,000. If Madison was right that larger districts make good choices more likely, modern districts should work well. If the Anti-Federalists were right that only the well-known and well-funded can win large districts, today's expensive campaigns may prove their point. The House reached 435 members after the 1910 census, and a 1929 law fixed it at that size. Since then, districts have kept growing along with the population.
:::

## The British example {orig=18}

Is the idea supported by **facts**? I showed in the last paper that the real representation in the British House of Commons is only a little more than one member for every thirty thousand people. In Britain there are many powerful forces, which don't exist here, that favor the claims of rank and wealth.

In addition, no one can be elected to represent a county unless he owns land worth a clear six hundred pounds sterling a year. To represent a city or borough, he must own land worth half that much each year. County voters also face a requirement. Only people who own land worth more than twenty pounds sterling a year, at today's value of money, can vote.

Despite these unfavorable conditions, and despite some very unequal laws in the British legal code, no one can say that the representatives of that nation have raised up the few on the ruins of the many.

:::vocab
House of Commons: The elected lower house of the British Parliament.
Borough: A town that sent its own members to Parliament.
Pounds sterling: British money.
:::

:::commentary
Madison is right that the British system in 1788 was far less democratic than the one proposed for America. The basic rule for county voters, dating back to 1430, let only men who owned land worth forty shillings (two pounds) a year vote. Madison adjusts that old figure to "the present rate of money." Some boroughs had only a handful of voters, and some large new towns had no members of Parliament at all.

His claim that Parliament had not sacrificed the many to the few was a matter of opinion. Many Americans had just fought a revolution partly because they believed Parliament had abused its power over them.
:::

## Our own experience with large districts {orig=19-21}

But we don't need to look to foreign experience on this subject. Our own experience is clear and decisive.

- In **New Hampshire**, the districts where the people directly elect state senators are nearly as large as the state's districts for Congress will need to be.
- The districts in **Massachusetts** are larger than needed for that purpose, and those in **New York** are larger still.
- In New York, the members of the state Assembly for the city and county of New York and for Albany are elected by nearly as many voters as will be entitled to one representative in Congress, even counting on only sixty-five representatives.

It makes no difference that in these senate districts and counties, each voter votes for several representatives at once. If the same voters at the same time can choose four or five representatives, they can't be unable to choose one.

**Pennsylvania** is another example. Some of its counties, which elect its state representatives, are almost as large as its districts for federal representatives will be. The city of Philadelphia is thought to contain between fifty and sixty thousand people. So it will form nearly two districts for choosing federal representatives. Yet it forms only one county, in which every voter votes for each of its representatives in the state legislature. And, even more to the point, the whole city elects a SINGLE MEMBER of the state's executive council. The same is true in all the other counties of the state.

Aren't these facts the most convincing proof that the argument against this branch of the federal government is false? Have the senators of New Hampshire, Massachusetts, and New York, or Pennsylvania's executive council, or the Assembly members in New York and Pennsylvania, shown any special tendency to sacrifice the many to the few? Are they in any way less worthy of their places than the representatives and officials chosen in other states by very small groups of people?

But there are even stronger cases than any I have mentioned. In Connecticut, every member of one branch of the legislature is elected by the whole state. So is the governor of Connecticut, the governor of Massachusetts, the governor of this state, New York, and the president of New Hampshire. I leave it to each person to decide whether any of these experiments supports the suspicion that choosing representatives from large areas tends to raise traitors to power and undermine public liberty.

:::commentary
Madison ends with a good debating point. New Yorkers already elected their governor, George Clinton, by a statewide vote. Clinton was a leading opponent of the Constitution. If a statewide vote didn't produce a tyrant in New York, why would a district of a few thousand voters produce one in Congress?

Under New Hampshire's constitution at the time, the state's chief executive was called its "president" rather than its governor. Pennsylvania's "executive council" was a group of officials, one from each county and one from Philadelphia, that ran the state in place of a single governor.
:::

:::today
The question in this paper is still alive: does Congress represent ordinary Americans, or mostly the wealthy and well-connected? Today a large share of members of Congress are millionaires, and running for office often requires raising huge sums of money. Some argue this proves the Anti-Federalists' fear that large districts favor the elite. Others point out that voters still choose freely, that candidates from modest backgrounds still win, and that Congress has become far more diverse than it was in Madison's day. Madison's principle that lawmakers must live under the same laws as everyone else remains widely accepted across the political spectrum.
:::

:::questions
- Madison says representatives are tied to the people by "duty, gratitude, interest, ambition itself." Which of these do you think is strongest today? Which is weakest?
- Should members of Congress have to look like the people they represent (in income, job, or background), or is it enough that the people freely choose them?
- Madison argues larger districts make it harder to bribe voters. Do they also make it harder for ordinary people to run for office?
- Can you think of a modern example where lawmakers exempted themselves from rules that apply to everyone else? How did people react?
:::
