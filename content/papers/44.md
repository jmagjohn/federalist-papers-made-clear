:::summary
Madison finishes his review of the Constitution's powers. First he explains the limits it places on the states. They can't make treaties, coin money, print paper money, pass laws that punish people without a trial or after the fact, or pass laws that break contracts. They also can't tax imports and exports or keep warships without Congress's consent. Then he defends the provisions that make all the other powers work. The **necessary and proper clause** was the best of five possible ways to handle the means of carrying out the government's powers. The **supremacy clause** keeps the parts from ruling the whole. And an **oath** binds state officials to support the Constitution. His conclusion: no power in the Constitution is unnecessary, so the real question is whether the Union will be preserved.
:::

:::keypoints
- The states may not make **treaties**, grant **letters of marque**, **coin money**, issue **paper money**, or grant **titles of nobility**.
- **Bills of attainder**, **ex post facto laws**, and laws **breaking contracts** violate basic justice. Experience shows the states need firm limits against them.
- The states may not tax **imports and exports**, keep **troops or warships** in peacetime, or make **agreements** with other states or foreign nations without Congress's consent.
- Without the *substance* of the **necessary and proper clause**, the Constitution would be a "dead letter." Every other way of writing it would have been worse.
- If Congress abuses the clause, the **executive and the courts** can refuse to carry out its acts, and the **people** can elect new representatives. The **state governments** will sound the alarm.
- Without the **supremacy clause**, national laws could mean one thing in one state and nothing in another. It would be "a monster, in which the head was under the direction of the members."
- State officials must swear to **support the Constitution**, because they play a key part in running national elections.
- Since every power is needed, the real question is **whether the Union itself will be preserved**.
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of Madison's four papers (Nos. 41–44) on whether each power in the Constitution is necessary. In [No. 41](/papers/41/) he divided the powers into six classes. This paper covers the last two: limits on the states, and provisions that make the national powers effective.

Madison had a personal stake in these topics. He believed the worst problem of the 1780s was state legislatures passing unjust laws, such as paper-money and debt-relief laws. At the Convention, he had wanted Congress to have a veto over all state laws. The Convention said no. Instead, it listed specific things states could not do and made the Constitution supreme, binding state judges to follow it. Hamilton had already defended the necessary and proper and supremacy clauses in [No. 33](/papers/33/).
:::

## Things the states may not do {orig=1-3}

A *fifth* class of provisions in favor of the national authority consists of the following limits on the states:

> "No State shall enter into any treaty, alliance, or confederation; grant letters of marque and reprisal; coin money; emit bills of credit; make any thing but gold and silver a legal tender in payment of debts; pass any bill of attainder, ex post facto law, or law impairing the obligation of contracts; or grant any title of nobility."

In plain terms, no state may:

- make a treaty, alliance, or confederation;
- license private ships to attack enemy shipping;
- coin money or issue paper money;
- force people to accept anything but gold and silver as payment of debts;
- pass a law punishing someone without a trial, punishing an act after the fact, or weakening the duties of contracts;
- grant titles of nobility.

The ban on treaties, alliances, and confederations is already part of the present Articles of Union. For reasons that need no explanation, it is copied into the new Constitution.

The ban on letters of marque is also part of the old system, but it is somewhat broader in the new one. Under the old system, states could grant letters of marque after war was declared. Under the new one, these licenses must come from the government of the United States, during war as well as before it is declared. This change is fully justified. It is an advantage to have the same rules in everything that concerns foreign powers. And the nation should be directly responsible for everyone whose actions the nation itself will be held responsible for.

:::vocab
Letters of marque: Government licenses that allowed private ship owners, called privateers, to attack and capture enemy ships in wartime.
Bills of credit: Paper money issued by a government, promising to pay its value later.
Legal tender: Money that the law says must be accepted to pay a debt.
Bill of attainder: A law that declares a person or group guilty and punishes them without a trial.
Ex post facto law: A law that makes an act a crime after it was committed, or increases the punishment for it.
Titles of nobility: Inherited ranks like duke, earl, or lord, which gave special status by birth.
:::

## No state coins or paper money {orig=4-5}

The Confederation left the states the right to coin money, sharing it with Congress. Only Congress had the right to set the metal content and value of coins. Here too the new rule is better than the old one.

As long as the value and metal content were controlled by the national government, a state's right to coin money could only lead to more expensive mints and more different shapes and weights of coins in use. The second problem defeats one of the reasons the power was given to the national government in the first place. As for the first, local mints might save the trouble of sending gold and silver to a central mint to be recoined. But that goal can be met just as well by local mints set up under national authority.

Extending the ban to paper money should please every citizen, in proportion to his love of justice and his knowledge of what truly makes a country prosper. Since the peace, paper money has done America enormous harm. It has damaged the trust people need to have in each other, and the trust they need to have in their government. It has hurt the hard work and the morals of the people, and the reputation of republican government. The states that adopted this unwise measure owe a huge debt for this harm, one that will long remain unpaid. Or rather, they have built up a load of guilt. They can only make up for it by freely sacrificing, on the altar of justice, the power that caused it.

Beyond these persuasive points, there is another. The same reasons that show states must not regulate coins prove just as strongly that they must not replace coins with paper. If every state could set the value of its coins, there might be as many different currencies as there are states. That would block trade among them. States might change the value of their money after the fact, hurting citizens of other states and stirring up hatred among the states themselves. Citizens of foreign countries might be hurt in the same way. Then the whole Union would be disgraced and dragged into conflict by the foolishness of a single member. A state's power to issue paper money would cause every one of these harms, just as much as a power to coin gold or silver. The power to make anything but gold and silver legal tender for debts is taken from the states for the same reason as the power to issue paper money.

:::commentary
In the 1780s, several states printed paper money to help struggling farmers and debtors pay their debts and taxes. Rhode Island's paper money became the most famous example. Its legislature tried to force merchants and lenders to accept the paper at full value, even though it was worth much less than gold or silver. To Madison and many other leaders, this was a way of cheating creditors by law. To many debtors, it was a lifeline in hard times. See [No. 10](/papers/10/), where Madison lists "a rage for paper money" as a "wicked project."

Notice that the ban applies to the *states*, not to Congress. During the Civil War, Congress printed paper money called "greenbacks" and made it legal tender, and the Supreme Court eventually upheld this. Today, all U.S. paper money is issued under national authority, and no state prints its own.
:::

## Laws that punish without trial or break contracts {orig=6}

Bills of attainder, ex post facto laws, and laws that weaken the obligation of contracts go against the basic principles of the social compact and against every principle of sound lawmaking. The first two are expressly banned by the declarations of rights at the start of some state constitutions. All three are banned by the spirit and purpose of these founding documents. Yet our own experience has taught us that we shouldn't leave out extra fences against these dangers. So the Convention very properly added this constitutional wall to protect personal security and private rights. And unless I am badly mistaken, in doing so it followed the true feelings of the people as faithfully as their clear interests.

The sober people of America are tired of the shifting policies that have guided their governments. They have watched with regret and anger as sudden changes, and lawmakers' meddling in cases that affect personal rights, have become money-making schemes for bold and well-connected speculators, and traps for the hardworking and less-informed part of the community. They have also seen that one act of meddling by the legislature is only the first link in a long chain. Each new act of meddling naturally grows out of the effects of the one before. So they rightly conclude that a thorough reform is needed. It should end betting on public measures, encourage general prudence and hard work, and give a steady course to the business of society.

The ban on titles of nobility is copied from the Articles of Confederation and needs no comment.

:::vocab
Social compact: The idea that people agree to form a society and a government to protect their rights. It is also called the social contract.
Speculator: Someone who buys something, such as land or government debt, hoping to sell it later for a big profit.
Contract clause: The part of Article 1, section 10 that bars states from passing laws "impairing the obligation of contracts."
:::

:::commentary
The **contract clause** was aimed at state laws that helped debtors by changing the terms of loans after the fact, for example by postponing payments or letting debts be paid in nearly worthless paper. Madison saw these laws as unjust and unstable. Every time a legislature changed the rules, insiders who knew what was coming could profit, while ordinary people were caught off guard.

The clause became one of the most important limits on the states in the early republic. In *Dartmouth College v. Woodward* (1819), the Supreme Court ruled that New Hampshire couldn't rewrite a private college's charter, because the charter was a contract. But in *Home Building and Loan Association v. Blaisdell* (1934), during the Great Depression, the Court allowed Minnesota to temporarily delay mortgage foreclosures. Since then, the clause has been much weaker. Critics of the change say it leaves contracts less secure. Supporters say states need room to respond to emergencies.
:::

## Taxes on trade and other limits {orig=7-8}

The Constitution also says:

> "No State shall, without the consent of the Congress, lay any imposts or duties on imports or exports, except what may be absolutely necessary for executing its inspection laws, and the net produce of all duties and imposts laid by any State on imports or exports, shall be for the use of the treasury of the United States; and all such laws shall be subject to the revision and control of the Congress. No State shall, without the consent of Congress, lay any duty on tonnage, keep troops or ships of war in time of peace, enter into any agreement or compact with another State, or with a foreign power, or engage in war unless actually invaded, or in such imminent danger as will not admit of delay."

In plain terms, without Congress's consent, no state may:

- tax imports or exports, except what it truly needs to pay for inspecting goods. Even then, the money goes to the national treasury, and Congress can revise such laws;
- tax ships based on their size;
- keep troops or warships in peacetime;
- make an agreement with another state or a foreign power;
- go to war, unless it is actually invaded or in such immediate danger that it can't wait.

The limit on the states' power over imports and exports is supported by all the arguments that prove trade must be regulated by the national government. So I need only add one point. The way the limit is qualified seems well designed to do two things at once. It gives the states reasonable freedom to make their imports and exports convenient. And it gives the United States a reasonable check against abuse of that freedom. The rest of this clause rests on reasons that are either obvious or have already been fully explained, so we can pass over them.

:::vocab
Imposts: Taxes on imported goods.
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
Tonnage: A tax on ships based on their size or how much cargo they can carry.
Compact: A formal agreement between two or more parties.
:::

:::commentary
Interstate agreements, called **compacts**, are still common, but they need Congress's approval. One well-known example is the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey, created by a compact between the two states in 1921. It runs bridges, tunnels, airports, and seaports that both states share.
:::

## The necessary and proper clause {orig=9-12}

The *sixth* and last class consists of the powers and provisions that make all the rest effective.

The first of these is the "power to make all laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the foregoing powers, and all other powers vested by this Constitution in the government of the United States, or in any department or officer thereof."

Few parts of the Constitution have been attacked more wildly than this one. Yet if you examine it fairly, no part is more completely safe from criticism. Without the *substance* of this power, the whole Constitution would be a dead letter. So those who object to it can only mean that the *form* of the provision is wrong. But have they thought about whether a better form could have been used?

There are four other methods the Constitution could have used on this subject:

1. It could have copied the second article of the present Confederation, which bans using any power not *expressly* given.
2. It could have tried to list, one by one, all the powers included in the general words "necessary and proper."
3. It could have tried to list the powers *not* included, the exceptions to the general rule.
4. It could have said nothing at all, leaving these necessary and proper powers to be worked out by interpretation and inference.

:::vocab
Necessary and proper clause: The last clause of Article 1, section 8. It lets Congress make the laws needed to carry out its other powers. It is sometimes called the "elastic clause."
Dead letter: A law that still exists on paper but has no real effect.
:::

## Why every other method would fail {orig=13-16}

Suppose the Convention had taken the first method and copied the Confederation's second article. Clearly, the new Congress would face the same choice its predecessors have faced again and again. It could read the word *expressly* so strictly that the government would be stripped of all real authority. Or it could read it so loosely that the limit would mean nothing at all. It would be easy to show, if needed, that Congress has not carried out, and cannot carry out, any important power under the Articles without relying to some degree on *interpretation* or *implication*. The powers in the new system are broader. So the new government would be even more trapped by the choice between betraying the public interest by doing nothing, and violating the Constitution by using powers that are absolutely necessary and proper but not *expressly* granted.

Suppose the Convention had tried the second method, a full list of the powers necessary and proper for carrying out its other powers. That would have required a complete code of laws on every subject the Constitution touches. And the list would have had to fit not only present conditions, but every possible change the future might bring. Every time a general power is applied in a new way, the *particular powers* that serve as means to its *goal* must change along with the goal. They often need to change even when the goal stays the same.

Suppose the Convention had tried the third method, a list of the particular powers or means that are *not* necessary or proper. That task would have been just as impossible. And it would have had a further problem: every gap in the list would have counted as a grant of power. To avoid this, they might have listed only some of the exceptions, and described the rest with the general words "not necessary or proper." But then the list would cover only a few of the forbidden powers. And these would be the ones least likely to be tried or tolerated anyway, since the list would naturally pick the powers that were least necessary or proper. Meanwhile, the unnecessary and improper powers left in the general category would be forbidden less forcefully than if no list had been made at all.

Suppose the Constitution had said nothing on this subject. There is no doubt that all the particular powers needed as means of carrying out the general powers would have come to the government anyway, by unavoidable implication. No rule is more clearly established in law or in reason than this: wherever the goal is required, the means are allowed. Wherever a general power to do something is given, every particular power needed to do it is included. So if the Convention had used this last method, every objection now made against its plan would still seem just as convincing. And there would be a real disadvantage. The Convention would have left in place an excuse that people could seize on in a crisis to challenge the Union's essential powers.

:::quote
No axiom is more clearly established in law, or in reason, than that wherever the end is required, the means are authorized; wherever a general power to do a thing is given, every particular power necessary for doing it is included.

Meaning: If the Constitution gives the government a job, it also gives the government the tools it needs to do that job, even if the tools aren't listed.
:::

:::vocab
Axiom: A basic truth that everyone accepts without needing proof.
Expressly: Clearly and directly stated in words, rather than implied.
Implied powers: Powers the government has because they are needed to carry out the powers the Constitution lists, even though they aren't named.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's reasoning here is the same as Hamilton's in [No. 33](/papers/33/): a power to do something includes the means to do it, so the clause adds no new power. But Madison adds something new. He walks through every alternative and shows why each would be worse. His point about the word "expressly" is especially important. The Articles limited Congress to powers "expressly delegated," and Madison says that rule had left Congress either helpless or forced to break it.

The same debate came up in 1789, when Congress drafted the Bill of Rights. A proposal to add "expressly" to what became the **10th Amendment** was voted down. The amendment reserves to the states and the people "the powers not delegated to the United States," without saying "expressly."
:::

## What if Congress abuses this power? {orig=17}

Someone may ask what will happen if Congress misreads this part of the Constitution and uses powers its true meaning doesn't allow. My answer is: the same thing that would happen if Congress misread or stretched any other power given to it. The same as if the general power had been broken down into a list, and Congress violated one of the items. The same, in short, as if the state legislatures violated their own constitutional limits.

In the first place, whether the power grab succeeds will depend on the executive and the courts, which are to interpret and carry out the laws Congress passes. In the last resort, the remedy must come from the people. They can elect more faithful representatives and undo the acts of those who grabbed power.

In fact, this final remedy can be trusted more against unconstitutional acts of the national legislature than of the state legislatures, for a simple reason. Every such act of the national legislature will invade the rights of the states. So the states will always be ready to spot the change, sound the alarm to the people, and use their local influence to elect new national representatives. There is no such body standing between the state legislatures and the people, with an interest in watching what the legislatures do. So violations of the state constitutions are more likely to go unnoticed and uncorrected.

:::commentary
Here Madison names three checks on Congress: the president and the courts, who can refuse to give effect to unconstitutional laws; the voters; and the states, which will warn the people when Congress oversteps. The idea that courts can strike down unconstitutional laws, called **judicial review**, was confirmed by the Supreme Court in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803). The "Madison" in that case was James Madison himself, then secretary of state.

Madison's idea that the states would "sound the alarm" proved true sooner than he expected, and in a way he helped lead. In 1798, Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts, which made it a crime to publish "false, scandalous and malicious" writing against the government. Madison and Thomas Jefferson secretly wrote the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, protesting that the acts were unconstitutional. Voters turned against the party behind the laws in the election of 1800.
:::

## The supremacy clause {orig=18-23}

The second provision that makes the national powers effective is this:

> "This Constitution and the laws of the United States which shall be made in pursuance thereof, and all treaties made, or which shall be made, under the authority of the United States, shall be the supreme law of the land, and the judges in every State shall be bound thereby, any thing in the constitution or laws of any State to the contrary notwithstanding."

The reckless eagerness of the Constitution's opponents has led them to attack this part too. Yet without it, the Constitution would clearly be defective at its root. To see this fully, just imagine for a moment that the state constitutions had been left completely supreme, by a clause protecting them.

First, the state constitutions give their legislatures absolute sovereignty in every case not excepted by the present Articles of Confederation. So every power in the proposed Constitution that goes beyond those listed in the Confederation would have been canceled. The new Congress would have been left as powerless as the old one.

Second, the constitutions of some states don't even clearly and fully recognize the existing powers of the Confederacy. In those states, an express clause keeping the state constitutions supreme would have called into question every power in the proposed Constitution.

Third, the state constitutions are very different from each other. So a treaty or national law, equally important to all the states, might conflict with some state constitutions and not others. It would then be valid in some states and have no effect in others.

In short, the world would have seen, for the first time, a system of government built on turning the basic principles of all government upside down. It would have seen the authority of the whole society placed everywhere beneath the authority of its parts. It would have seen a monster whose head was directed by its limbs.

:::quote
...it would have seen a monster, in which the head was under the direction of the members.

Meaning: If state constitutions were supreme over the national Constitution, the parts would control the whole, like a body whose arms and legs give orders to its head.
:::

:::vocab
Supremacy clause: Article 6, clause 2 of the Constitution. It makes the Constitution, national laws made under it, and treaties the "supreme law of the land," above state laws.
Sovereignty: Supreme, independent political authority.
:::

:::commentary
Notice the words "the judges in every State shall be bound thereby." This was the Convention's substitute for the veto over state laws that Madison had wanted. Instead of Congress striking down state laws, judges, including *state* judges, would refuse to apply state laws that conflicted with the Constitution, national laws, or treaties.

The clause's most famous test came in *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819). Congress had created the Second Bank of the United States, and Maryland tried to tax its branch in Baltimore. The Supreme Court, led by Chief Justice John Marshall, ruled on both clauses in this paper. First, Congress had the power to create a bank under the necessary and proper clause. "Necessary," Marshall wrote, did not mean "absolutely necessary," but useful and appropriate to a legitimate goal. Second, under the supremacy clause, a state could not tax or interfere with a lawful national institution. Marshall's reasoning closely followed the arguments of Hamilton in [No. 33](/papers/33/) and Madison here.

There is an irony here. In 1791, Madison, then in Congress, had argued that the *first* national bank was unconstitutional because it was not truly necessary. But as president, he signed the law creating the Second Bank in 1816, the very bank at issue in *McCulloch*. Ever since, Americans have argued over how broadly to read these clauses. Supporters of a broad reading say it lets the government meet new needs. Critics say it makes the limits on national power too easy to escape.
:::

## The oath to support the Constitution {orig=24-26}

The third provision is this:

> "The Senators and Representatives, and the members of the several State legislatures, and all executive and judicial officers, both of the United States and the several States, shall be bound by oath or affirmation to support this Constitution."

Some have asked why state officials must swear to support the federal Constitution, while officers of the United States need not swear a similar oath to support the state constitutions.

I could give several reasons for the difference. I will settle for one that is obvious and conclusive. Members of the national government will play no part in putting the state constitutions into effect. But members and officers of the state governments will play an essential part in putting the federal Constitution into effect. The election of the president and the Senate will always depend on the state legislatures. The election of the House of Representatives will also depend on the same state authority at first. And House elections will probably always be run by state officers, under state laws.

:::vocab
Affirmation: A formal, serious promise that is not sworn to God. The Constitution allows it for people, such as Quakers, whose religion forbids swearing oaths.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's point still holds. States still run national elections: they print ballots, register voters, staff polling places, and count votes. Some things have changed. Since the 17th Amendment (1913), voters, not state legislatures, elect senators. And states now choose presidential electors by popular vote. But the oath still binds every state legislator, governor, and judge to support the U.S. Constitution. The same clause of Article 6 also says that no religious test may ever be required for any national office.
:::

## The conclusion: preserve the Union {orig=27-28}

Among the provisions that make the national powers effective, we could also include those that belong to the executive and the courts. But since these are saved for separate examination later, I pass over them here.

We have now reviewed, in detail, every article that makes up the total amount of power the proposed Constitution gives the national government. And we have reached an undeniable conclusion: no part of this power is unnecessary or improper for achieving the necessary goals of the Union. So the question of whether to grant this amount of power comes down to another question. Shall we establish a government equal to the needs of the Union? In other words, shall the Union itself be preserved?

:::commentary
Madison's ending sums up his strategy in Nos. 41–44. He doesn't claim that every power is risk-free. He claims every power is *needed*, so rejecting them means rejecting the Union. Opponents of the Constitution saw it differently. Many of them wanted a stronger Union too, but with fewer powers, more limits, or a bill of rights. In the next two papers, [No. 45](/papers/45/) and [No. 46](/papers/46/), Madison turns to the second question he raised in No. 41: whether all these powers together threaten the states.
:::

:::today
The two great clauses in this paper are behind many of today's biggest legal fights. The **necessary and proper clause** supports much of what Congress does that isn't listed by name, from creating federal agencies to passing criminal laws. People still argue about how far it reaches. The **supremacy clause** settles conflicts between state and national law, on issues like immigration, drug laws, gun laws, and environmental rules. When a state law and a national law clash, courts ask whether the national law was made "in pursuance" of the Constitution, and if it was, the national law wins. Madison's limits on the states still matter too. No state can print its own money, and no state can pass a law punishing a person without a trial.
:::
