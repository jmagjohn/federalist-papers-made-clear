:::summary
John Jay argues that one national government will keep America safer from foreign wars than thirteen separate states or several smaller confederacies. Wars usually start from "just causes," mainly broken treaties and acts of violence. A national government will attract the most capable leaders, interpret treaties the same way everywhere, and resist the local pressures that tempt a single state to break its promises or attack its neighbors. And when disputes do arise, a strong united nation can settle them more calmly, and other countries will accept its explanations more readily, than they would from a small, weak state.
:::

:::keypoints
- Intelligent, well-informed people rarely hold a mistaken belief about their own interests for long. Americans have long believed in **union**.
- **Safety** is the first thing a free people must provide for.
- The fewer **just causes of war** a nation gives, the more likely it is to stay at peace.
- Just causes of war come mainly from **broken treaties** and **direct violence**.
- A national government will draw the **best people**, interpret treaties **consistently**, and resist **local temptations** to break faith.
- Violence that provokes war usually comes from **one part** of a country, such as a border state, not the whole.
- A national government can **settle disputes** more calmly, without the pride that makes a state refuse to admit fault.
- Other nations accept apologies from a **strong** nation that they would reject from a weak one.
:::

:::context Background
This is the second of John Jay's four essays (Nos. 2–5) on the dangers America would face from foreign nations if the Union broke apart. Jay was the nation's Secretary for Foreign Affairs, so he knew these problems firsthand. In 1787, Britain still held forts on American soil, Spain had closed the Mississippi River to American boats, and states had violated the 1783 peace treaty, which gave Britain an excuse to keep its troops in place. Conflicts between settlers and Native nations on the frontier also threatened to drag the whole country into war.
:::

## The people have long believed in union {orig=1-2}

It's a familiar observation that the people of any country, if they are intelligent and well-informed like Americans, rarely adopt a wrong opinion about their interests and stick to it for many years. That naturally gives us great respect for the strong opinion Americans have held so long and so consistently: that it's important to stay firmly united under one federal government with enough power for all general and national purposes.

The more carefully I consider and examine the reasons behind this opinion, the more convinced I am that they are strong and conclusive.

:::commentary
Jay begins with a compliment to his readers: Americans are smart, and they've believed in union for a long time, so they're probably right. This is a gentle persuasive move. It also quietly casts the Anti-Federalists, who were talking about separate confederacies, as people going against the long-held wisdom of the public.
:::

## Safety comes first {orig=3-4}

Of the many things a wise and free people must pay attention to, providing for their SAFETY seems to come first. The people's SAFETY involves many different circumstances and considerations, so it gives a lot of room to those who want to define it precisely and fully.

For now, I'll only consider safety as it relates to keeping peace and calm, both against dangers from FOREIGN WEAPONS AND INFLUENCE, and against dangers of the SAME KIND that come from causes at home. Since foreign danger comes first in order, it should be discussed first. So let's examine whether the people are right to believe that a friendly Union, under an effective national government, gives them the best possible protection against HOSTILITIES from abroad.

## Fewer causes of war means more peace {orig=5}

The number of wars that have happened or will happen in the world always matches the number and seriousness of the causes, REAL or PRETENDED, that PROVOKE or INVITE them. If that's true, it's useful to ask whether UNITED America is likely to give as many JUST causes of war as DIVIDED America. If united America will probably give the fewest, then in this respect, the Union does the most to keep the people at peace with other nations.

:::vocab
Just cause of war: A legitimate reason for one nation to go to war with another, such as a broken treaty or an attack, according to the international rules of the time.
Law of nations: The rules that civilized countries were expected to follow in dealing with each other. Today we call this international law.
:::

## Broken treaties: why one government keeps them better {orig=6-12}

JUST causes of war mostly come from either breaking treaties or direct violence. America has already made treaties with at least six foreign nations. All of them except Prussia are sea powers, so they're able to bother and harm us. America also has a lot of trade with Portugal, Spain, and Britain. And with Spain and Britain, it also has to consider that they are its neighbors.

It's very important to America's peace that it obey the law of nations toward all these powers. It seems clear to me that one national government will do this more completely and reliably than thirteen separate states, or three or four separate confederacies. Here's why.

**First, a national government will attract the best people.** Once an effective national government is set up, the best people in the country will not only agree to serve in it, but will generally be chosen to run it. Local influence from a town or county may get people elected to state legislatures, state senates, state courts, or state executive offices. But it will take a broader and wider reputation for talent and other qualities to win national office. That's especially true since the national government will have the largest pool of people to choose from, and will never suffer from the shortage of qualified people that is not unusual in some states. As a result, the national government's administration, policies, and court decisions will be wiser, more consistent, and more sensible than those of individual states. So they will satisfy other nations better, and keep us SAFER.

**Second, treaties will be interpreted the same way everywhere.** Under the national government, treaties and their terms, as well as the law of nations, will always be explained in one sense and carried out in the same way. But in thirteen states, or three or four confederacies, court decisions on the same questions won't always agree or be consistent. That's partly because there would be many independent courts and judges appointed by different governments, and partly because different local laws and interests may affect them. The Convention's wisdom in giving these questions to courts appointed by, and answerable only to, one national government can't be praised enough.

**Third, local temptations won't sway the national government.** The chance of a short-term loss or gain may often tempt the ruling party in one or two states to abandon good faith and justice. But those temptations won't reach the other states, so they'll have little or no influence on the national government. The temptation will fail, and good faith and justice will be preserved. The case of the peace treaty with Britain strongly supports this reasoning.

**Fourth, the national government can stop wrongs a state can't.** Even if a state's ruling party wanted to resist such temptations, they often come from circumstances special to that state and may affect many of its people. So the ruling party may not always be able to prevent the planned injustice or punish the wrongdoers, even if it wants to. But the national government, not being affected by those local circumstances, won't be tempted to commit the wrong itself. And it won't lack the power or the will to prevent or punish wrongs committed by others.

So to the extent that broken treaties and violations of the law of nations, whether deliberate or accidental, give JUST causes of war, they are less to be feared under one general government than under several smaller ones. In that respect, one government does the most for the people's SAFETY.

:::commentary
Jay's point about "the treaty of peace with Britain" was a sore subject. The 1783 Treaty of Paris required Americans to pay debts owed to British merchants from before the war, and to stop punishing Loyalists. But several state legislatures passed laws blocking British creditors from collecting. Britain pointed to these violations to justify keeping its forts in the Great Lakes region. As Secretary for Foreign Affairs, Jay had reported to Congress in 1786 that America had broken the treaty first. Under the Articles, Congress had no way to force the states to comply.

The Constitution fixed this in two ways. Article VI made treaties "the supreme Law of the Land," binding on state judges. And Article III gave federal courts power over cases arising under treaties.
:::

:::vocab
Maritime: Having to do with the sea. A maritime nation has a strong navy or merchant fleet.
Adjudication: A court's decision on a case.
:::

## Violence: why border states are the risk {orig=13-15}

As for just causes of war that come from direct and unlawful violence, it seems just as clear to me that one good national government offers far more protection against them than anything else could.

That's because acts of violence more often come from the passions and interests of a part than of the whole, from one or two states rather than the Union. Not a single war with Native nations has yet been caused by the aggression of the current federal government, weak as it is. But there are several cases where hostilities with Native nations were provoked by the improper conduct of individual states. Those states, either unable or unwilling to restrain or punish offenses, led to the slaughter of many innocent people.

Spanish and British territories border some states and not others, so the causes of conflict naturally fall mainly on the border states. It's the border states, if any, that will be most likely to start a war with these nations by direct violence, out of sudden irritation and a quick sense of their own interest or injury. Nothing can prevent that danger as effectively as a national government, whose wisdom and caution won't be weakened by the passions of the parties directly involved.

:::commentary
Jay's phrase in the original is "Indian wars," using the language of his time. His point was accurate: much frontier violence came from settlers pushing onto Native lands, often with the encouragement of state governments. Georgia and North Carolina, for example, made their own land deals and treaties with Native nations, sometimes conflicting with agreements made by Congress.

But a stronger national government did not bring peace to Native nations. Under the Constitution, the United States fought a series of wars against Native peoples in the Northwest Territory in the 1790s, and later carried out forced removals, such as the Trail of Tears in the 1830s. A national government could restrain the states, but it could also pursue expansion on a much larger scale.
:::

## Settling disputes calmly {orig=16-18}

The national government will not only give fewer just causes of war. It will also be better able to settle disputes peacefully. It will be calmer and cooler, and in that respect, as in others, better able to act wisely than the state that caused the offense.

The pride of states, like the pride of people, naturally makes them want to justify everything they do. It makes them resist admitting, correcting, or making up for their mistakes and offenses. The national government, in such cases, won't be affected by this pride. It will consider, with moderation and honesty, the best way to get out of the difficulties that threaten.

Besides, it's well known that apologies, explanations, and compensation are often accepted as enough when they come from a strong, united nation. The same offers would be rejected as not enough if they came from a state or confederacy with little importance or power.

In 1685, the Republic of Genoa offended King Louis XIV of France and tried to calm him down. He demanded that they send their Doge, their chief official, along with four of their senators, to FRANCE to beg his pardon and accept his terms. They had to give in for the sake of peace. Would he ever have demanded, or received, such humiliation from Spain, or Britain, or any other POWERFUL nation?

:::vocab
Genoa: A small but wealthy trading republic in northern Italy.
Doge: The elected chief official of the republics of Genoa and Venice.
Louis XIV: King of France from 1643 to 1715, the most powerful monarch in Europe in his time.
:::

:::commentary
The Genoa story makes Jay's point vividly. In 1684, France bombarded Genoa from the sea for supporting Spain. Afterward, Louis XIV forced the Doge to travel to the palace of Versailles and personally apologize. Small, weak nations get pushed around. Big, strong ones get respect. Jay wants Americans to imagine what would happen if a small American confederacy offended a European power.
:::

:::today
Jay's argument that a nation speaks more effectively with one voice in foreign affairs is still central to American law. The Constitution gives treaty-making and foreign relations to the national government, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly ruled that states can't run their own foreign policies. But states still sometimes clash with national foreign policy, for example by passing their own climate agreements or sanctions on foreign countries. And Jay's point about border states is still relevant: states along the borders with Mexico and Canada often have different concerns about immigration and trade than states far from any border.
:::
