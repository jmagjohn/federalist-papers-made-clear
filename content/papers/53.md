:::summary
Madison takes on a popular saying: "where annual elections end, tyranny begins." He argues that there is nothing magic about one year. The states already use terms from six months to two years with no clear difference in liberty. The saying made sense in Britain, where Parliament could change the length of its own terms, and once did exactly that. In America, a written Constitution fixes the term, and the government can't change it. Then he argues that two-year terms are useful. National lawmakers must learn about the laws, trade, and conditions of thirteen different states, plus foreign affairs. Two years also makes the long trip to the capital more worthwhile, helps new members stand up to experienced ones, and gives time to remove anyone who wins a seat by fraud.
:::

:::keypoints
- There is no natural link between **the length of a year** and how long people can resist the temptations of power.
- The states already differ: **six-month** terms in Connecticut and Rhode Island, **two-year** terms in South Carolina, with no clear difference in how free they are.
- The saying "where annual elections end, tyranny begins" came from Britain, where **Parliament could change the constitution**, including its own terms.
- In America, a **Constitution made by the people** fixes the term, so the government can't stretch it.
- Good lawmakers need **knowledge**, and some of it can only be learned on the job.
- National lawmakers must understand **thirteen states' laws and economies**, plus **foreign affairs**.
- Two years helps new members avoid being manipulated by **long-serving members**, and gives time to settle **disputed elections**.
:::

:::context Background
This paper continues [No. 52](/papers/52/), where Madison argued that two-year terms for the House are *safe*. Now he answers the popular slogan behind the critics' fears and then argues that two-year terms are also *useful*.

In 1788 most Americans were used to electing their state legislatures every year. Two states, Connecticut and Rhode Island, held elections every six months. The memory of the British Parliament, which had once extended its own term, made many people fear that any longer term was a step toward tyranny.
:::

## "Where annual elections end, tyranny begins" {orig=1}

Here I may be reminded of a popular saying: "where annual elections end, tyranny begins." It has often been said that sayings which become proverbs are usually based on good reasons. But it is just as true that, once established, they are often applied to cases where those reasons don't fit. I need look no further than the case in front of us for proof.

What is the reason behind this saying? No one will make himself look foolish by claiming there is some natural connection between the sun or the seasons and the length of time that human virtue can resist the temptations of power. Luckily for humanity, liberty is not tied to any single length of time. It lies between two extremes, and the space between them leaves enough room for all the differences that various societies and situations may require. Officials could be elected daily, weekly, or monthly, as well as yearly, if that were found useful. In some cases this has actually happened. And if circumstances can require a change from the rule in one direction, why not also in the other direction?

Look at the terms we have set for ourselves in electing the most numerous branches of the state legislatures. We find they don't match any more than the terms for other officials do. In Connecticut and Rhode Island, the terms are six months. In the other states, except South Carolina, they are one year. In South Carolina they are two years, as is proposed for the federal government. That is a difference of four to one between the longest and shortest terms. Yet it would not be easy to show that Connecticut or Rhode Island is better governed, or enjoys more sensible liberty, than South Carolina. Nor is it easy to show that any of these states differ, for this reason, from the states whose terms are different from all three.

:::quote
No man will subject himself to the ridicule of pretending that any natural connection subsists between the sun or the seasons, and the period within which human virtue can bear the temptations of power.

Meaning: There's nothing special about one year. The time it takes the Earth to go around the sun has nothing to do with how long a politician can be trusted.
:::


:::commentary
This is Madison at his most practical. He doesn't attack the idea that frequent elections protect liberty. He accepts it. He simply points out that the line between "frequent enough" and "too long" can't be drawn by a slogan. His evidence is the states themselves: their terms ranged from six months to two years, and none of them had become a tyranny.
:::

## Where the saying came from {orig=2}

When I search for the basis of this doctrine, I can find only one, and it doesn't apply to our case at all.

In America we understand an important distinction. A Constitution is set up by the people and can't be changed by the government. A law is set up by the government and can be changed by the government. This distinction seems to have been poorly understood, and even more poorly followed, in every other country. Wherever the supreme power to make laws has been placed, people have assumed that the full power to change the form of government was there too.

Even in Great Britain, where the principles of political and civil liberty have been discussed most, and where we hear most about the rights of the constitution, it is claimed that Parliament's authority is supreme and can't be controlled. This is said to be true for the constitution as well as for ordinary laws. So Parliament has, in several cases, actually changed some of the most basic parts of the government by passing ordinary laws. In particular, it has changed the length of its own term several times. On the last occasion, it not only replaced three-year terms with seven-year terms, but in the same law kept its own members in office four years longer than the people had elected them for.

Attention to these dangerous practices has naturally alarmed the friends of free government, for whom frequent elections are the cornerstone. It has led them to look for some protection for liberty against this danger. Where no Constitution above the government existed or could be obtained, no constitutional protection like the one established in the United States could be tried. So some other protection had to be found.

And what better protection was possible than choosing some simple and familiar length of time? It could serve as a standard for measuring the danger of changes, for focusing the nation's feelings, and for uniting the efforts of patriots. The simplest and most familiar length of time for this purpose was a year. So, out of a praiseworthy desire to build some barrier against the gradual changes of an unlimited government, people taught the doctrine that the march toward tyranny could be measured by how far a government moved away from the fixed point of yearly elections.

But why would we need this device for a government limited, as the federal government will be, by the authority of a Constitution that stands above it? Who will claim that the liberties of the American people will not be safer under two-year elections, fixed by such a Constitution so that they can't be changed, than the liberties of any other nation where elections are yearly, or even more frequent, but can be changed by the government's ordinary power?

:::quote
The important distinction so well understood in America, between a Constitution established by the people and unalterable by the government, and a law established by the government and alterable by the government, seems to have been little understood and less observed in any other country.

Meaning: In America, the Constitution comes from the people and sits above the government. Ordinary laws come from the government. Other countries rarely made this distinction.
:::

:::vocab
Septennial: Happening every seven years.
:::

:::commentary
Madison is describing the British idea of **parliamentary sovereignty**: Parliament can pass any law it likes, including laws that change the structure of the government. The English legal writer William Blackstone described Parliament's power in just these sweeping terms. Madison's example is the **Septennial Act of 1716**. Parliament had been elected in 1715 for a term of up to three years. The act stretched the limit to seven years and applied it to the sitting members, keeping them in office four extra years without a new election.

This passage explains one of America's most important political ideas. A written constitution, approved by the people and hard to amend, protects the rules of the game from the people currently playing it. Congress cannot extend its own terms. That would take a constitutional amendment, which requires the approval of three-fourths of the states.
:::

## Lawmakers need knowledge {orig=3-4}

The second question I raised is whether two-year elections are necessary or useful. Several very obvious points show that the answer is yes.

No one can be a capable lawmaker unless he has some knowledge of the subjects he will make laws about, in addition to honest intentions and good judgment. Part of this knowledge can be gained from information available to people in private life as well as in public office. Another part can only be gained, or at least fully gained, through actual experience in the job that requires it. So the length of service should, in all such cases, be in some proportion to how much practical knowledge the job needs.

As we have seen, most states set the term for the larger branch of their legislature at one year. So the question can be put simply: is two years no more fitting for the knowledge needed in federal lawmaking than one year is for the knowledge needed in state lawmaking? Just asking the question this way suggests the answer.

## A large and varied country {orig=5-6}

In a single state, the knowledge a lawmaker needs concerns the existing laws, which are the same throughout the state and which all citizens know something about. It also concerns the general affairs of the state. These cover a small area, are not very varied, and take up much of the attention and conversation of every class of people.

The great stage of the United States presents a very different scene. The laws are far from uniform; they differ in every state. The public affairs of the Union are spread across a very large region. They are greatly varied by the local affairs connected with them. And they can hardly be learned correctly anywhere except in the national government, where representatives from every part of the nation will bring knowledge of them. Yet the members from each state ought to have some knowledge of the affairs, and even the laws, of all the states. Consider the main subjects of federal lawmaking:

- How can **foreign trade** be properly regulated by uniform laws without some familiarity with the commerce, ports, customs, and regulations of the different states?
- How can **trade between the states** be properly regulated without some knowledge of how they compare in these and other ways?
- How can **taxes** be wisely set and effectively collected unless they are adjusted to the different laws and local conditions in the different states?
- How can uniform rules for the **militia** be properly made without similar knowledge of the many internal differences among the states?

These main subjects of federal lawmaking show very clearly how much information the representatives must gain. Other internal subjects will require a matching amount of knowledge.

It is true that all these difficulties will, over time, become much smaller. The hardest task will be launching the government and creating the first body of federal law. Improving on those first drafts will become easier, and less needed, every year. The government's past actions will be a ready and accurate source of information for new members. The affairs of the Union will become more and more a subject of interest and conversation among ordinary citizens. And more contact among people from different states will help spread knowledge of one another's affairs. That, in turn, will help make their customs and laws more alike.

But even with all these reductions, federal lawmaking will still be so much newer and harder than lawmaking in a single state that it justifies the longer term for those who carry it out.

:::vocab
Commerce: Trade, especially between nations or states.
Militia: Ordinary citizens who are trained to serve as soldiers when needed, rather than full-time professional soldiers.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's list matches the main powers given to Congress in Article I, Section 8: regulating foreign and interstate commerce, laying and collecting taxes, and organizing the militia. In 1788 these were genuinely new tasks. The Congress under the Articles of Confederation could not regulate trade or collect taxes at all.

His prediction that Americans would grow more alike as they traded and traveled more came partly true. Roads, canals, railroads, and eventually national newspapers, radio, and television helped create a shared national culture. But deep regional differences remained, most tragically over slavery, which split the nation in the Civil War.
:::

## Foreign affairs {orig=7}

There is a branch of knowledge a federal representative needs that I have not yet mentioned: foreign affairs. In regulating our own trade, he should know not only the treaties between the United States and other nations, but also the trade policies and laws of other nations. He should not be completely ignorant of the law of nations. To the extent that it is a proper subject for a nation's own laws, it is placed under the federal government.

The House of Representatives will not take a direct part in negotiating with foreign nations. But because all areas of public affairs are connected, these subjects will often need attention in ordinary lawmaking. Sometimes they will even require the House to pass laws or cooperate in other ways. Some of this knowledge can surely be gained by private study at home. But some of it can only come from public sources of information. And all of it will be learned best through practical attention to the subject while actually serving in the legislature.

:::vocab
Law of nations: The rules that nations were expected to follow in dealing with each other. Today we call this international law.
:::

:::commentary
The Constitution gives the Senate, not the House, a role in approving treaties. But Madison is right that the House is still involved in foreign affairs. It must vote on the money to carry out treaties, on tariffs, and on declarations of war. This became a real issue soon after the Constitution took effect. In 1796 the House fiercely debated whether to fund the Jay Treaty with Britain. Madison, then a member of the House, argued that it had the right to refuse.
:::

## Travel, veterans, and newcomers {orig=8-9}

There are other points that may matter less but still deserve notice. Many representatives will have to travel long distances and make arrangements because of it. If the term were only one year, this might be a much more serious reason for qualified men to turn down the job than if it were two years.

No argument on this subject can be drawn from the delegates to the current Congress. It is true that they are elected every year. But the state legislatures treat their reelection almost as automatic. The election of representatives by the people would not work on the same principle.

As happens in all such assemblies, a few members will have superior talents. Through frequent reelection they will become long-serving members. They will master the public business, and perhaps they won't mind using those advantages. The greater the share of new members, and the less informed most of the members are, the more likely they will be to fall into traps set for them. This point applies just as much to the relationship between the House of Representatives and the Senate.

:::commentary
Travel in 1789 was slow and hard. The first Congress met in New York City, and members from Georgia or New Hampshire faced long trips by horse, coach, or ship. Some members arrived weeks late.

Madison's point about experienced members is a clever one. Many people assume that frequent turnover is always good. Madison warns that a House full of beginners would be easy for a few skilled insiders, or for a more experienced Senate, to manipulate. This is the same reason some people today oppose strict term limits for Congress. Supporters of term limits answer that long-serving members become too close to lobbyists and too far from voters.
:::

## Disputed elections {orig=10-11}

Our frequent elections have an inconvenience mixed in with their advantages, even in single states, when the states are large and the legislature meets only once a year. Fraudulent elections can't be investigated and overturned in time for the decision to matter. If a candidate can get himself declared the winner, by whatever unlawful means, he takes his seat as a matter of course, and he is sure to keep it long enough to serve his purposes. This gives a very harmful encouragement to using unlawful means to win false election results.

If elections for Congress were yearly, this practice might become a very serious abuse, especially in the more distant states. Each house is, as it must be, the judge of the elections, qualifications, and returns of its own members. Experience may suggest ways to simplify and speed up the process in disputed cases. But so much of a year would still pass before an illegitimate member could be removed from his seat that the chance of removal would do little to stop unfair and unlawful ways of winning one.

All these points together justify the claim that two-year elections will be as useful to public affairs as we have seen they will be safe for the liberty of the people.

:::vocab
Returns: The official count of votes in an election.
:::

:::commentary
Article I, Section 5 makes each house of Congress "the Judge of the Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members." The House still decides contested elections today, though such cases are rare.

Madison's worry about timing was real. In his day, the counting, reporting, and investigating of votes could take months. A dishonest winner with a one-year term might serve most of it before anyone could remove him.
:::

:::today
The debate over term length is still with us, but it has flipped in an interesting way. In 1788, critics thought two years was too *long*. Today, many people think it is too *short*. They say House members must start raising money for the next campaign almost as soon as they take office. Others defend the two-year term as the best way to keep the House close to the people. Madison's central idea, that a written constitution should fix the terms of office so that lawmakers can't extend their own power, is now so widely accepted that it is easy to forget how new it was.
:::
