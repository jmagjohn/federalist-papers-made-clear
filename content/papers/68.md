:::summary
Hamilton defends the Electoral College, the system for choosing the president. He notes it was almost the only part of the Constitution its opponents didn't attack. The people in each state choose **electors**, who meet in their own states and vote for president. Hamilton says this design reflects the will of the people, puts the final choice in the hands of informed citizens, avoids riots and disorder, makes bribery and foreign meddling hard, and keeps the president independent of Congress. He predicts it will almost guarantee presidents of real ability and good character. He also explains why the Constitution creates a vice president.
:::

:::keypoints
- The method of choosing the president was **barely criticized**, even by the Constitution's opponents.
- The **"sense of the people"** should guide the choice, so the people choose the electors.
- A **small group of electors** chosen for this one purpose can judge candidates more carefully than the public at large.
- Electors meet **separately in each state**, which makes it harder to stir up mobs or organize plots.
- The biggest danger is **foreign powers** trying to put "a creature of their own" in the presidency. Electors are temporary and scattered, so they're hard to bribe.
- **No member of Congress or federal official** can be an elector.
- The president depends **only on the people** for re-election, not on Congress.
- Hamilton predicts the office will rarely go to anyone without real merit. "Talents for low intrigue" might win a state, but not the whole nation.
- The **vice president** presides over the Senate and can replace the president if needed.
:::

:::context Background
How to choose the president was one of the hardest questions at the Constitutional Convention. The delegates voted on it many times. Some wanted Congress to choose the president, but that would make the president dependent on Congress. Others wanted a direct popular vote, but many delegates worried that voters in a large country wouldn't know candidates from distant states. Southern delegates also knew that a popular vote would give them less power, since enslaved people couldn't vote, while the Electoral College would count them under the three-fifths rule. The Electoral College was a compromise worked out late in the Convention.
:::

## Excellent, if not perfect {orig=1}

The method of choosing the president is almost the only important part of the system that has escaped harsh criticism, or that has received even the slightest approval from its opponents. The most persuasive of them to appear in print has even admitted that the election of the president is pretty well protected.

I'll go a step further. I don't hesitate to say that if the method isn't perfect, it is at least excellent. It combines, to a remarkable degree, all the advantages we could hope for.

:::commentary
The opponent Hamilton mentions is the "Federal Farmer," an anonymous Anti-Federalist writer whose letters were widely read. It's true that the Electoral College got relatively little criticism in 1787–88. Today, it is one of the most debated parts of the Constitution.
:::

## The people's will, filtered through electors {orig=2-3}

It was desirable that the will of the people should guide the choice of the person given such an important trust. This goal is met by giving the right to choose, not to any body that already exists, but to people chosen by the voters for this special purpose, at this particular time.

It was equally desirable that the actual election be made by the people most capable of judging the qualities needed for the office. They should also act in conditions that favor careful thought and a wise weighing of all the reasons that should guide their choice. A small number of people, chosen by their fellow citizens from the general public, will be most likely to have the information and judgment needed for such a complicated decision.

:::vocab
Elector: A person chosen to cast a vote for president. Each state gets as many electors as it has members of the House plus its two senators.
Electoral College: The name later given to the whole group of electors. The Constitution doesn't use the phrase.
Chief Magistrate: An old term for the president, the highest executive official.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton imagined electors as independent, thoughtful citizens who would weigh the candidates and use their own judgment. That's not how it turned out. Within a few elections, political parties began running slates of electors who pledged in advance to vote for their party's candidate. Today, electors are almost always loyal party members who vote as expected, and many states have laws requiring them to. In *Chiafalo v. Washington* (2020), the Supreme Court ruled that states can require electors to vote for the candidate who won the state's popular vote.

So the "deliberation" Hamilton praised has mostly disappeared. The Electoral College now works as a way of counting votes state by state.
:::

## Avoiding riots and disorder {orig=4}

It was also especially desirable to give as little chance as possible to riots and disorder. This danger was serious in choosing an official with such an important role in the government as the President of the United States. But the precautions so wisely built into this system promise effective protection.

Choosing SEVERAL people to form a middle group of electors will be much less likely to throw the community into extraordinary or violent upheavals than choosing ONE person who is himself the final object of the public's wishes. And since the electors chosen in each state are to meet and vote in their own state, their separate and scattered situation will expose them much less to heated passions, which could spread from them to the people, than if they all met at one time in one place.

:::commentary
Hamilton's worry was that a single national contest for one powerful office could become explosive. Splitting the election into many smaller contests, held in separate states, would cool things down. Today, presidential elections are intensely national, with campaigns, media, and money focused on a single race. Some people argue the state-by-state system now *concentrates* attention on a few close "swing states" instead of calming passions.
:::

## Guarding against corruption and foreign influence {orig=5}

Nothing was more desirable than placing every possible obstacle in the way of plots, scheming, and corruption. These deadliest enemies of republican government could be expected to come from more than one direction. But they would come mainly from the desire of foreign powers to gain improper influence in our government. How better to achieve that than by raising someone they control to the presidency?

But the Convention has guarded against this danger with very wise and careful attention. They didn't make the president's appointment depend on any group that already exists, whose members could be secretly worked on in advance and persuaded to sell their votes. Instead, they gave it first to a direct act of the American people, who choose people for the temporary and single purpose of making the appointment.

They also made ineligible all those who, because of their position, might be suspected of being too devoted to the president in office. No senator, representative, or other person holding an office of trust or profit under the United States can be an elector.

So without corrupting the people themselves, the people who actually cast the votes will at least begin their task free from any bad bias. Because they exist only briefly and are scattered, as already mentioned, we can reasonably expect them to stay that way until the end. Corrupting so many people takes time as well as money. And it wouldn't be easy to suddenly draw them, spread across thirteen states, into any scheme based on motives that, even if not exactly corrupt, might lead them away from their duty.

:::quote
How could they better gratify this, than by raising a creature of their own to the chief magistracy of the Union?

Meaning: The best way for a foreign country to control America would be to put its own puppet in the White House.
:::

:::vocab
Cabal: A small group secretly plotting to gain power.
Intrigue: Secret scheming.
Ascendant: Controlling influence or power.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's fear of foreign influence was real. In the 1700s, European powers often bribed officials and meddled in the elections of other countries. Poland was a famous example. Its kings were elected by nobles, and foreign powers like Russia regularly bribed their way to choosing the winner.

The Constitution has several protections against foreign influence. The president must be a "natural born citizen." Federal officials can't accept gifts or titles from foreign governments without Congress's consent (the "emoluments clause"). And no federal official can be an elector. Concerns about foreign interference in American elections have come back strongly in recent years, especially after investigations into Russian interference in the 2016 election.
:::

## Keeping the president independent {orig=6-7}

Another important goal was that the president should depend on no one but the people themselves to stay in office. Otherwise he might be tempted to neglect his duty to please those whose support he needed to keep his position. This advantage is also secured by making his re-election depend on a special group of representatives, chosen by the people for the single purpose of making this important choice.

All these advantages combine nicely in the plan the Convention designed. Here is how it works:

- The people of each state choose a number of electors equal to the state's number of senators and representatives in Congress.
- The electors meet in their own state and vote for a qualified person as president.
- Their votes are sent to the national capital. The person with a majority of all the electoral votes becomes president.
- A majority might not always go to one person, and it might be unsafe to let less than a majority decide. So in that case, the House of Representatives chooses from among the five candidates with the most votes, picking the one it thinks best qualified.

:::commentary
Hamilton's description is a little loose. The Constitution doesn't say "the people" choose electors. It says each state chooses them "in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct." In the first elections, many state legislatures chose the electors themselves. By the 1830s, almost every state let the people vote for electors, and today all do.

Also notice the House's role. If no one wins a majority of electoral votes, the House picks the president, with each state delegation casting one vote. This happened in 1800 (when Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr tied, and Hamilton helped tip the House to Jefferson) and in 1824 (when the House chose John Quincy Adams over Andrew Jackson). The 12th Amendment, ratified in 1804, changed the House's choice to the top *three* candidates.
:::

## A guarantee of good presidents? {orig=8-9}

This process of election gives a near certainty that the presidency will never go to anyone who doesn't have the needed qualifications to a high degree. Talents for low scheming and the little tricks that win popularity might be enough to raise a man to the highest honors in a single state. But it will take other talents, and a different kind of merit, to earn the respect and trust of the whole Union, or of enough of it to win the distinguished office of President of the United States.

It won't be going too far to say that we can always expect to see the office filled by people outstanding in ability and good character. Anyone who understands how much the executive in every government affects whether it is run well or badly will see this as a strong point in the Constitution's favor.

A poet once wrote: "For forms of government let fools contest — That which is best administered is best." We can't accept this political error, which suggests the form of government doesn't matter. But we can safely say that the true test of a good government is its ability and tendency to produce good administration.

:::quote
Talents for low intrigue, and the little arts of popularity, may alone suffice to elevate a man to the first honors in a single State; but it will require other talents, and a different kind of merit, to establish him in the esteem and confidence of the whole Union...

Meaning: A smooth-talking schemer might win a state election, but to win the presidency, a candidate would need real ability and a strong reputation across the whole country.
:::

:::vocab
Moral certainty: Something so likely that it's reasonable to treat it as certain, even if it can't be proven.
Heresy: A belief that goes against accepted teaching. Hamilton calls the poet's idea a "political heresy."
:::

:::commentary
This is Hamilton's boldest prediction, and it's worth thinking about whether it came true. He believed the Electoral College would act as a filter, making it nearly impossible for an unqualified person to become president. Americans have long disagreed about whether every president since then has met his standard of "ability and virtue." What is clear is that the filter he described, independent electors using their own judgment, no longer exists. So whether the system produces good presidents now depends on the voters, the parties, and the campaigns.

The poet Hamilton quotes is Alexander Pope, from *An Essay on Man* (1734). Pope's line suggests that it doesn't matter what kind of government you have, as long as it is run well. Hamilton disagrees, since the whole point of the Federalist Papers is that the *form* of government matters. But he agrees that good administration is the real test.
:::

## The vice president {orig=10-11}

The vice president is to be chosen the same way as the president. The only difference is that if there is a tie for vice president, the Senate decides, instead of the House.

Some have objected that creating a special office of vice president is unnecessary, or even harmful. They argue it would have been better to let the Senate choose one of its own members to serve in that role. But two points support the Convention's idea.

First, to make sure the Senate can always reach a final decision, its presiding officer should have only a tie-breaking vote. If a senator from some state were taken out of his regular seat to preside over the Senate, his state would trade a vote it always has for a vote it would only have in a tie.

Second, since the vice president may sometimes replace the president as the head of the executive branch, all the reasons for choosing the president this way apply with great, if not equal, force to choosing the vice president.

Notably, here as in most other cases, the objection would apply just as well to New York's own constitution. We have a lieutenant governor, elected by all the people, who presides over the state Senate and is the official substitute for the governor in situations like those in which the vice president would take over the president's powers and duties.

:::vocab
Casting vote: A tie-breaking vote.
Lieutenant governor: A state's second-highest official, who takes over if the governor can't serve.
:::

:::commentary
Under the original Constitution, each elector cast two votes for president. Whoever got the most votes became president, and the runner-up became vice president. This caused trouble. In 1796, rivals John Adams and Thomas Jefferson ended up as president and vice president. In 1800, Jefferson and his own running mate, Aaron Burr, tied, throwing the election to the House. The 12th Amendment (1804) fixed this by having electors cast separate votes for president and vice president.

The vice president's main constitutional jobs are still the ones Hamilton describes: presiding over the Senate, breaking ties, and replacing the president if necessary. Nine vice presidents have become president because the president died or resigned.
:::

:::today
The Electoral College is now one of the most argued-about parts of the Constitution. Five times in American history, including in 2000 and 2016, a candidate won the presidency while losing the national popular vote. Critics say this violates the principle of "one person, one vote" and lets a few swing states decide elections. Supporters say it protects smaller states, forces candidates to build broad coalitions across regions, and contains disputes within individual states. Some states have joined an agreement, the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, to give their electoral votes to the winner of the national popular vote once enough states sign on. Hamilton's essay is quoted on both sides.
:::

:::questions
- Hamilton thought electors would use their own judgment. Today they almost always vote as pledged. Does that change whether the Electoral College makes sense?
- Hamilton worried most about foreign influence in choosing the president. How might a foreign power try to influence an American election today?
- Should the president be chosen by a national popular vote instead? What would be gained and lost?
- Hamilton predicted the system would almost always produce presidents "pre-eminent for ability and virtue." How would you test whether he was right?
:::
