:::summary
This essay is a history lesson. Publius looks at two leagues of ancient Greek city-states to show what happens when a central council can act only on its member states. The first, the **Amphictyonic Council**, had impressive powers on paper. But its members stayed sovereign, the strongest cities bullied the weaker ones, and the Greeks fought each other instead of uniting. In the end, Philip of Macedon bribed his way into the council and took control of Greece. The second, the **Achaean League**, was a closer and wiser union, and its cities were calmer and more just. But foreign powers, first Macedon and then Rome, split its members apart and finally destroyed it. The lesson: weak leagues fall apart into conflict among their members much more often than they turn into tyranny at the center.
:::

:::keypoints
- The **Amphictyonic Council** looked strong on paper. It could make war, settle disputes, fine members, and use the whole league's force against them.
- In practice it failed, because its deputies were chosen by the cities as governments and its powers acted only on the cities.
- The strongest members, **Athens, Sparta, and Thebes**, took turns dominating the rest. Deputies of strong cities pressured and bribed those of weak ones.
- Even during wars with Persia and Macedon, the Greeks never acted together, and some members were always working for the enemy.
- **Philip of Macedon** used a quarrel among the members to gain a seat on the council and make himself master of Greece.
- The **Achaean League** was a closer union with a wiser structure. Its cities were more moderate and just than cities that stood alone.
- Even so, Macedon and then **Rome** pulled its members apart by appealing to their pride and their "sovereignty."
- History shows that federal bodies tend toward **anarchy among the members**, not **tyranny in the head**.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 15](/papers/15/) through [No. 17](/papers/17/), Hamilton argued that the Articles of Confederation failed because Congress could only make rules for states, not for individual people. Nos. 18, 19, and 20 back up that claim with history. They review earlier confederacies, from ancient Greece to the Dutch Republic of the 1780s.

These three essays are usually credited to Madison, with help from Hamilton. Before the Constitutional Convention, Madison had studied old confederacies closely and written up notes on them. Much of the material here comes from that research. Opponents of the Constitution warned that a strong central government would crush the states. Publius uses these examples to argue the opposite danger was greater.
:::

## A famous Greek league {orig=1-3}

Among the confederacies of the ancient world, the most important was the union of Greek republics joined together under the **Amphictyonic Council**. From the best accounts we have of this famous institution, it was a lot like the present Confederation of the American states, and it can teach us a great deal.

Its members kept their character as independent and sovereign states, and each had an equal vote in the federal council. This council had general authority to:

- propose and decide whatever it judged necessary for the common welfare of Greece;
- declare and carry on war;
- settle, as a final authority, all disputes between members;
- fine the member that started a conflict;
- use the whole force of the confederacy against members who disobeyed;
- admit new members.

The Amphictyons also guarded religion and the great treasures that belonged to the temple at Delphi. There they had the right to judge disputes between the people of Delphi and the visitors who came to consult the oracle. To make the federal powers even more effective, the members swore an oath to defend and protect the united cities. They promised to punish anyone who broke this oath, and to take revenge on anyone who robbed the temple.

In theory, and on paper, this set of powers seems more than enough for all general purposes. In several important ways, they go beyond the powers listed in our Articles of Confederation. The Amphictyons had control of the religious beliefs of the time, which were one of the main tools used to keep governments running then. They had a declared right to use force against cities that resisted. And they were bound by oath to use that right when necessary.

:::vocab
Confederacy: A league of independent states that agree to act together for certain purposes.
Amphictyonic Council: The council of an ancient league of Greek peoples, centered on the temple of Apollo at Delphi.
Oracle: At Delphi, a priestess believed to speak for the god Apollo. People traveled from all over Greece to ask her questions.
Sovereign: Having supreme, independent authority; not subject to any higher power.
Refractory: Stubborn and disobedient.
:::

:::commentary
Delphi, in central Greece, held the temple of Apollo, one of the holiest places in the Greek world. Cities and rulers sent rich gifts there, so the temple became very wealthy. The council that protected it had real religious prestige.

Madison treats the Amphictyonic Council as a political government, much like Congress. Many modern historians see it as mainly a religious association that looked after Delphi and its festivals, rather than a true federal government. Madison relied on the history books available in his day. So the comparison is a little neater than the real history. But his basic point, that the council could not control powerful members, matches what we know.
:::

## Theory versus practice {orig=4-7}

But the real experience was very different from the theory. Like the powers of our present Congress, the council's powers were used by deputies who were appointed entirely by the cities as political bodies. And those powers acted on the cities only as political bodies. This caused the weakness, the disorder, and finally the destruction of the confederacy.

The more powerful members were not kept in respect and obedience. Instead, they took turns ruling over all the rest. According to Demosthenes, Athens was the judge and master of Greece for seventy-three years. Sparta then ruled it for twenty-nine years. Later, after the battle of Leuctra, it was Thebes's turn to dominate.

The Greek writer Plutarch tells us that it happened all too often that the deputies of the strongest cities frightened and bribed those of the weaker ones. Decisions then went in favor of the most powerful side.

Even during dangerous wars of defense against Persia and Macedon, the members never acted together. Some of them, more or fewer at different times, were always being fooled or paid by the common enemy. The breaks between foreign wars were filled with internal upheavals, violence, and slaughter.

After the war with Xerxes, the king of Persia, Sparta apparently demanded that a number of cities be thrown out of the confederacy for their disloyalty. Athens saw that Sparta would lose fewer supporters than Athens would if this happened, and that Sparta would then control the council's decisions. So Athens fought the proposal hard and defeated it.

This piece of history proves three things at once: the union was ineffective, its most powerful members were ambitious and jealous, and the rest were dependent and humiliated. In theory, the smaller members had the right to circle the common center as equals, with equal pride and dignity. In fact, they had become mere moons circling the large planets.

:::vocab
Demosthenes: A famous Athenian speaker of the 300s BC who warned the Greeks against Philip of Macedon.
Plutarch: A Greek writer (about AD 46 to after 119) known for his biographies of famous Greeks and Romans.
Xerxes: King of Persia who invaded Greece in 480 BC and was driven back.
Lacedaemonians: Another name for the Spartans. Lacedaemon was the official name of Sparta's territory.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's main point is the same one Hamilton made in [No. 15](/papers/15/). The council worked through the member cities, not on the people in them. So whenever a powerful city refused to cooperate, the council's grand powers meant nothing.

The battle of Leuctra, in 371 BC, was a famous victory of Thebes over Sparta. It ended Sparta's long reputation as unbeatable on land and began a short period when Thebes was the leading Greek power.
:::

## Victory over Persia, then war among Greeks {orig=8}

The French historian Abbé Millot says that if the Greeks had been as wise as they were brave, experience would have taught them that they needed a closer union. They would have used the peace that followed their victory over the Persians to make that reform. Instead of this obvious policy, Athens and Sparta grew proud of their victories and glory. They became first rivals and then enemies. They did each other far more harm than they had suffered from Xerxes. Their jealousies, fears, hatreds, and injuries ended in the famous Peloponnesian War. That war ended in the ruin and enslavement of the Athenians, who had started it.

:::commentary
The Greeks defeated Xerxes' invasion at the battles of Salamis (480 BC) and Plataea (479 BC). About fifty years later, the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) pitted Athens and its allies against Sparta and its allies. Athens lost.

Millot's claim that Athens "had begun it" is one view. The ancient historian Thucydides, who lived through the war, wrote that its real cause was the growth of Athenian power and the fear this caused in Sparta. So blame for the war is still debated.

Notice the pattern Madison is pointing to. The Greeks united only when a foreign enemy forced them to. Once the danger passed, they turned on each other. Publius warned in [No. 6](/papers/6/) through [No. 8](/papers/8/) that the American states might do the same thing.
:::

## How Philip of Macedon took control {orig=9-10}

A weak government, when it is not at war, is always torn by internal conflict. And internal conflict always brings new disasters from abroad.

The Phocians, one of the Greek peoples, plowed up some sacred land that belonged to the temple of Apollo. Following the religious beliefs of the time, the Amphictyonic Council fined them for this offense against the gods. Athens and Sparta encouraged the Phocians, and they refused to obey the decision. Thebes and some other cities set out to uphold the council's authority and avenge the insulted god. Since they were the weaker side, they asked for help from Philip, the king of Macedon, who had been secretly feeding the conflict.

Philip happily seized the chance to carry out the plans he had long made against the liberties of Greece. Through plots and bribes, he won over the popular leaders of several cities. Through their influence and votes, he got a seat on the Amphictyonic Council. And through his cunning and his armies, he made himself master of the confederacy.

These were the results of the false principle this interesting institution was built on. As one wise writer on the fate of Greece has said, if Greece had been united by a tighter confederation, and had stuck with it, she would never have worn the chains of Macedon. She might even have been a barrier against the vast ambitions of Rome.

:::quote
Had Greece, says a judicious observer on her fate, been united by a stricter confederation, and persevered in her union, she would never have worn the chains of Macedon; and might have proved a barrier to the vast projects of Rome.

Meaning: A closer union could have saved Greek freedom. A loose one let outsiders divide and conquer.
:::

:::vocab
Macedon: An ancient kingdom just north of Greece. It is also called Macedonia.
Sacrilegious: Disrespectful toward something holy.
Intrigue: Secret plotting to gain an advantage.
:::

:::commentary
Philip II ruled Macedon from 359 to 336 BC and was the father of Alexander the Great. The conflict Madison describes is known as the Third Sacred War (356–346 BC). When it ended, Macedon took over the Phocians' seats on the Amphictyonic Council. In 338 BC Philip defeated Athens and Thebes at the battle of Chaeronea, and after that Macedon dominated Greece.

This is a classic case of what is sometimes called "divide and conquer." A league that couldn't settle its own quarrels invited a stronger outsider to settle them, and the outsider stayed. Madison wants Americans to see that European powers could play the same game with thirteen quarreling states.
:::

## The Achaean League: a closer union {orig=11-15}

The Achaean League, as it is called, was another union of Greek republics. It also teaches us valuable lessons.

This union was much closer, and much more wisely organized, than the one just described. As we will see, it did not escape a similar disaster. But it did not deserve that fate nearly as much.

The cities in this league kept their local powers, appointed their own officials, and were completely equal. The league's senate, where the cities were represented, had the sole right to:

- make war and peace;
- send and receive ambassadors;
- make treaties and alliances;
- appoint a chief official, called the **praetor**.

The praetor commanded the league's armies. With the advice and consent of ten of the senators, he ran the government when the senate was not meeting. He also had a large role in its debates when it was meeting. Under the league's original constitution, two praetors shared this job. But after trying it, the league decided it preferred one.

It appears that all the cities had the same laws and customs, the same weights and measures, and the same money. But how far this came from the authority of the federal council is uncertain. We are told only that the cities were in a way required to accept the same laws and customs. When the general Philopoemen brought Sparta into the league, Sparta had to give up the institutions and laws of Lycurgus, its ancient lawgiver, and adopt those of the Achaeans. The Amphictyonic confederacy, which Sparta had also belonged to, had left Sparta completely free to run its own government and make its own laws. This fact alone shows a very important difference in the character of the two systems.

It is a great pity that so few records remain of this remarkable political structure. If we could learn how it was built inside and how it normally worked, it would probably teach us more about the science of federal government than any other similar experiment we know of.

:::vocab
Achaean League: An alliance of Greek city-states (about 280–146 BC) that was eventually conquered by Rome.
Praetor: The title Publius uses for the Achaean League's elected chief official, who led its armies. In Greek, he was called the *strategos*, or general.
Advice and consent: Approval given after being consulted. The Constitution uses the same phrase for the Senate's role in treaties and appointments.
Municipal: Having to do with a city's own local government.
Lycurgus: The legendary founder of Sparta's strict laws and way of life.
:::

:::commentary
The Achaean League was centered in the Peloponnese, the large peninsula of southern Greece. Madison clearly admired it. Its central government could make war, run foreign policy, and even push its members toward common laws. That is much closer to what the Constitution proposed than to the Articles.

Notice that the Achaeans tried a two-person executive and switched to one. Hamilton uses this same example in [No. 70](/papers/70/) to argue that the presidency should be held by one person, not a council.

Madison is honest about the limits of his evidence. He admits that "such imperfect monuments remain" of the league. Much of what we know comes from the Greek historian Polybius, whose own father was an Achaean leader.
:::

## Calmer cities, but not a perfect union {orig=16-17}

All the historians who write about Achaean affairs seem to agree on one important fact. Both after the league was renewed by Aratus and before it was broken up by the schemes of Macedon, its government was far more moderate and just than any city that held all the powers of sovereignty ALONE. Its people were also much less given to violence and rebellion.

The French writer Abbé Mably, in his study of Greece, says that popular government, which was so stormy elsewhere, caused no disorder in the members of the Achaean republic. That was BECAUSE THERE IT WAS CALMED BY THE GENERAL AUTHORITY AND LAWS OF THE CONFEDERACY.

However, we should not jump to conclusions. Factions surely did stir up the individual cities to some degree. And we certainly shouldn't conclude that proper order and harmony ruled the system as a whole. The changing fortunes and final fate of the league show the opposite clearly enough.

:::vocab
Sedition: Speech or action that stirs up rebellion against a government.
Faction: A group, majority or minority, united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community. Madison explains it in detail in No. 10.
Aratus: A leader from the city of Sicyon who revived and led the Achaean League in the 200s BC.
:::

:::commentary
This passage connects to the argument Hamilton made in [No. 9](/papers/9/) and Madison made in [No. 10](/papers/10/). Small republics on their own were famous for violent swings between mob rule and tyranny. But a union could calm them down. Mably's point is that the league's general laws acted as a check on the passions inside each city. That is close to what Madison argues about a large republic controlling faction.
:::

## How Macedon and Rome broke the league apart {orig=18-19}

While the Amphictyonic confederacy lasted, the Achaean League, which included only the less important cities, played a small role in Greek affairs. When the Amphictyonic league fell to Macedon, Philip and his son Alexander chose to leave the Achaeans alone. But the kings who came after them followed a different policy.

They used the tactic of dividing the Achaeans. Each city was tempted into following its own separate interest, and the union broke apart. Some of the cities fell under the harsh rule of Macedonian soldiers stationed in them. Others fell under tyrants who rose out of their own disorder.

Before long, shame and oppression woke up their love of liberty. A few cities reunited. Others followed their example as they found chances to get rid of their tyrants. Soon the league covered almost the whole Peloponnese. Macedon saw it growing, but its own internal conflicts kept it from stopping it. All of Greece caught the excitement and seemed ready to unite in one confederacy. But then Sparta and Athens, jealous and envious of the Achaeans' rising glory, threw cold water on the effort.

Fearing Macedon's power, the league sought an alliance with the kings of Egypt and Syria. As heirs to parts of Alexander's empire, those kings were rivals of the king of Macedon. But Cleomenes, the king of Sparta, ruined this plan. Driven by ambition, he launched an unprovoked attack on his neighbors, the Achaeans. As an enemy of Macedon, he had enough influence with the rulers of Egypt and Syria to get them to break their agreements with the league.

The Achaeans now faced a hard choice. They could give in to Cleomenes, or they could beg for help from Macedon, their former oppressor. They chose Macedon. The quarrels of the Greeks always gave that powerful neighbor a welcome chance to meddle in their affairs. A Macedonian army quickly appeared, and Cleomenes was defeated.

But the Achaeans soon learned, as often happens, that a victorious and powerful ally is just another name for a master. All their most humble obedience could get from him was permission to keep using their own laws.

Philip, who was now king of Macedon, soon provoked the Greeks into forming new alliances against his harsh rule. The Achaeans had been weakened by internal conflicts and by the revolt of Messene, one of their members. But joined by the Aetolians and the Athenians, they raised the flag of resistance. Even with this support, they found they were not strong enough. So once again they turned to the dangerous step of calling in foreign armies. The Romans, who were invited, eagerly accepted. Philip was beaten and Macedon was conquered.

Then a new crisis hit the league. Quarrels broke out among its members, and the Romans encouraged them. Callicrates and other popular leaders were paid by Rome to mislead their own people. To feed conflict and disorder even more, the Romans had already proclaimed "universal liberty" throughout Greece, to the surprise of those who trusted them. With the same sly purpose, they now lured members away from the league by telling them it violated their sovereignty.

By these tricks, this union, the last hope of Greece and the last hope of ancient liberty, was torn to pieces. It was left so weak and confused that Rome's armies easily finished the ruin that Rome's schemes had begun. The Achaeans were cut to pieces, and Achaia was put in chains. It is still groaning under them today.

:::quote
The Achaeans soon experienced, as often happens, that a victorious and powerful ally is but another name for a master.

Meaning: When a weak people calls in a strong foreign power to protect it, the protector often ends up in charge.
:::

:::note
Publius's footnote explains what the Romans meant by "universal liberty." It was, he says, just a more attractive name for making each member independent of the league's central authority.
:::

:::commentary
This section covers more than a century of history, and Madison moves fast. Some names to know:

- **Cleomenes** was Cleomenes III, king of Sparta. Macedon's army defeated him at the battle of Sellasia in 222 BC.
- **"Philip, who was now on the throne"** is a different Philip from the one earlier in this essay. He was Philip V of Macedon. Rome defeated him in 197 BC.
- **"Universal liberty"**: In 196 BC, the Roman general Flamininus announced that the Greek cities would be free. Publius sees this as a trick to keep the Greeks divided.
- **The end:** In 146 BC Rome crushed the Achaean League and destroyed the city of Corinth.

"Groaning at this hour" refers to the situation in 1787, when Greece was ruled by the Ottoman Empire. Greece did not win its independence until the Greek War of Independence, which began in 1821.

Notice the Roman tactic Madison highlights. Rome told each city that the league was an insult to its sovereignty. Anti-Federalists were telling Americans that the Constitution threatened the sovereignty of their states. Madison doesn't say so directly, but readers would have noticed the parallel.
:::

## The lesson: weak leagues fall apart {orig=20}

I have thought it worthwhile to sketch this important part of history for two reasons. It teaches more than one lesson. And as an addition to my outline of the Achaean constitution, it strongly shows that federal bodies tend to fall into **anarchy among the members**, rather than **tyranny in the head**.

:::quote
...it emphatically illustrates the tendency of federal bodies rather to anarchy among the members, than to tyranny in the head.

Meaning: Leagues of states are more likely to be destroyed by their members pulling apart than by their central government growing too strong.
:::

:::vocab
Anarchy: A state of disorder with no effective government.
Tyranny: Cruel and unjust use of power, usually by one ruler or a small group.
:::

:::commentary
This one sentence is the point of the whole essay. Anti-Federalists feared that a strong national government would become a tyrant over the states. Madison answers that, in history, the usual danger runs the other way. The members of a league pull it apart, and foreign enemies take advantage.

Critics could reply that ancient Greek leagues were very different from the United States. They were surrounded by powerful kingdoms and had no ocean protecting them. And a stronger central government might bring dangers the ancient leagues never faced. Still, the pattern Madison describes, members being bribed, divided, and picked off one by one, was a real worry for a young country with Britain, Spain, and France all nearby.
:::

:::today
The tactic Madison describes, an outside power dividing an alliance by making separate deals with its members, is still common in world politics. Alliances and unions of countries today often worry that a rival nation will pull members away one at a time with trade deals, pressure, or appeals to national pride. Supporters of closer unions say members are stronger together. Critics say closer unions take away too much of each member's right to govern itself. Madison's history lesson is one side of a debate that is still going on.
:::
