:::summary
Critics attacked the House of Representatives for being too small. With only 65 members at first, they said, it couldn't be trusted with power, couldn't know local conditions, would favor the rich, and would fall further behind as the population grew. Madison answers the first charge here. He argues that there is no exact right size for an assembly. It must be large enough for real debate and to resist plots, but not so large that it becomes a mob: "Had every Athenian citizen been a Socrates, every Athenian assembly would still have been a mob." The House will grow quickly with the population. He can't imagine 65 or 100 representatives, chosen by the people every two years and watched by the states, plotting tyranny. He ends by warning that people need checks, but they also have qualities that justify "esteem and confidence," or self-government would be impossible.
:::

:::keypoints
- Critics made **four charges** about the House's size. This paper answers the first: that so few members can't safely be trusted.
- There is **no precise answer** to how large an assembly should be. The states differ widely.
- The ratio of representatives to people **should not stay the same** as a population grows.
- An assembly needs **enough members** for free discussion and to prevent easy plotting, but **not so many** that it turns into a crowd ruled by passion.
- The House will start with **65 members** and, Madison predicts, will grow to 100, then 200, then 400 as the population grows.
- The Revolutionary Congress was smaller, less accountable, and held more power, yet it **was not corrupted**.
- Members of Congress **can't take new federal offices** created during their term, which limits the president's power to buy them off.
- People have faults that call for distrust, but also virtues that call for **"esteem and confidence."** Republics depend on those virtues.
:::

:::context Background
The size of the House was one of the Anti-Federalists' favorite targets. Many of them believed that a representative should know his constituents personally and share their circumstances. A House of 65 members for some three million people seemed to them far too small. George Mason of Virginia, who refused to sign the Constitution, complained that the House had "not the substance but the shadow only of representation."

The size question also came up at the very end of the Constitutional Convention. On its last day, September 17, 1787, George Washington spoke up in favor of changing the minimum district size from 40,000 people to 30,000, so that the House could be larger. It was one of the only times he spoke in the debates, and the change was approved. In this paper, Madison begins a series of answers: the first charge here, and the others in [No. 56](/papers/56/), [No. 57](/papers/57/), and [No. 58](/papers/58/).
:::

## Four charges against the House's size {orig=1}

The number of members in the House of Representatives is another very interesting way to look at this branch of Congress. Hardly any part of the whole Constitution seems more worthy of attention, because of the reputation of the people who have attacked it and the apparent strength of their arguments. The charges against it are these:

1. So small a number of representatives will be an unsafe guardian of the public interests.
2. They will not have proper knowledge of the local conditions of their many constituents.
3. They will be taken from the class of citizens who will sympathize least with the feelings of the mass of the people, and who will be most likely to aim at permanently raising up the few by pushing down the many.
4. However small the number is at first, it will fall further and further behind as the population grows, because obstacles will prevent the number of representatives from growing to match.

:::vocab
Constituents: The people a representative serves.
:::

## No exact answer: the states differ widely {orig=2}

In general, no political problem is harder to solve precisely than the question of the best size for a representative legislature. And there is no point on which the states differ more, whether we compare their legislatures directly with each other or look at how many voters each representative serves.

Set aside the difference between the smallest and largest states. Delaware's most numerous branch has twenty-one representatives, while Massachusetts's has between three and four hundred. Even among states with nearly equal populations, there is a large difference.

- Pennsylvania has no more than one-fifth as many representatives as Massachusetts.
- New York's population is to South Carolina's as six is to five, yet New York has little more than one-third as many representatives.
- There is just as big a gap between Georgia and Delaware or Rhode Island.
- In Pennsylvania, there is no more than one representative for every four or five thousand people. In Rhode Island, there is at least one for every thousand.
- Under Georgia's constitution, there may be as many as one representative for every ten voters, which must be far more than in any other state.

:::commentary
Madison's point is that the states themselves had no agreed-upon rule. The Massachusetts house was enormous because each town had the right to send its own representative. Georgia, a frontier state with few voters spread over a large area, was at the other extreme. If Americans couldn't agree on a ratio within their own states, Madison suggests, it was unfair to demand a perfect one for the nation.
:::

## Too few or too many {orig=3}

Another general point is that the ratio of representatives to people should not be the same when the people are very numerous as when they are very few. If Virginia's representatives were set by Rhode Island's standard, Virginia would now have between four and five hundred of them. Twenty or thirty years from now, it would have a thousand. On the other hand, if Pennsylvania's ratio were applied to Delaware, Delaware's assembly would shrink to seven or eight members.

Nothing could be more misleading than to base our political calculations on simple arithmetic. Sixty or seventy men may be trusted with a certain amount of power more safely than six or seven. But it doesn't follow that six or seven hundred would be a proportionally better guardian. And if we carry the idea further, to six or seven thousand, the whole argument should be turned upside down.

The truth is that, in all cases, a certain minimum number seems necessary. It secures the benefits of free discussion and debate, and it guards against a small group too easily joining together for improper purposes. On the other hand, the number should be kept within a certain maximum, to avoid the confusion and lack of self-control of a crowd. In all very large assemblies, no matter what kind of people they are made of, passion always grabs the scepter from reason. Even if every Athenian citizen had been a Socrates, every Athenian assembly would still have been a mob.

:::quote
In all very numerous assemblies, of whatever character composed, passion never fails to wrest the sceptre from reason. Had every Athenian citizen been a Socrates, every Athenian assembly would still have been a mob.

Meaning: The problem with huge assemblies isn't the people in them. Even a crowd of the wisest people in history would get swept up in emotion. Size itself is the problem. (A "sceptre," or scepter, is a rod held by a king as a sign of power.)
:::

:::commentary
This is the most famous line in the paper, and it is a striking claim. Socrates was the great philosopher of ancient Athens, a symbol of calm reason. In Athens, the assembly was open to all adult male citizens, and thousands could attend a single meeting. Madison's point is that crowds behave differently from individuals. People in a large group feel less personal responsibility, are easily swayed by skilled speakers, and get caught up in the mood of the moment.

Notice that Madison is arguing in two directions at once. He is defending the House against the charge of being too *small*, but he is also warning against making it too *large*. Today's House has 435 members. Some people say this is too few for a nation of more than 330 million. Others say a much larger House would become exactly the kind of unruly crowd Madison feared.
:::

## Limited powers need fewer members {orig=4-5}

We should also remember here the points I made about two-year elections. Congress has limited powers and will be checked by the state legislatures. For that reason, it can be elected less often than public safety might otherwise require. For the same reason, its members can be fewer than if Congress held all lawmaking power and faced only the usual limits that other legislatures face.

With these general ideas in mind, let us weigh the objections to the number of members proposed for the House of Representatives. The first objection is that so small a number can't be safely trusted with so much power.

:::connection
Madison made the argument about Congress's limited powers and the watchful state legislatures in [No. 52](/papers/52/), when he defended two-year terms.
:::

## How the House will grow {orig=6}

When the government begins, the House will have sixty-five members. Within three years a census will be taken. Then the number may be increased to one for every thirty thousand inhabitants. Every ten years after that, a new census is to be taken, and the number may keep increasing within the same limit.

It is not a wild guess that the first census, at one representative for every thirty thousand people, will raise the number to at least one hundred. Counting the Black population at three-fifths, as the Constitution provides, there can be little doubt that the population of the United States will reach three million by that time, if it hasn't already. After twenty-five years, at the expected rate of growth, the number of representatives will reach two hundred. After fifty years, it will reach four hundred. I assume that number will end all fears about the House being too small.

Here I take for granted something I will prove later, when I answer the fourth objection: that the number of representatives will be increased from time to time in the way the Constitution provides. If that were not true, I would admit that the objection carries very great weight.

:::vocab
Census: An official count of a country's population. The Constitution requires one every ten years.
Three-fifths clause: The part of the Constitution that counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a person when dividing up seats in the House.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's phrase "estimating the negroes in the proportion of three fifths" refers to the **three-fifths clause** in Article I, Section 2. When seats in the House were divided among the states, each enslaved person counted as three-fifths of a free person. Enslaved people, of course, could not vote. The rule gave slaveholding states more seats in the House, and more votes in the Electoral College, than their free population alone would have earned. Madison defends the clause in [No. 54](/papers/54/). The 13th Amendment (1865) abolished slavery, and the 14th Amendment (1868) replaced the three-fifths rule by counting "the whole number of persons in each State."

Madison's first prediction was right: after the 1790 census, the House grew to 105 members. His longer-range numbers were too high. The House did not pass 400 members until after the 1910 census, and it actually *shrank* once, after the 1840 census. It reached 435 members in 1913, and a 1929 law fixed it at that size.
:::

## Can sixty-five men be trusted? {orig=7}

So the real question is this: is the small number, as a temporary arrangement, dangerous to public liberty? Can sixty-five members for a few years, and one or two hundred for a few more, be safely trusted with a limited and well-guarded power to make laws for the United States?

I must admit I could not answer "no" without first erasing every impression I have formed about the present character of the American people, the spirit that drives the state legislatures, and the principles that are part of the political character of every class of citizens.

- I can't imagine that the American people, in their present mood or under any circumstances likely to arise soon, will choose sixty-five or a hundred men inclined to form and carry out a plan of tyranny or betrayal, and then repeat that choice every second year.
- I can't imagine that the state legislatures, which will have so many reasons to watch Congress and so many ways to counter it, would fail to discover or defeat a plot by Congress against the liberties of the people they both serve.
- I am equally unable to imagine that there are now, or will soon be, sixty-five or a hundred men in the United States able to win the votes of the people at large, who would either want or dare to betray their solemn trust within the short span of two years.

What changes in circumstances, time, and a larger population may bring about in our country would take a prophet to predict, and I don't claim to be one. But judging from the present situation, and its likely state for a reasonable time to come, I must declare that the liberties of America can't be unsafe in the number of hands proposed by the federal Constitution.

:::commentary
Madison is careful not to overclaim here. He doesn't say a small House would be safe forever. He says it will be safe *for now*, given the character of the American people and the watchfulness of the states. He openly admits he can't predict what a much larger, older country might become.

Critics might reply that a constitution is supposed to protect against bad times, not just good ones. Madison's own words elsewhere seem to agree. In [No. 10](/papers/10/), he warns that "enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm."
:::

## Are we afraid of foreign gold? {orig=8}

Where could the danger come from? Are we afraid of foreign gold? If foreign gold could so easily corrupt our federal rulers and lead them to trap and betray their constituents, how is it that we are now a free and independent nation?

The Congress that led us through the Revolution was a smaller body than the House will be. Its members were not chosen by the people at large and did not answer to them. They were appointed every year and could be recalled at any time, yet they generally stayed for three years. Before the Articles of Confederation were ratified, they often stayed even longer. They always met in secret. They alone handled our dealings with foreign nations. Throughout the war, they held the fate of their country in their hands more than we can hope will ever be true of our future representatives. And with so great a prize at stake, and such eagerness on the part of the side that lost it, we can well suppose that the enemy would not have hesitated to use means other than force.

Yet we know from happy experience that the public trust was not betrayed. Nor has the honesty of our public councils on this point ever been damaged, even by the whispers of rumor.


:::commentary
The "party which lost it" is Great Britain. Madison's point is that the British would surely have tried bribery if it could have worked. The Continental Congress was not perfect. It was often slow and short of money, and it had real quarrels. But Madison's main claim holds up well: historians know of no case in which Britain managed to buy off Congress as a body.

The most famous American traitor of the war, Benedict Arnold, was an army general, not a member of Congress. His betrayal in 1780 shocked the country precisely because it was so rare.
:::

## Corruption by the president or Senate, and the case for trust {orig=9}

Is the danger feared from the other branches of the federal government? But where would the president, or the Senate, or both, find the means to corrupt the House? Their salaries, we can assume, will not be more than enough for very different purposes. And they can't be raised without first corrupting the House of Representatives. Their private fortunes can't be a source of danger, since they must all be American citizens. So the only means they could have is the power to hand out appointments to office.

Is this where suspicion lays its charge? Sometimes we are told that the president will use up this supply of corruption in overpowering the virtue of the Senate. Now we are told the faithfulness of the House will be the victim. Such a greedy and treacherous alliance among the different parts of government is very unlikely. They rest on foundations as different as republican principles allow, and they are all answerable to the society they govern. That alone should calm this fear.

But luckily, the Constitution has provided an extra safeguard. Members of Congress can't be appointed to any federal office that is created, or whose salary is increased, during the term they were elected for. So the only offices that could be handed out to current members are ones that open up through ordinary events, such as a death or a resignation. To suppose that these would be enough to buy the guardians of the people, chosen by the people themselves, is to throw away every rule for judging what is likely. It replaces reason with a blind and unlimited suspicion, against which all reasoning must be useless.

The sincere friends of liberty who give in to this extreme suspicion don't realize the harm they do to their own cause. There is a degree of wickedness in human beings that calls for a certain amount of caution and distrust. But there are also other qualities in human nature that justify a certain amount of respect and trust. Republican government assumes these good qualities exist more than any other form of government does. If the pictures some of us have painted, out of political suspicion, were true likenesses of human character, the conclusion would be this: people don't have enough virtue to govern themselves, and only the chains of despotism can keep them from destroying and devouring one another.

:::quote
As there is a degree of depravity in mankind which requires a certain degree of circumspection and distrust, so there are other qualities in human nature which justify a certain portion of esteem and confidence.

Meaning: People are capable of real wrongdoing, so we need to be careful and build in checks. But people also have real virtues, so some trust is justified too. "Depravity" means wickedness, and "circumspection" means caution.
:::

:::quote
Republican government presupposes the existence of these qualities in a higher degree than any other form.

Meaning: Self-government only works if ordinary people and their representatives have some honesty and good sense. A republic depends on virtue more than any other kind of government does.
:::

:::vocab
Emoluments: Salary and other payments from an office.
Despotism: Rule by one person with unlimited power.
Depravity: Wickedness or moral corruption.
:::

:::commentary
The safeguard Madison mentions is in Article I, Section 6, often called the **Ineligibility Clause** or **Emoluments Clause**. It still applies today. When a member of Congress is named to a cabinet post whose salary was raised during that member's term, Congress has sometimes passed a special law lowering the salary back down, a workaround often called a "Saxbe fix" after a senator who became attorney general in the 1970s.

The final paragraph is one of the most important statements of Madison's view of human nature. It balances his famous warning in [No. 51](/papers/51/) that "if men were angels, no government would be necessary." Madison was neither a cynic nor an optimist. He believed people need checks because they are not angels, but that self-government is possible because they are not devils either. The Anti-Federalists' deep distrust of power, he warns, can be pushed so far that it becomes an argument against democracy itself.
:::

:::today
The size of the House is a live debate again. The House has stayed at 435 members since 1913, while the U.S. population has more than tripled. Each member now represents about 760,000 people. Supporters of a larger House say it would bring representatives closer to voters and make the Electoral College more closely match the population. Opponents say a much larger House would be harder to manage and, as Madison warned, more like a crowd than a deliberative body. A proposed amendment about House size was one of the twelve amendments Congress sent to the states in 1789, along with what became the Bill of Rights, but it was never ratified. Madison's closing balance, between healthy distrust of power and basic trust in people, remains a good test for any argument about government.
:::
