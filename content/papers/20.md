:::summary
Publius ends his history lesson with the **United Netherlands**, the Dutch Republic. On paper, its central assembly, the **States-General**, could make war and peace, raise armies, and demand money from the seven provinces. But every important decision needed unanimous agreement, and the provinces paid their shares only when they chose. In practice the government was weak, the provinces quarreled, and foreign diplomats played them against each other. To survive emergencies, the States-General often had to break its own constitution. Publius warns that this kind of rule-breaking is how tyranny often begins. He closes the three essays with their central lesson: "a sovereignty over sovereigns, a government over governments," replaces law with violence.
:::

:::keypoints
- The Dutch union is made of **seven sovereign provinces**, each made of independent cities, and important decisions require **unanimity**.
- The **States-General** has broad powers on paper, but it needs the approval of the provinces for anything important.
- The **stadtholder**, a hereditary prince, is the union's executive and military chief. His real influence comes from his family, wealth, and offices in each province.
- In practice the system shows "imbecility in the government; discord among the provinces; foreign influence and indignities."
- Provinces that won't pay their share are sometimes forced to pay **"at the point of the bayonet."** This only works because one province, **Holland**, is far stronger than the rest.
- In emergencies the States-General **oversteps its constitution**. Publius warns that tyranny often grows out of powers seized in a crisis by a government that was too weak.
- The Dutch tried four times to reform their system through special assemblies, and failed every time. Americans should be grateful their Convention agreed.
- The lesson of all these examples: a government over governments, rather than over individuals, puts **violence in place of law**.
:::

:::context Background
The Dutch Republic was well known to Americans. It had broken away from Spain, and in 1579 its northern provinces formed a union, the Union of Utrecht. By the 1600s it had become one of the richest trading nations in the world. New York itself began as a Dutch colony, New Netherland. During the American Revolution, the Dutch Republic recognized the United States in 1782, and Dutch bankers lent money to the new nation.

When this essay appeared in late 1787, the Dutch Republic was in crisis. A reform movement called the Patriots had challenged the power of the stadtholder, and in September 1787 a Prussian army invaded to put him back in control. Publius refers to this crisis near the end of the essay. This is the last of three history essays, following [No. 18](/papers/18/) and [No. 19](/papers/19/).
:::

## How the Dutch union is built {orig=1-4}

The United Netherlands is a confederacy of republics, or rather of aristocracies, with a very unusual structure. Yet it confirms all the lessons we have learned from the confederacies already reviewed.

The union is made up of seven equal and sovereign states, or provinces. Each province, in turn, is made up of equal and independent cities. In all important matters, not only the provinces but also the cities must agree unanimously.

The sovereignty of the union is represented by the **States-General**. This assembly usually has about fifty deputies, appointed by the provinces. Some deputies hold their seats for life, some for six years, some for three, and some for one. The deputies from two provinces serve for as long as their province wishes.

The States-General has authority to:

- make treaties and alliances;
- make war and peace;
- raise armies and build fleets;
- set each province's quota and demand contributions.

In all these matters, however, it needs unanimous agreement and the approval of the provinces it represents.

It also has authority to appoint and receive ambassadors, carry out treaties and alliances already made, provide for collecting taxes on imports and exports, and regulate the mint while respecting the rights of the provinces. And it governs, as a sovereign, the territories that depend on the union.

The provinces may not, without general consent, make treaties with foreign nations. Nor may they set import taxes that hurt the other provinces, or charge their neighbors higher taxes than their own people. A council of state, a chamber of accounts, and five boards of admiralty help and strengthen the federal government.

:::vocab
States-General: The central assembly of the Dutch Republic, made up of deputies sent by the seven provinces.
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged class. In the Dutch cities, power was held by a small group of wealthy families.
Unanimity: Complete agreement, with no one opposed.
Quota: A fixed share that each member was supposed to contribute.
Admiralty: A board in charge of a navy.
:::

:::commentary
Notice how the Dutch system stacks unanimity on top of unanimity. Each city had to agree before its province could act, and each province had to agree before the union could act. A single stubborn city could, in theory, block the whole country. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress needed nine of thirteen states for major decisions and all thirteen to amend the Articles. Madison wants Americans to see that the Dutch problem was the American problem, only worse.

Madison calls the provinces "aristocracies" rather than republics. In [No. 39](/papers/39/), he says Holland had been called a republic even though none of its supreme authority came from the people.
:::

## The stadtholder {orig=5-10}

The chief executive of the union is the **stadtholder**, who is now a hereditary prince. His main weight and influence in the republic come from several sources:

- his independent title as a prince;
- his large family estates;
- his family ties to some of the leading rulers of Europe;
- and, perhaps most of all, the fact that he is stadtholder in the separate provinces as well as for the union.

As stadtholder of a province, he appoints town officials under certain rules, carries out provincial decisions, presides over provincial courts whenever he wishes, and has the power of pardon everywhere.

As stadtholder of the union, he also has important powers.

In his **political** role, he can settle disputes between the provinces when other methods fail. He takes part in the meetings of the States-General and in their special conferences. He receives foreign ambassadors, and he keeps agents at foreign courts for his own business.

In his **military** role, he commands the federal troops, provides for garrisons, and generally manages military affairs. He fills all army posts from colonel down to ensign, and he appoints the governors and officers of fortified towns.

In his **naval** role, he is admiral-general. He oversees and directs everything to do with the navy and other naval matters. He presides over the admiralty boards in person or through a stand-in. He appoints lieutenant-admirals and other officers. And he sets up military courts, whose sentences can't be carried out until he approves them.

His income, not counting his private wealth, is three hundred thousand florins. The standing army he commands has about forty thousand men.

:::vocab
Stadtholder: The chief executive and military commander of the Dutch Republic. The word originally meant "place-holder," a governor who ruled a province for an absent king.
Patrimonial: Inherited from one's family.
Garrison: Troops stationed in a town or fort to defend it.
Ensign: In this period, the lowest rank of army officer.
Florin: A Dutch coin, also called a guilder.
Standing army: A permanent army kept even in peacetime.
:::

:::commentary
Before the Dutch revolt, a stadtholder governed a province on behalf of the Spanish king. After independence, the princes of the House of Orange usually held the office, often in several provinces at once. In 1747 the office was made hereditary. In 1787 the stadtholder was William V. His wife was a sister of the king of Prussia, and his mother was a daughter of King George II of Britain. Those are the "family connections" Madison means.

At times the Dutch had no stadtholder at all, during the "stadtholderless periods" of the 1600s and 1700s. Madison mentions these gaps later in this essay. The office shows a confusing mix: a republic with a hereditary prince at its head.
:::

## The constitution on paper and in practice {orig=11-14}

That is the famous Belgic confederacy as it is described on parchment. What marks has practice actually stamped on it?

- weakness in the government;
- conflict among the provinces;
- foreign influence and humiliations;
- an insecure existence in peacetime;
- special disasters in war.

Long ago, the Dutch writer Grotius observed that only his countrymen's hatred of the House of Austria kept them from being ruined by the flaws in their constitution.

Another respected writer says that the Union of Utrecht gives the States-General enough authority, it seems, to keep harmony. But the jealousy of each province makes the practice very different from the theory.

Another says that the same document requires each province to raise certain contributions. But this rule never could be carried out, and probably never will be, because the inland provinces, which have little trade, can't pay an equal share.

:::vocab
Belgic confederacy: An old name for the Dutch union. *Belgica* was the Latin name for the Low Countries. It does not refer to modern Belgium.
Grotius: Hugo Grotius (1583–1645), a Dutch legal scholar often called the father of international law.
House of Austria: The Habsburg family, which ruled Spain and Austria. The Dutch fought a long war of independence against Habsburg Spain.
Union of Utrecht: The 1579 agreement that joined the northern Dutch provinces and served as their constitution.
:::

:::commentary
Grotius's point is that a common enemy can hold a weak union together. The Dutch stayed united because they hated and feared Spain. Americans in 1787 had a similar experience. The war with Britain held the states together, but once the war ended, the Union began to come apart. That's part of why Publius thought a stronger, permanent structure was needed in peacetime.
:::

## Breaking the rules to get money {orig=15-16}

When it comes to contributions, the usual practice is to set aside the rules of the constitution. Delay is dangerous. So the provinces that agree go ahead and pay their shares without waiting for the others. Then they try to get paid back by the others, often by sending delegations, or by whatever means they can. The great wealth and influence of the province of Holland allow it to do both of these things.

More than once, the missing payments have finally had to be collected at the point of the bayonet. This is possible, though terrible, in a confederacy where one member is stronger than all the rest put together, and several are too small to think of resisting. But it would be completely impossible in a confederacy where several members are equal to each other in strength and resources, and each is strong enough by itself to put up a determined defense.

:::commentary
This is the free-rider problem Hamilton described in [No. 15](/papers/15/). Some provinces paid while others didn't, and the paying provinces had to chase down the rest.

Holland was by far the richest province and paid the largest share of the union's costs. That gave it the power to force smaller provinces to pay. Madison's point is that the United States had no "Holland." Virginia, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, and New York were roughly comparable in strength. Trying to force any of them to pay would mean real civil war, as Hamilton argued in [No. 16](/papers/16/).
:::

## Foreign meddling, and powers seized in a crisis {orig=17-18}

Sir William Temple, who was himself a foreign diplomat in the Netherlands, says that foreign ministers get around matters sent back to the provinces for approval by tampering with the provinces and cities. In 1726, the Treaty of Hanover was delayed a whole year in this way. Examples like this are many and well known.

In serious emergencies, the States-General is often forced to jump over its constitutional limits. In 1688, they made a treaty on their own, at the risk of their heads. The Treaty of Westphalia in 1648, which formally and finally recognized their independence, was made without the consent of Zeeland. Even as recently as the last peace treaty with Great Britain, they abandoned the constitutional rule of unanimity.

A weak constitution must end in one of two ways. Either it falls apart because it lacks the powers it needs, or it seizes the powers that public safety requires. Once such seizures begin, will they stop at a healthy point, or go on to a dangerous extreme? That depends on the accidents of the moment. Tyranny has perhaps grown more often out of powers seized in emergencies, because a defective constitution demanded them, than out of the full use of the largest powers a constitution grants.

:::quote
Tyranny has perhaps oftener grown out of the assumptions of power, called for, on pressing exigencies, by a defective constitution, than out of the full exercise of the largest constitutional authorities.

Meaning: A government that is too weak will end up grabbing power in a crisis, and that grabbed power is more dangerous than strong powers granted openly by law.
:::

:::vocab
Ad referendum: Latin for "for reference." A matter taken *ad referendum* had to be sent back to the provinces for approval before it could be decided.
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Exigency: An urgent need or emergency.
Sir William Temple: An English diplomat who served in the Netherlands in the 1600s and wrote a well-known book describing the Dutch Republic.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the most important ideas in the three history essays. Anti-Federalists argued that giving the national government strong powers would invite tyranny. Madison turns the argument around. A government that is *too weak* will be forced to break its own rules whenever a crisis comes. Once leaders get used to breaking the rules, there is no telling where they will stop. It is safer, he suggests, to give a government enough power openly and then limit it with clear rules.

Madison's examples are brief. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended the Dutch war of independence against Spain. The "last treaty of peace with Great Britain" ended the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–1784), which began partly because of Dutch trade with the American rebels. The year 1688 is when the stadtholder William of Orange invaded England and became King William III. But Madison doesn't say which treaty he means.
:::

## What has held the Dutch together {orig=19-20}

Even with all the harm the office of stadtholder has caused, people have believed that, without his influence in the provinces, the causes of disorder in the confederacy would have broken it up long ago.

"Under such a government," says the French writer Abbé Mably, "the Union could never have survived if the provinces had not had a spring within themselves that could speed up their slowness and force them to think alike. This spring is the stadtholder." Sir William Temple remarks that "in the intermissions of the stadtholdership, Holland, by her riches and her authority, which drew the others into a sort of dependence, supplied the place." In other words, when there was no stadtholder, Holland's wealth and power took his place.

These are not the only things that have held back the slide toward disorder and breakup. The surrounding powers make some degree of union absolutely necessary. But at the same time, their plots feed the flaws in the constitution that keep the republic always somewhat at their mercy.

:::commentary
Madison is describing a union held together by things *outside* its constitution: a powerful prince, one dominant province, and fear of its neighbors. Each of these is risky. A prince can become a tyrant. A dominant province can bully the rest. And neighbors who keep you weak can turn on you. The Constitution's answer was to build the needed strength into the government itself, in the form of a president, a Congress, and courts that act directly on citizens.
:::

## Failed attempts at reform {orig=21-22}

True Dutch patriots have long mourned the deadly effects of these flaws. They have made no fewer than four formal attempts to fix them through EXTRAORDINARY ASSEMBLIES called for that special purpose. Each time, their admirable efforts found it impossible to UNITE THE PUBLIC COUNCILS in reforming the known, admitted, and deadly evils of the existing constitution.

Let us pause, my fellow citizens, for one moment over this sad and warning lesson of history. As we shed a tear for the disasters that people's opposing opinions and selfish passions have brought on humanity, let us also offer a prayer of thanks to Heaven for the fortunate harmony that has marked the meetings held for our political happiness.

They also proposed a general tax to be collected by the federal government. This plan, too, had its opponents and failed.

:::commentary
Here Publius praises the Constitutional Convention by contrast. The Dutch had tried four times to reform and failed every time. The Americans had met once and agreed on a plan.

That "propitious concord" was real but not complete. The Convention was full of sharp disagreements, and several delegates refused to sign the final document, including Edmund Randolph and George Mason of Virginia and Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts. Publius is emphasizing the success to persuade his readers.
:::

## A nation in crisis {orig=23}

These unhappy people now seem to be facing the crisis of their fate. They suffer from popular uprisings, from conflict among the provinces, and from an actual invasion by foreign armies. All nations are watching this terrible scene.

Our first wish, as fellow human beings, is that this hard trial will end in a change of government that strengthens their union and makes it the source of peace, freedom, and happiness. Our next wish is that, if it ends in disaster for them, they may find shelter and comfort in this country, where we trust these blessings will soon be secured.

:::commentary
The "invasion by foreign armies" was the Prussian invasion of September 1787. The Patriot movement had tried to limit the stadtholder's power, and Prussia's king sent troops to restore his brother-in-law, William V. Many Patriots fled, mostly to France.

Publius's hope for a peaceful reform did not come true. In 1795, French revolutionary armies overran the country and ended the old Dutch Republic. William V fled to England. The Netherlands later became a kingdom in 1815.
:::

## The lesson of experience {orig=24}

I make no apology for spending so long on these examples of federal governments. Experience is the oracle of truth. Where its answers are clear, they should be treated as final and sacred.

In this case, experience clearly teaches one important truth. A sovereignty over sovereigns, a government over governments, a system of laws for communities instead of individuals, is nonsense in theory. And in practice it destroys the order and purposes of civil government. It does this by putting VIOLENCE in place of LAW, or the destructive COERCION of the SWORD in place of the mild and helpful COERCION of the MAGISTRACY, the courts and officers of justice.

:::quote
Experience is the oracle of truth; and where its responses are unequivocal, they ought to be conclusive and sacred.

Meaning: History is our best guide. When it teaches a lesson clearly, we should accept it.
:::

:::quote
...a sovereignty over sovereigns, a government over governments, a legislation for communities, as contradistinguished from individuals, as it is a solecism in theory, so in practice it is subversive of the order and ends of civil polity, by substituting VIOLENCE in place of LAW, or the destructive COERCION of the SWORD in place of the mild and salutary COERCION of the MAGISTRACY.

Meaning: A union that governs states instead of people can only enforce its laws by war. A real government enforces its laws on individuals through courts.
:::

:::vocab
Solecism: A mistake or contradiction; something that doesn't make sense.
Coercion: Forcing someone to do something, by threat or by force.
Magistracy: Courts, judges, and officials who enforce the law.
Civil polity: A society's system of government.
:::

:::commentary
This final paragraph ties the three history essays back to Hamilton's argument in [No. 15](/papers/15/) and [No. 16](/papers/16/). Greece, Germany, Poland, Switzerland, and the Netherlands all made the same mistake as the Articles of Confederation. Their central governments acted on member states, not on people. So whenever a member refused to cooperate, the only options were to give up or to send an army.

Madison's history is persuasive, but it is also selective. He chose examples that fit his argument, and he relied on the history books of his time. Critics could point out that these confederacies lasted for long periods, and that their problems had many causes besides their structure. Still, the core lesson, that a government needs a way to enforce its laws without war, became the foundation of the Constitution.
:::

:::today
Madison's warning that weak governments grab power in emergencies still comes up in debates today. Governments around the world use emergency powers during wars, disasters, and health crises. Some people argue that clear, strong powers written into law are safer than improvised ones, as Madison suggests. Others worry that giving any government strong powers in advance makes abuse more likely. Both sides agree on one thing Madison stressed: what a government does in a crisis can shape how much power it keeps afterward.
:::
