:::summary
Madison continues his case for the Senate. A small, stable body with long terms will care about the nation's reputation abroad, which a large, changing assembly can't do. It can also be held responsible for long-term goals that take years to achieve. Most famously, he argues that a Senate can protect the people "against their own temporary errors and delusions," slowing things down until calmer judgment returns. He then answers the charge that a six-year Senate will turn into a tyrannical aristocracy. To succeed, the Senate would have to corrupt the state legislatures, the House, and the people themselves. Experience in Maryland, Britain, and the ancient world shows that the branch closest to the people usually wins.
:::

:::keypoints
- A nation needs a body that cares about its **reputation** with other countries. A large assembly that changes often can't feel that responsibility.
- Short terms can actually weaken **responsibility**. Long-term policies take years, so a body elected for one year can't fairly be judged on the results.
- A Senate can be "a defense to the people against their own **temporary errors and delusions**" until "reason, justice, and truth" regain control.
- A large country protects against some dangers, but it may also leave people misled by false reports for a longer time.
- History shows **no long-lived republic without a senate**.
- The ancients did know about representation. America's real difference is that the people as a whole take **no direct part** in governing, plus the country's large size.
- To become a tyranny, the Senate would have to corrupt itself, the state legislatures, the House, and finally the people.
- Maryland's senate, Britain's House of Lords, and ancient senates all show that the **popular branch** tends to win power struggles.
:::

:::context Background
This is Madison's second essay on the Senate, picking up right where [No. 62](/papers/62/) ended. There he listed four reasons a republic needs a senate: to double the checks against corrupt lawmakers, to cool sudden passions, to supply knowledge, and to provide stable laws. Here he adds a fifth and sixth reason, and then answers critics.

Opponents of the Constitution, often called **Anti-Federalists**, feared the Senate more than almost any other part of the plan. Senators would not be elected by the people. They would serve six years, and they would share in treaty-making and appointments with the president. To many critics, this looked like the beginnings of an American House of Lords. Madison's job in this paper is to show that a Senate is a safeguard for liberty, not a threat to it.
:::

## Caring about the nation's reputation {orig=1-3}

A **fifth** need that shows the usefulness of a senate is a proper sense of national character, meaning concern for the nation's reputation. Without a select and stable part of the government, we would lose the respect of foreign powers through unwise and changing policies, for the reasons I have already given. But there is more. Our national government would also lack sensitivity to the opinion of the world. That sensitivity is needed to *deserve* the world's respect and trust, perhaps as much as it is needed to *win* them.

Paying attention to the judgment of other nations matters to every government for two reasons. First, apart from whether a particular plan is good or bad, it is useful in many ways for other nations to see it as the product of a wise and honorable policy. Second, in doubtful cases, the expected opinion of the impartial world may be the best guide to follow. This is especially true when the government may be bent out of shape by some strong passion or short-term interest. What has America not lost through its poor reputation with foreign nations? And how many errors and foolish acts could it have avoided, if it had first tested each measure by asking how it would look to fair-minded people elsewhere?

But however necessary a sense of national character may be, a large and changing body can never have enough of it. It can only be found in a group small enough that each member feels a real share of the praise or blame for public measures. Or it can be found in a body that holds the public trust for so long that its members' pride and standing become tied to the reputation and success of the community. Rhode Island elects its representatives every six months. They would probably not have been moved, when they debated that state's unjust measures, by arguments about how those measures would look to foreign nations, or even to the other states. Yet there can be little doubt of this: if the approval of a select and stable body had been required, concern for the state's reputation alone would have prevented the disasters that misguided people now suffer.

:::vocab
National character: A nation's reputation and standing in the eyes of other countries.
:::

:::commentary
Rhode Island was everyone's favorite bad example in 1787 and 1788. In 1786 its legislature printed large amounts of paper money and tried to force creditors to accept it at full value, even as it quickly lost worth. Merchants closed their shops, and people who had lent money felt robbed. Rhode Island also refused to send any delegates to the Constitutional Convention. It was the last of the thirteen states to ratify the Constitution, in 1790.

Madison's point is about incentives. A member who serves six months and is one of many will not feel personally blamed when the state looks foolish to the world. A senator who serves six years in a small body will.
:::

## Short terms can weaken responsibility {orig=4-6}

I add a **sixth** defect: in some important cases, the government lacks proper responsibility to the people, and the cause is the same frequent elections that create responsibility in other cases. This remark may seem not only new but contradictory. Yet once it is explained, it must be admitted to be as true as it is important.

To be reasonable, responsibility must be limited to things the responsible party actually has power over. To be effective, it must relate to actions that the voters can quickly and properly judge. The goals of government fall into two general types. Some depend on single measures that have an immediate and visible effect. Others depend on a series of well-chosen and well-connected measures that work gradually, perhaps without anyone noticing.

The importance of the second type to the lasting welfare of every country needs no explanation. Yet consider an assembly elected for a term so short that it can only supply one or two links in a long chain of measures on which the general welfare may depend. It should not be held answerable for the final result. A steward or tenant hired for one year could not fairly be blamed for improvements to a farm that could not be finished in less than six years. Nor can the people measure how much *share* of the influence each of their yearly assemblies had on events that came from the mixed actions of several years. It is hard enough to keep the members of a *large* body personally responsible even for acts that have an immediate, separate, and obvious effect on their voters.

The proper remedy for this defect must be an additional body in the legislature. It must last long enough to look after goals that need continued attention and a series of measures. Then it can be fairly and effectively held responsible for achieving them.

:::vocab
Responsibility: Being answerable for one's actions, so that one can be blamed, voted out, or punished.
Steward: A person hired to manage someone else's land or property.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Madison's cleverest arguments. Most people assume that more frequent elections always mean more accountability. Madison says that is only true for quick, visible decisions. For long projects, like paying off a national debt, building a navy, or carrying out a foreign policy over many years, no one-year assembly can fairly take the credit or the blame. A body with six-year terms can.

Notice that this argument depends on the Senate having long terms, not on how senators are chosen. It still applies today, even though senators have been elected by the people since the 17th Amendment (1913).
:::

## Protecting the people from their own passing mistakes {orig=7}

So far I have looked at the reasons for a well-built Senate only as they relate to the people's representatives. But I am speaking to a people who are not easily blinded by prejudice or spoiled by flattery. So I will not hesitate to add that such a body may sometimes be needed to defend the people against their own temporary errors and delusions.

In all governments, the cool and careful judgment of the community ought to win out in the end over the views of its rulers. In all free governments, it actually will. But there are particular moments in public affairs when the people may call for measures that they themselves will later be the first to regret and condemn. They may be stirred up by some wild passion, or by the hope of some improper advantage. Or they may be misled by the clever lies of people with selfish interests.

In these critical moments, how helpful it will be to have a calm and respected body of citizens step in! It can check the misguided course and hold back the blow the people are about to strike against themselves, until reason, justice, and truth can regain their authority over the public mind. How much bitter suffering might the people of Athens have avoided, if their government had included such a wise safeguard against the tyranny of their own passions? Popular liberty might then have escaped the lasting shame of giving the same citizens poison one day and statues the next.

:::quote
...such an institution may be sometimes necessary as a defense to the people against their own temporary errors and delusions.

Meaning: Sometimes the people themselves get carried away, and a Senate can protect them from decisions they will later regret.
:::

:::quote
...to suspend the blow meditated by the people against themselves, until reason, justice, and truth can regain their authority over the public mind...

Meaning: The Senate's job in these moments isn't to overrule the people forever. It is to delay a hasty decision until people calm down and think clearly.
:::

:::commentary
This is the most famous and most debated passage in the paper. Supporters see it as wise: everyone, including whole nations, can make rash choices in moments of anger or fear, and a "cooling-off" period protects us from ourselves. Critics see it as distrust of democracy, since it lets a small group decide when the majority is "wrong." Notice how carefully Madison frames it. He says the people's "cool and deliberate sense" *ought to* and *will* win in the end. The Senate only buys time.

"Hemlock" was the poison the Athenians used for executions. Madison is pointing to the philosopher Socrates, whom an Athenian jury sentenced to drink hemlock in 399 B.C., and to the way Athens sometimes honored and condemned the same people. Another famous Athenian example of a "temporary error" comes from the historian Thucydides. In 427 B.C., the Athenian assembly voted to kill all the men of the rebellious city of Mytilene. The next day, many regretted it. A second vote reversed the order, and a second ship raced to arrive just in time.
:::

## Doesn't a large country already protect us? {orig=8}

Someone may say that a people spread over a large region cannot catch violent passions, or unite in pursuit of unjust measures, the way people crowded into a small district can. I am far from denying that this difference is very important. In fact, in an earlier paper I tried to show that it is one of the main advantages of a federal republic.

At the same time, this advantage should not be seen as replacing the need for extra precautions. In fact, the same large size that will protect Americans from some dangers of smaller republics will expose them to another problem. They may stay longer under the influence of false reports that selfish people, working together, manage to spread among them.

:::connection
The "former paper" is [No. 10](/papers/10/), where Madison argued that a large republic makes it hard for a majority faction to form and act together. He also calls for "auxiliary precautions," or extra safeguards, in [No. 51](/papers/51/).
:::

:::commentary
In 1788, news traveled by horse, ship, and newspaper. A false rumor could spread across a large country, and it might take weeks for the truth to catch up. Madison's worry sounds even more modern today, when misinformation can spread instantly. But today's corrections can also travel instantly, so it's worth debating whether his point is stronger or weaker now.
:::

## Every long-lived republic had a senate {orig=9}

It adds real weight to all these points to remember that history tells of no long-lived republic that did not have a senate. Sparta, Rome, and Carthage are in fact the only states that can be called long-lived. The first two each had a senate for life. Less is known about how Carthage's senate was set up. Indirect evidence makes it likely that it was the same in this way as the other two. At the least, it is certain that it had some quality that made it an anchor against the swings of popular opinion. It is also certain that a smaller council, drawn from the senate, served for life and filled its own empty seats.

These examples are unfit for America to copy, and they go against the spirit of our people. Yet compared with the short and stormy lives of other ancient republics, they are very instructive proofs that we need some institution that blends stability with liberty.

I am aware of the differences between the American government and other popular governments, ancient and modern. These differences mean we must be extremely careful when we reason from one case to the other. But even after giving them due weight, there are still many similarities that make these examples worth our attention.

As we have seen, many of the defects that only a senate can supply are shared by a large assembly elected often by the people and by the people themselves. Other defects belong only to the assembly, and they need the control of a senate. The people can never knowingly betray their own interests. But they may be betrayed by their representatives. And that danger is clearly greater when all lawmaking power is placed in one body of men than when every public act requires the agreement of separate and different bodies.

:::vocab
Sparta: A powerful city-state in ancient Greece, famous for its military.
Carthage: An ancient city in North Africa that became a great trading power and fought three wars with Rome.
:::

:::commentary
Madison is careful here. He doesn't want America to copy senates for life, which would clash with American ideals. He only uses them to show that lasting republics had some steady body to balance popular swings.

Sparta's council of elders, the Gerousia, was made up of men over sixty who served for life, along with Sparta's two kings. Rome's Senate was made up of former officials who generally served for life. Madison's information about Carthage was thinner, as he admits, because most of what we know about Carthage was written by its enemies, the Greeks and Romans.
:::

## Did the ancients know about representation? {orig=10-14}

The difference most often stressed between America and other republics is the principle of representation. It is the center around which the American governments turn, and it is supposed to have been unknown to other republics, or at least to the ancient ones. The way I have used this difference in earlier papers shows that I have no wish to deny it or play down its importance. So I feel freer to point out that the common claim, that ancient governments knew nothing about representation, is not exactly true in the broad sense it is usually given. I won't go into a long discussion here, which would be out of place. I will only point to a few known facts.

In the purest democracies of Greece, many executive duties were carried out not by the people themselves, but by officers elected by the people and REPRESENTING the people in their EXECUTIVE role.

Before the reforms of Solon, Athens was governed by nine Archons, or chief officials, ELECTED EACH YEAR BY THE PEOPLE AS A WHOLE. How much power was given to them is very unclear. After that time, we find an assembly, first of four hundred and later of six hundred members, ELECTED EACH YEAR BY THE PEOPLE. It PARTLY represented them in their LAWMAKING role. These members not only joined with the people in making laws, but also had the sole right to propose laws to the people. The senate of Carthage also seems to have been ELECTED by the votes of the people, whatever its power or the length of its terms. Similar examples could be found in most, if not all, of the popular governments of the ancient world.

Finally, in Sparta we find the Ephors, and in Rome the Tribunes. These were two bodies, small in number, but ELECTED EACH YEAR BY THE WHOLE BODY OF THE PEOPLE. They were considered the REPRESENTATIVES of the people, with almost FULL POWER to act for them. The Cosmi of Crete were also ELECTED EACH YEAR BY THE PEOPLE. Some writers consider them similar to the bodies in Sparta and Rome. The only difference is that in electing them, only part of the people had the right to vote.

From these facts, and many others could be added, it is clear that the principle of representation was neither unknown to the ancients nor completely ignored in their constitutions. The true difference between those governments and the American ones lies here: in America, THE PEOPLE AS A COLLECTIVE BODY ARE TOTALLY EXCLUDED from any share in governing. In the ancient republics, by contrast, the REPRESENTATIVES of the people were not totally excluded from the government. The difference is not that the ancients had no representatives at all.

With this qualification, the difference still leaves a very great advantage on the side of the United States. But to give this advantage its full effect, we must be careful not to separate it from our other advantage, a large territory. It is hard to believe that any form of representative government could have succeeded within the narrow borders occupied by the democracies of Greece.

:::quote
The true distinction between these and the American governments, lies IN THE TOTAL EXCLUSION OF THE PEOPLE, IN THEIR COLLECTIVE CAPACITY, from any share in the LATTER...

Meaning: Ancient republics had some elected officials, but the people also gathered to govern directly. In America, the people govern only through their representatives, never as one gathered assembly.
:::

:::vocab
Archon: One of the chief officials of ancient Athens.
Ephors: Five officials elected each year in ancient Sparta who held great power, even over the kings.
Tribunes: Officials in ancient Rome elected to protect the common people, who could block actions of other officials.
:::

:::commentary
Madison is being precise, and slightly against the grain. Many writers of his time said that representation was a modern invention. Madison says no: the ancients used elected officials, too. What's new in America is that the people never govern directly as a single assembly. Everything goes through elected representatives, and the country is large.

Madison relied on the histories of his day, and modern historians would question some details. For example, scholars today doubt that the early Athenian archons were chosen by all the people. Athens's famous council is also usually described as the Council of Five Hundred. But Madison's larger point, that ancient republics used elected officials, holds up.
:::

## The fear of a Senate aristocracy {orig=15-17}

In answer to all these arguments, which come from reason, are shown by examples, and are confirmed by our own experience, the suspicious opponent of the Constitution will probably just keep repeating the same thing. He will say that a senate not chosen directly by the people, and serving six years, must slowly gain a dangerous dominance in the government, and finally turn it into a tyrannical aristocracy.

To this general answer, a general reply should be enough. Liberty can be endangered by the abuse of liberty as well as by the abuse of power. There are many examples of both. And the abuse of liberty, more than the abuse of power, seems to be the greater danger for the United States. But a more specific reply can also be given.

Before such a revolution could happen, the Senate would have to do four things:

- First, corrupt itself.
- Next, corrupt the state legislatures.
- Then, corrupt the House of Representatives.
- Finally, corrupt the people at large.

Clearly, the Senate must first be corrupted before it can try to set up a tyranny. Without corrupting the state legislatures, it could not continue the attempt, because the regular change of members would otherwise renew the whole body. Without using corruption with equal success on the House of Representatives, the opposition of that equal branch of the government would surely defeat the attempt. And without corrupting the people themselves, a series of new representatives would soon restore everything to its original order. Can anyone seriously believe that the proposed Senate could reach the goal of lawless ambition through all these obstacles, by any means within human skill?

:::quote
...liberty may be endangered by the abuses of liberty as well as by the abuses of power...

Meaning: Freedom can be lost not only when rulers grab too much power, but also when the people misuse their freedom, for example through mob rule or unjust laws.
:::

:::vocab
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged class, usually nobles.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's "chain of corruption" argument depends on how senators were chosen in 1788: by state legislatures, which were elected by the people. A Senate plotting tyranny would need friendly legislatures to keep reappointing its members. Since one-third of the Senate is chosen every two years, its membership would keep changing.

Since the **17th Amendment** (1913), voters choose senators directly, so the state legislatures are no longer a link in the chain. But Madison's basic point still applies. To get anything done, the Senate needs the House, and its members still face the voters.
:::

## The example of Maryland {orig=18}

If reason rejects this suspicion, so does experience. The constitution of Maryland gives the most fitting example. The senate of that state is elected indirectly by the people, as the federal Senate will be. Its term is only one year shorter than the federal Senate's. It also has the unusual power of filling its own empty seats during its term. And it is not subject to any rotation like the one planned for the federal Senate. There are other smaller differences that would give critics of Maryland's senate believable objections that do not apply to the federal Senate.

So if the federal Senate really held the danger that has been so loudly announced, Maryland's senate should by now have shown at least some signs of a similar danger. But no such signs have appeared. On the contrary, the fears first felt by men of the same kind as those who now dread the matching part of the federal Constitution have gradually died out as the experiment went on. And the Maryland constitution is daily earning a reputation from this part of it, a reputation that probably no other state's constitution will match.

:::commentary
Under Maryland's 1776 constitution, voters in each county chose electors, and those electors chose the state's senators for five-year terms. The whole senate was chosen at once, which is why Madison notes it had no "rotation." The federal Senate, by contrast, replaces one-third of its members every two years.

Maryland's senate was a strong example for Madison because, during the paper-money debates of the 1780s, it blocked a paper-money bill that the more popular lower house had passed. To supporters of the Constitution, this showed a senate protecting the state from a rash measure.
:::

## Britain and the ancient world {orig=19-21}

But if anything could silence the fears on this subject, it should be the British example. There, instead of being elected for six years and open to people of any family or wealth, the Senate is a hereditary assembly of rich nobles. The House of Representatives, instead of being elected for two years by the whole body of the people, is elected for seven years, and largely by a very small share of the people. Here, surely, we ought to see on full display the aristocratic power grabs and tyranny that are supposed to come someday to the United States.

Unfortunately for the Anti-Federalist argument, however, British history tells us something different. This hereditary assembly has not been able to defend itself against the constant advances of the House of Representatives. And as soon as it lost the support of the king, it was actually crushed by the weight of the popular branch.

As far as ancient history can teach us on this subject, its examples support our reasoning. In Sparta, the Ephors, the yearly representatives of the people, proved stronger than the senate for life. They kept gaining on its authority, and finally drew all power into their own hands. The Tribunes of Rome, who were the representatives of the people, won almost every contest with the senate for life, as is well known. In the end they gained the most complete victory over it. This fact is even more remarkable because every act of the Tribunes required all of them to agree, even after their number was increased to ten. It proves the irresistible strength of the branch of a free government that has the people on its side. To these examples we can add Carthage. According to the Greek historian Polybius, its senate did not draw all power into itself. Instead, by the start of the Second Punic War, it had lost almost all of its original share.

Taken together, these facts are conclusive evidence that the federal Senate will never be able to turn itself, by gradual power grabs, into an independent and aristocratic body. And we have good reason to believe something more. Suppose such a revolution ever did happen, from causes that human foresight cannot guard against. Even then, the House of Representatives, with the people on its side, will always be able to bring the Constitution back to its original form and principles. Against the strength of the people's direct representatives, nothing will be able to keep even the Senate's lawful authority, except a display of wise policy and devotion to the public good. Only that would let the Senate share with the House the affection and support of the whole body of the people.

:::vocab
House of Lords: The upper house of the British Parliament, made up mostly of nobles and bishops in the 1700s. It also served as Britain's highest court until 2009.
Hereditary: Passed down within a family. Hereditary nobles inherit their power instead of being chosen.
Punic Wars: Three wars between Rome and Carthage. Rome won all three and destroyed Carthage in 146 B.C.
:::

:::commentary
When Madison says the British "Senate" and "House of Representatives," he means the House of Lords and the House of Commons. In 1788, members of the Commons could serve up to seven years between elections, and only a small share of men could vote. His claim that the Lords "was actually crushed" refers to the English Civil War. After King Charles I was executed in 1649, the House of Commons abolished the House of Lords. The Lords returned when the monarchy was restored in 1660.

Madison's prediction about Britain proved right in the long run. Over the following centuries, the elected House of Commons gained power at the Lords' expense. The Parliament Act of 1911 took away the Lords' power to veto most laws. In America, too, the fear of a Senate aristocracy never came true in the way critics expected. But critics today raise a different concern: that the Senate gives small states outsized power, and that its rules, like the filibuster, let a minority block action.
:::

:::today
Madison's idea of the Senate as a "cooling" body still shapes American debates. When the Senate slows down a bill that passed the House, supporters often say it is doing exactly what Madison intended: giving the public time to think. Critics respond that the modern Senate often does more than delay. They say it can block measures that most Americans support for years, which goes beyond the temporary pause Madison described. His deeper question remains open: how can a democracy guard against its own rash moments without letting a small group overrule the people's settled will?
:::
