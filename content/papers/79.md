:::summary
In this short essay, Hamilton looks at two more protections for judges: their pay and the rules for holding them accountable. The Constitution says Congress can never cut a federal judge's salary while that judge is in office. Hamilton explains why: power over a person's paycheck is power over that person's will. Congress may still raise judges' pay as prices change, since judges serve for life. Judges who misbehave can be impeached and removed. But Hamilton opposes removing judges for "inability," because no one can fairly measure a mind. He also criticizes New York's rule forcing judges to retire at age 60.
:::

:::keypoints
- After lifetime tenure, a **fixed salary** is the most important protection for judges' independence.
- **"A power over a man's subsistence amounts to a power over his will."** Judges who depend on lawmakers for their pay are not truly independent.
- Congress **can raise** judges' pay but **can never lower** it while they serve. A fixed dollar amount in the Constitution would not work, because the value of money changes.
- The president's pay can be neither raised nor lowered during a term. Judges are treated differently because they serve much longer.
- Judges can be **impeached** by the House and **tried** by the Senate for misconduct. This is the only kind of accountability that fits with their independence.
- A rule for removing judges for **mental inability** would be either ignored or abused, since there's no reliable way to measure the mind.
- New York's rule forcing judges to retire at **age 60** is a bad idea. Most minds stay sharp well past that age.
:::

:::context Background
This is the second of Hamilton's six essays on the federal courts (Nos. 78–83). In [No. 78](/papers/78/), he argued that judges should serve "during good behavior," which in practice means for life. Now he turns to the other parts of judges' terms of office that he listed there: how they are paid and how they can be held responsible.

The essay answers two kinds of critics. Some Anti-Federalists said judges would be too independent, with no way to remove a judge who became unfit. Others worried that Congress could control judges through their salaries. Hamilton argues the Constitution strikes the right balance.
:::

## Power over pay is power over the will {orig=1}

Next to permanent tenure, nothing can do more for the independence of judges than a fixed provision for their pay. The point I made about the President applies here too. In the ordinary course of human nature, a power over a man's living amounts to a power over his will.

We can never hope to see the judicial power fully separated from the legislative power in practice under any system that leaves judges dependent on whatever money the legislature chooses to grant from time to time. Wise friends of good government in every state have regretted that the state constitutions lack clear and exact safeguards on this point.

It's true that some state constitutions have declared that judges should receive *permanent* salaries. But experience has shown, in some cases, that such words aren't precise enough to stop legislatures from getting around them. Something even firmer and clearer has proven necessary. So the Convention's plan says that the judges of the United States "shall at stated times receive for their services a compensation which shall not be diminished during their continuance in office."

:::quote
In the general course of human nature, a power over a man's subsistence amounts to a power over his will.

Meaning: If someone controls your paycheck, they can control what you do. A judge whose salary could be cut by Congress would always be tempted to please Congress.
:::

:::vocab
Subsistence: The money or means a person needs to live.
Compensation: Payment for work; salary.
Pecuniary: Having to do with money.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton made the same point about the president in [No. 73](/papers/73/), and Madison made it about all three branches in [No. 51](/papers/51/). Americans had seen this danger firsthand. The Declaration of Independence complained that King George III had "made Judges dependent on his Will alone, for the tenure of their offices, and the amount and payment of their salaries."

Hamilton's footnote points to the Massachusetts constitution of 1780, which promised judges "permanent and honorable salaries." His complaint is that a promise like "permanent" is too vague. A legislature can say it is keeping a salary "permanent" while letting it shrink, or argue about what the word means. The federal Constitution uses a simple, clear rule instead: the pay can't be "diminished."
:::

## Why pay can go up but not down {orig=2-3}

All things considered, this is the best rule that could have been designed. It's easy to see why the Constitution couldn't set a fixed salary amount. The value of money and the state of society keep changing. A salary that seems too generous today might, in fifty years, be too small to live on. So the legislature had to be left free to adjust salaries as circumstances change. But it also had to be restricted, so that it could never make a particular judge's situation worse. That way, a judge can be sure where he stands. He can never be scared away from his duty by the fear of being put in a worse position.

The clause I quoted gives both advantages. Judges' salaries may be changed from time to time, as needed. But a particular judge's pay can never drop below the amount he was earning when he took office.

Notice that the Convention treated the President's pay differently from the judges' pay. The President's pay can be neither raised nor lowered. The judges' pay simply can't be lowered. This difference probably comes from how long each office lasts. The President is elected for only four years at a time, so a fair salary set at the start of his term will almost always still be fair at the end. But judges who behave properly will keep their jobs for life. Especially in the early years of the government, a salary that was quite enough when a judge was appointed might become too small over the course of his service.

This rule for supporting the judges shows every sign of wisdom and good results. Combined with their permanent tenure, I can safely say it promises more independence for judges than any state constitution gives its own judges.

:::commentary
The two pay rules are in different parts of the Constitution. Article II says the president's pay "shall neither be increased nor diminished" during his term. That way Congress can neither punish nor reward a sitting president. Article III, the judges' article, only forbids cutting their pay. As Hamilton explains, the difference is about time. A four-year term is short, but a judge might serve for thirty years or more.

Hamilton's worry about inflation turned out to be real. Because Congress controls raises, judges have sometimes gone many years without one, while prices kept rising. In 2006, Chief Justice John Roberts called Congress's failure to raise judicial pay a "constitutional crisis." Critics of his view pointed out that federal judges were still paid far more than most Americans. The Constitution protects judges from pay *cuts*, but not from the slow effects of inflation.
:::

:::vocab
Stipend: A regular, fixed payment or salary.
Inflation: A general rise in prices over time, which means the same amount of money buys less. (Hamilton describes this as "fluctuations in the value of money.")
:::

## Holding judges accountable through impeachment {orig=4}

The safeguards for holding judges responsible are found in the section on impeachments. Judges can be impeached for misconduct by the House of Representatives and tried by the Senate. If they are convicted, they can be removed from office and barred from holding any other office.

This is the only rule on this subject that fits with the independence judges need. It is also the only one our own New York constitution has for our own judges.

:::vocab
Impeachment: The process of formally charging a government official with serious misconduct. If convicted, the official is removed from office.
Malconduct: Wrong or improper behavior in office. Today we would say "misconduct."
:::

:::commentary
Under the Constitution, the House impeaches, which is like bringing formal charges. Then the Senate holds a trial, and a two-thirds vote is needed to convict. In practice, this has been used rarely. Only a small number of federal judges have ever been removed this way, mostly for crimes like bribery or lying under oath.

No Supreme Court justice has ever been removed. Justice Samuel Chase was impeached by the House in 1804, mainly because his rulings and speeches angered President Jefferson's party. The Senate acquitted him in 1805. Many historians see that acquittal as an important moment. It suggested that judges could not be impeached simply for their political views or unpopular decisions, which is what Hamilton hoped.
:::

## Why judges shouldn't be removed for "inability" {orig=5}

Some people have complained that the Constitution has no provision for removing judges who become unable to do their jobs. But every thoughtful person will see that such a rule would either never be used or would be more likely to be abused than to do any good.

As far as I know, measuring the powers of the mind is not one of the known sciences. Any attempt to draw a line between ability and inability would much more often give room to personal and party friendships and hatreds than it would serve justice or the public good. Except in cases of insanity, the result would mostly be arbitrary. And insanity can safely be considered a disqualification in practice, even without any formal rule saying so.

:::commentary
Hamilton's point is that a rule allowing removal for "inability" could become a political weapon. A party that disliked a judge's rulings could claim he had lost his mental powers. Since no one could prove it either way, the decision would come down to politics.

This problem did come up early. In 1804, Judge John Pickering of New Hampshire became the first federal judge removed by impeachment. He was widely believed to be mentally ill and to have a drinking problem, and he could no longer do his job. But the Constitution allows impeachment only for "high crimes and misdemeanors," so his case raised hard questions. Some senators doubted that illness was a proper reason to impeach anyone. The Constitution still has no separate way to remove a judge who becomes disabled. By contrast, the 25th Amendment (1967) created a process for when a president is unable to serve.
:::

:::vocab
Arbitrary: Based on personal choice or whim rather than a fair rule or reason.
:::

## New York's retirement age of sixty {orig=6}

The New York constitution tries to avoid inquiries into ability, which would always be vague and dangerous. Instead, it uses a set age as the test of inability: no one can be a judge past sixty. I believe few people today don't disapprove of this rule.

There's no job for which an age limit is less proper than a judge's. In people who live past sixty, the powers of careful thinking and comparing usually stay strong well beyond that age. Consider also how few people outlive their years of mental strength. And consider how unlikely it is that a large part of the bench, whether it has many judges or few, would be in that condition at the same time. Then we will be ready to conclude that limits like this have little to recommend them.

In a republic, people don't have large fortunes, and government pensions aren't advisable. Some judges have served their country long and well. They depend on their office for a living, and it would be too late for them to start another career. Throwing such men out of office needs a better excuse, for the sake of basic humanity, than the imaginary danger of a bench full of judges too old to do their work.

:::vocab
Superannuated: Too old to work well; past one's prime.
The bench: Judges as a group. The word comes from the seat where judges sit in court.
Pension: Regular payments made to someone after retirement.
:::

:::commentary
New York's first constitution, written in 1777, did require judges to leave office at sixty. Hamilton's criticism had a local target. Today, federal judges still have no retirement age. Many states, however, do require their judges to retire, usually somewhere around age 70 to 75.

The debate continues. Supporters of age limits or term limits say people are living much longer than in 1788, so lifetime appointments now last much longer than the founders expected. Opponents agree with Hamilton that wisdom and experience often grow with age, and that a fixed cutoff is a crude tool. Notice also Hamilton's concern for judges' livelihoods. In 1788 there was no Social Security and few pensions, so forced retirement could mean poverty.
:::

:::today
Debates about judges' independence still turn on the questions in this short essay. People argue over whether federal judges are paid enough, whether there should be age limits or term limits for Supreme Court justices, and what should happen when a judge's health declines. The Constitution's simple pay rule has worked largely as Hamilton hoped: Congress has never cut federal judges' salaries to punish them for their rulings. And impeachment has stayed what Hamilton wanted it to be, a tool for removing judges who commit serious misconduct, not for removing judges whose decisions are unpopular.
:::
