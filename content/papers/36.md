:::summary
Hamilton ends his series on taxes by answering the last fears about internal taxes, meaning taxes collected inside the country rather than at the ports. He first finishes his argument about representation: a House made up mostly of landowners, merchants, and professionals can still represent everyone. Then he takes up the claim that Congress wouldn't know enough about local conditions to tax fairly. Members from each state can supply that knowledge, and Congress can simply use each state's own system for collecting taxes. He answers fears of double sets of tax collectors and double taxation, and admits he dislikes poll taxes. Still, he argues, the national government should keep every tool it might need in an emergency. He closes by saying his review of the government's key powers is finished.
:::

:::keypoints
- Congress will be made up mostly of **landowners, merchants, and the learned professions**, but the door should be **open to all**, and talented people from any background can rise.
- Critics said Congress would lack the **local knowledge** to set internal taxes. Hamilton says members from each state can provide it, as county members do in state legislatures.
- For **indirect taxes** (duties and excises on goods), little local knowledge is needed. For **direct taxes** on land, the details are handled by local assessors anyway.
- Congress can use **each state's own tax system** within that state. And direct taxes must be divided among the states **by population**, which prevents favoritism.
- Having the power to tax directly will make the states more willing to **pay requisitions**, since they'll know the Union can collect without them.
- Fears of **double tax collectors** and **double taxation** are exaggerated. The nation can use state officers, and the people's total tax bill will be the same either way.
- Hamilton **dislikes poll taxes**, but says the government should keep the power to use them in a national emergency.
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of Hamilton's seven essays on the taxing power (Nos. 30–36) and the end of the third main part of the series, on why the Union needs an energetic government. The Anti-Federalists had raised many fears about internal taxes. They warned that federal tax collectors would swarm the countryside alongside state collectors, that people would be taxed twice, and that Congress might impose a hated poll tax, a tax on every person.

These fears were rooted in experience. Before the Revolution, Americans had resisted British tax officials, and many people distrusted any tax collector sent by a distant government.
:::

## A few exceptions don't change the rule {orig=1-2}

We have seen what my last paper showed. Because of the natural workings of the different interests and views of the various classes, the House will be made up almost entirely of landowners, merchants, and members of the learned professions. This will be true whether the House is large or small. And these men will truly represent all those different interests and views.

Someone may object that other kinds of people sit in our state legislatures. I answer that there are exceptions to the rule, I admit, but not enough to shape the general character of the government. There are strong minds in every walk of life that will rise above the disadvantages of their position. They will win the respect their merit deserves, not only from their own class but from society in general. The door should be equally open to all. And I trust, for the credit of human nature, that we will see such vigorous plants flourishing in the soil of national as well as state lawmaking. But occasional cases like these don't weaken reasoning based on the general course of things.

The subject could be looked at in several other ways that would all lead to the same result. For example, one might ask: what closer connection of interests can be imagined between the carpenter and the blacksmith, or the linen maker and the stocking weaver, than between the merchant and any of them? It is well known that different trades and crafts are often as fierce rivals as any other branches of work and business. So unless the House were far larger than would allow for orderly or wise debate, the critics' idea could never work in practice. But I won't spend any more time on an objection that has so far been stated too loosely to allow a careful look at its real shape or purpose.

:::quote
The door ought to be equally open to all; and I trust, for the credit of human nature, that we shall see examples of such vigorous plants flourishing in the soil of federal as well as of State legislation...

Meaning: Anyone, from any background, should be able to win a seat in Congress. Hamilton hopes talented people of humble origins will do so.
:::

:::vocab
Learned professions: Jobs that required advanced education, traditionally law, medicine, and the ministry.
Mechanic: In Hamilton's time, a skilled craftsman or artisan, such as a carpenter, blacksmith, or shoemaker.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's line that "the door ought to be equally open to all" may have had personal meaning for him. He was born in the British West Indies, was orphaned as a boy, and came to New York with little money. He rose through talent and hard work. Still, his "door" was open in 1788 only to men, and in most places only to white men with some property.

His point about rival trades is a clever one. The critics wanted every occupation represented. But carpenters and weavers have no more in common with each other than either has with a merchant. To truly represent every trade, the House would need to be enormous.
:::

## Can Congress know enough about local conditions? {orig=3-5}

There is another objection that is somewhat more precise. Critics claim that Congress could never use a power of internal taxation well. They say it would lack enough knowledge of local conditions, and that national and state tax laws would interfere with each other.

The claim that Congress would lack the needed knowledge seems to have no basis at all. When a state legislature considers a question about one of its counties that requires knowledge of local details, how does it get that knowledge? Surely from the members who come from that county. Can't Congress get the same knowledge from each state's representatives? And shouldn't we expect that the men usually sent there will be smart enough to share that information?

Besides, what does knowledge of local conditions mean when it comes to taxes? Is it a detailed map of every mountain, river, stream, highway, and back road in each state? Or is it a general understanding of the state's situation and resources: the condition of its farming, trade, and manufacturing; what it produces and consumes; and the different amounts and kinds of its wealth, property, and business?

Nations in general, even those with the most popular governments, usually place the management of their finances in the hands of a single person or a small board. These officials first study and prepare tax plans. Then the ruler or the legislature makes them into laws.

Everywhere, curious and well-informed statesmen are considered the best qualified to choose wisely what should be taxed. As far as the judgment of mankind counts on this question, that shows clearly what kind of local knowledge taxation requires.

:::vocab
Internal tax: A tax collected inside the country, such as a tax on land, on property, or on goods made and sold at home.
Topographical: Having to do with the physical features of land, such as hills, rivers, and roads.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton describes nations putting their finances in the hands of "single men or boards," and he soon became exactly that kind of official. When Congress created the Treasury Department in 1789, it asked the Secretary of the Treasury to prepare plans for raising revenue. As the first Secretary, Hamilton wrote a series of famous reports on public credit, a national bank, and manufacturing, which shaped the country's early finances.
:::

## Indirect taxes need little local knowledge {orig=6}

The taxes that fall under the general name of internal taxes can be divided into two kinds, DIRECT and INDIRECT. The critics object to both, but their reasoning seems to apply only to the first kind.

As for indirect taxes, which mean duties and excises on goods people buy and use, it's hard to see what difficulty anyone could fear. The knowledge they require will either be obvious from the nature of the product itself, or can easily be gotten from any well-informed person, especially a merchant. The differences in a product's situation from one state to another must be few, simple, and easy to understand.

The main thing to watch for would be to avoid taxing goods that a particular state already taxes for its own use. And it would be easy to learn each state's tax system. This could always be found in each state's laws, and from the members sent by each state.

:::vocab
Direct tax: A tax charged directly on people or their property, such as a tax on land or a "head tax" on each person.
Indirect tax: A tax built into the price of goods, which people pay without noticing when they buy them.
Excise: A tax on goods made or sold within a country, such as liquor. Collecting it often required inspectors to enter homes and businesses.
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
:::

## Direct taxes on land, and how they are limited {orig=7-9}

When the objection is applied to real estate, meaning houses and land, it seems at first to have more basis. But even here it won't stand up to close examination.

Land taxes are usually set in one of two ways. One is by ACTUAL valuations, done once for all time or repeated from time to time. The other is by OCCASIONAL assessments, made at the discretion, or according to the best judgment, of certain officers whose job it is to make them. Either way, the actual CARRYING OUT of the work, which is the only part that requires knowledge of local details, must be handed to careful people acting as commissioners or assessors. They may be elected by the people or appointed by the government for this purpose.

All the law can do is name these people, or set the method for electing or appointing them. It can fix their numbers and qualifications and sketch the general outline of their powers and duties. What part of this couldn't Congress do as well as a state legislature? Either one can only deal with general principles. As I have said, local details must be left to those who carry out the plan.

But there is a simple way to look at this that should fully settle the matter. Congress can use EACH STATE'S OWN SYSTEM WITHIN THAT STATE. The national government can adopt, in every detail, the method each state uses to set and collect this kind of tax.

Let us also remember that the share of these taxes paid by each state won't be left to Congress's choice. It will be set by each state's population, as described in Article 1, section 2. An actual census, or count, of the people must supply the rule. That effectively shuts the door to favoritism or oppression.

The Constitution guards carefully against abuse of the taxing power. Besides the safeguard just mentioned, it provides that "all duties, imposts, and excises shall be UNIFORM throughout the United States."

:::vocab
Assessor: An official who estimates the value of property so it can be taxed.
Census: An official count of the population. The Constitution requires one every ten years.
Apportionment: Dividing something among the states by a set rule, such as by population.
Uniform: The same everywhere.
:::

:::commentary
The rule Hamilton mentions has a dark side that should be stated plainly. Article 1, section 2 divided both seats in the House and direct taxes among the states by population. But it counted each enslaved person as only **three-fifths** of a free person. This "three-fifths compromise" gave slaveholding states extra seats in Congress, while also lowering their share of any direct tax.

In practice, the national government used direct taxes very rarely. One example came in 1798, when Congress taxed houses, land, and enslaved people to prepare for a possible war with France. Resistance to it in Pennsylvania led to a small uprising known as Fries's Rebellion. The question of what counts as a "direct" tax later became important. In 1895, the Supreme Court ruled that a tax on income from property was a direct tax that had to be divided by population, which made an income tax nearly impossible. The **16th Amendment** (1913) solved this by letting Congress tax incomes without dividing the tax among the states.
:::

## The power will make requisitions work better {orig=10}

Several speakers and writers on the Constitution's side have rightly pointed out something. If experience shows that the Union's use of internal taxation is truly inconvenient, the national government can stop using it and turn to requisitions instead.

In response, critics have asked, as if they had won the argument: Why not leave out that doubtful power from the start, and rely on requisitions? There are two solid answers.

The first is that using the power, if it proves convenient, will be better, because it will work better. And there's no way to prove in theory, or in any way except by trying it, that it can't be used well. In fact, the opposite seems most likely.

The second answer is that having this power in the Constitution will strongly help make requisitions work. When the states know the Union can act on its own without them, they will have a powerful reason to make an effort themselves.

:::vocab
Requisition: A formal request from Congress to the states for money or soldiers.
:::

:::commentary
This is a shrewd point about how power works. A power can be useful even if it's rarely used. Under the Articles, the states could ignore Congress's requests with no consequences. Under the Constitution, a state that refused to pay would know that national tax collectors could come instead. Hamilton suggests that the threat alone would make the states more cooperative. In fact, once the Constitution took effect, the national government stopped using requisitions and raised its money directly.
:::

## Avoiding clashes between the two tax systems {orig=11}

As for national and state tax laws interfering with each other, we have already seen that there can be no conflict of authority between them. So the laws can't interfere with each other in a legal sense. And it is quite possible to avoid interference even in the policies of the two systems.

An effective way to do this will be for each side to stay away from whatever things the other taxed first. Since neither can CONTROL the other, each will have a clear and practical interest in this kind of mutual restraint. And where there is an IMMEDIATE common interest, we can safely count on it to work.

Once the states' own debts are paid off and their expenses shrink to their natural size, the chance of interference will almost disappear. A small land tax will meet the states' needs and will be their simplest and most fitting source of revenue.

## Specters raised to frighten the people {orig=12-14}

Many frightening specters have been raised from this power of internal taxation to alarm the people: double sets of tax officers, double burdens from double taxes, and the terrifying shape of hateful and oppressive poll taxes. All of these have been paraded with all the clever skill of a political magic act.

On the first point, there are two cases where there can be no need for double sets of officers. One is where the right to tax belongs only to the Union, which is true of duties on imports. The other is where the thing being taxed isn't covered by any state rule or tax, which may be true of many things.

In other cases, the United States will probably either stay away entirely from things the states already tax for local purposes, or use the state officers and state rules to collect the extra tax. This will best serve the goal of raising revenue, because it will save on the cost of collection. It will also best avoid giving offense to the state governments and the people. In any case, this is a practical way to avoid the problem. And all I need to show is that the predicted evils won't necessarily result from the plan.

Some argue from a supposed scheme of influence, as if the national government would use tax officers to build up its own power. It's enough to answer that we shouldn't assume such a thing. But a more precise answer is possible. If such a spirit ever infected the national government, the surest way to reach its goal would be to employ state officers as much as possible, and to tie them to the Union by adding to their pay. This would turn the tide of state influence toward the national government, instead of making national influence flow against it. But all guesses of this kind are unfair, and they should be set aside when the people consider the great question before them. They can serve no purpose except to cloud the truth.

:::vocab
Poll tax: A tax of the same amount on every person, regardless of income or property. Also called a "head tax" or "capitation."
Specter: A ghost, or a frightening thought of something that might happen.
Legerdemain: Sleight of hand, like a magician's tricks; clever deception.
Emoluments: Salary, fees, or other payments that come with holding an office.
:::

:::commentary
The fear of swarms of tax officers was real. Excise taxes in particular were collected by inspectors who might visit homes, farms, and workshops, and they had been hated in Britain for that reason. Hamilton's own excise on whiskey, passed in 1791, later sparked the **Whiskey Rebellion** of 1794, when western Pennsylvania farmers attacked federal tax collectors.

Hamilton's idea that the national government would use state officers to collect its taxes did not become the usual practice. The national government built its own collection system instead. In modern times, the Supreme Court has ruled that Congress cannot *force* state officials to carry out federal programs. In *Printz v. United States* (1997), the justices debated what Hamilton meant in passages like this one, as part of deciding that case.
:::

## Not double taxation, and a lighter load on the poor {orig=15}

The answer to the fear of double taxation is plain. The Union's needs must be met one way or another. If the national government meets them with its own taxes, the state governments won't have to raise that money. The total amount of taxes the community pays must be the same either way.

And if the Union does it, there are advantages. First, the most important and convenient source of revenue, duties on imports, can be developed much further under national control than under state control. That will make it less necessary to turn to less convenient methods.

Second, to the extent that internal taxes are truly difficult to use, that difficulty will make the national government more careful in choosing and arranging its methods. It will naturally lead national leaders to make it a fixed policy to tax the luxuries of the rich as far as possible. That way, they can reduce the need for taxes that might anger the poorer and most numerous classes of society. It is a happy thing when a government's interest in keeping its own power matches a fair division of the public burdens, and helps protect the least wealthy part of the community from oppression!

:::quote
Happy it is when the interest which the government has in the preservation of its own power, coincides with a proper distribution of the public burdens, and tends to guard the least wealthy part of the community from oppression!

Meaning: A government that wants to stay popular has a good reason to tax the rich more and the poor less. That's a lucky case where self-interest leads to fairness.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is often remembered as a friend of the wealthy, so this passage may surprise some readers. He argues that the national government, wanting to avoid unrest, will try to tax luxuries rather than necessities. As Treasury Secretary, he did tax some luxury items. A national tax on carriages, passed in 1794, was challenged as an unconstitutional direct tax. Hamilton himself argued in its defense before the Supreme Court, which upheld the tax in *Hylton v. United States* (1796).

How much the wealthy should pay compared with everyone else is still one of the biggest debates in American politics. Some argue that higher taxes on the rich are fair and necessary. Others argue that they discourage investment and hurt economic growth.
:::

## Poll taxes: disliked, but a power worth keeping {orig=16}

As for poll taxes, I freely confess that I disapprove of them. They have been common from early times in the New England states, which have always been the most protective of their rights. Even so, I would be sorry to see them used by the national government.

But does it follow that, because there is a power to lay them, they will actually be laid? Every state in the Union has power to impose taxes of this kind, and yet in several states they are never used. Should the state governments be branded as tyrannies because they have this power? If not, how can the same power fairly justify such a charge against the national government, or be raised as a reason not to adopt it?

As little as I like this kind of tax, I am fully convinced that the national government should have the power to use it. There are certain national emergencies when measures that should be avoided in ordinary times become essential to the public good. Because such emergencies are possible, the government should always have the option of using such measures.

This country has a real shortage of things that can be taxed productively. That is a special reason, in our case, not to limit the judgment of the national government on this point. There may be dangerous and stormy times when a poll tax becomes an invaluable resource. And since I know of nothing that protects this part of the world from the disasters that have struck other parts, I admit that I oppose every plan designed to take away from the government a single weapon that might, in any possible situation, be useful for the common defense and security.

:::note
Hamilton's footnote explains that by the states that have long used poll taxes and "have uniformly been the most tenacious of their rights," he means the New England states.
:::

:::commentary
The poll tax Hamilton discusses here is a tax for raising money: a fixed charge on every person. It was unfair to the poor because a farmhand paid as much as a rich landowner. The Constitution made such taxes hard to use nationally, since a "capitation" tax must be divided among the states by population.

Later, "poll tax" took on a different meaning in American history. After Reconstruction, many Southern states required citizens to pay a poll tax before they could **vote**. These taxes were designed to keep Black citizens, and many poor white citizens, from the polls. The **24th Amendment** (1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections. Two years later, in *Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections* (1966), the Supreme Court struck them down in state elections too. That kind of poll tax was a barrier to voting, not the revenue tax Hamilton had in mind, but both fell hardest on people with the least money.
:::

## The review of the government's energy is complete {orig=17-18}

:::note
Different editions of *The Federalist* end this essay with two different closing paragraphs. Both are adapted below.
:::

I have now examined those powers proposed for the United States that relate most directly to the energy of the government. I have tried to answer the main objections made against them. I have said nothing about the lesser powers, which were either too small to draw the attacks of the Constitution's opponents or too obviously proper to be disputed. The judicial power as a whole might have deserved discussion here. But its organization and its extent can be better considered together. So I have decided to leave it for the next part of our inquiry.

In the other version: I have now examined those powers proposed for the national government that relate most closely to its energy and to its ability to achieve the great and primary goals of the Union. There are other powers that I have left out here. To make the picture more complete, they will be discussed under the next topic of our inquiry. I hope the progress made so far will have convinced the fair and thoughtful part of the community of something. Some of the objections most strongly pressed against the Constitution, which seemed most alarming when they first appeared, have no substance. Worse, if they had shaped the plan, they would have made it unable to achieve the great goals of public happiness and national prosperity. I also hope that a further and closer examination of the system will make it even more appealing to every sincere and unselfish supporter of good government. I hope it will leave such people no doubt that adopting it is right and wise. How happy for ourselves, and how honorable for human nature, if we have the wisdom and virtue to set so glorious an example for mankind!

:::commentary
With this essay, the third main part of *The Federalist* ends. Starting with [No. 37](/papers/37/), Madison begins a new part that examines the structure of the proposed government itself: how the Convention balanced competing goals, how power is divided between the nation and the states, and how the branches check one another. Hamilton later returns to the judicial power he mentions here in [No. 78](/papers/78/) through [No. 83](/papers/83/).
:::

:::today
The fears Hamilton answered here are still part of American debates about taxes. Americans today pay taxes to both the national and state governments, and many complain about the burden and complexity of two tax systems. Supporters of the current system say it lets each level of government pay for its own responsibilities. Critics say it is confusing and costly, and some would like the two systems better coordinated. Debates continue, too, over how much the wealthy should pay. And the history of the poll tax remains a reminder of how a tax can be used not only to raise money, but also to keep people from taking part in their government.
:::
