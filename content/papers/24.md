:::summary
Hamilton takes up the one specific objection he has heard to the national government's military powers: the Constitution doesn't ban **standing armies in time of peace**. He imagines a stranger who reads the angry newspapers and then checks the facts. The stranger finds that the power to raise armies belongs to **Congress**, an elected body, not the president. He finds that Congress can't fund an army for more than **two years** at a time. He finds that only two state constitutions say anything against peacetime armies, and that the Articles of Confederation say nothing at all. Hamilton then argues that a ban would be unwise anyway. America faces Britain and Spain on its borders, needs small forts on the western frontier, and will need protected naval yards.
:::

:::keypoints
- The only specific objection to the military powers is that the Constitution doesn't forbid **peacetime standing armies**.
- The power to raise armies belongs to the **legislature**, elected by the people, not to the **executive**.
- Congress may not set aside money for an army for **more than two years** at a time, a real check on keeping troops without need.
- Only **two state constitutions** (Pennsylvania and North Carolina) warn against peacetime armies. The rest say nothing or allow them with the legislature's consent.
- The **Articles of Confederation** put no limit at all on Congress's power to keep troops.
- Hamilton suggests the opponents are trying to **alarm people's feelings** rather than convince their reason.
- Britain to the north and Spain to the south and west could join against us. Small **frontier garrisons** are needed, and they amount to a small standing army.
- A future **navy** will need dock-yards, which will need forts and troops to protect them.
:::

:::context Background
Fear of standing armies ran deep in America. Many colonists had read English writers who warned that kings used permanent armies to crush liberty. The presence of British troops in Boston before the Revolution, and the Boston Massacre of 1770, made the fear real. The Declaration of Independence even complained that the king had "kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures."

So when the Constitution gave Congress power "to raise and support Armies" with no ban on peacetime forces, Anti-Federalist writers sounded the alarm. Hamilton had promised in [No. 8](/papers/8/) to answer this objection. Here, continuing from [No. 23](/papers/23/), he begins.
:::

## The one objection, and why it is weak {orig=1-2}

On the powers the national government would have to create and direct the nation's armed forces, I have met only one specific objection. If I understand it correctly, it is this: the Constitution does not make proper provision against standing armies in time of peace. I will now try to show that this objection rests on weak foundations.

The objection has been made in the vaguest and most general way, supported only by bold claims with no sign of real argument. It doesn't even have the backing of political theorists. It contradicts the practice of other free nations, as well as the general opinion of America as expressed in most of our state constitutions.

You will see why I say this as soon as you remember what the objection really demands. It asks us to restrict the nation's LEGISLATIVE power over military forces, a principle that is unheard of except in one or two of our state constitutions and has been rejected in all the rest.

:::vocab
Standing army: A permanent, professional army kept even in peacetime.
Legislature: The branch of government that makes laws. In the national government, this is Congress.
Executive: The branch of government that carries out the laws, led by a president, governor, or king.
:::

## What a stranger would expect to find {orig=3-4}

Imagine a stranger to our politics who reads our newspapers right now, without having first looked at the Convention's plan. He would naturally reach one of two conclusions: either the plan *requires* that standing armies be kept in peacetime, or it gives the EXECUTIVE the whole power to raise troops, without any control by the legislature.

Then suppose he went on to read the plan itself. He would be surprised to find that neither is true. The whole power to raise armies is placed in the LEGISLATURE, not the EXECUTIVE, and this legislature is a popular body made up of representatives whom the people elect at regular times.

Instead of a rule in favor of standing armies, he would find an important limit even on the legislature's choices. One clause forbids Congress to set aside money to support an army for any period longer than two years. Looked at closely, this precaution will prove to be a great and real protection against keeping troops without clear need.

:::quote
...a precaution which, upon a nearer view of it, will appear to be a great and real security against the keeping up of troops without evident necessity.

Meaning: The rule that Congress can fund an army for only two years at a time is a strong protection. It keeps the government from holding onto troops it doesn't clearly need.
:::

:::commentary
The clause Hamilton means is in Article I, Section 8, which gives Congress power "To raise and support Armies, but no Appropriation of Money to that Use shall be for a longer Term than two Years." Since a new House is elected every two years, every Congress must decide again whether to pay for the army. Hamilton explains why this matters in [No. 26](/papers/26/).

Notice the contrast Hamilton draws with Britain. There, the king commanded the army. Under the Constitution, the president is commander in chief, but only Congress can create and pay for an army.
:::

:::vocab
Appropriation: A decision by a legislature to set aside money for a specific use.
:::

## Searching the state constitutions {orig=5-6}

Disappointed in his first guess, our stranger would probably look a little further, saying to himself: "All this passionate and emotional speechmaking can't be without some believable reason. This people is so protective of its liberties. Surely, in all the earlier constitutions they have written, they included the most exact and strict precautions on this point. Leaving those out of the new plan must be what caused all this fear and noise."

With this idea in mind, suppose he reviewed the state constitutions. How disappointed he would be! He would find that ONLY TWO of them ban standing armies in time of peace. The other eleven are either completely silent on the subject, or they openly admit the legislature's right to allow such armies.

:::note
Hamilton's footnote explains his count. **Pennsylvania** and **North Carolina** say: "As standing armies in time of peace are dangerous to liberty, THEY OUGHT NOT to be kept up." Hamilton calls this "rather a CAUTION than a PROHIBITION." **New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Delaware, and Maryland** say standing armies "ought not to be raised or kept up WITHOUT THE CONSENT OF THE LEGISLATURE," which admits the legislature's power. **New York** has no bill of rights, and its constitution says nothing on the subject. The other states' constitutions are also silent. Hamilton adds that he has heard one or two states have bills of rights missing from his printed collection, but that these also recognize the legislature's authority.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's count was based on the printed collection he had, and he admits it might be incomplete. One document it seems to have missed is Virginia's Declaration of Rights (1776), which said standing armies in peacetime "should be avoided, as dangerous to liberty." Like Pennsylvania's, though, that was a warning rather than a flat ban. Hamilton's larger point holds: almost no state had actually forbidden its legislature to keep troops.
:::

## Searching the Articles of Confederation {orig=7-8}

Still, the stranger would be sure there must be some reasonable basis for the outcry. As long as any source of information was left unchecked, he could never believe that it was just a test of how much the public would swallow, coming either from a deliberate plan to deceive or from a zeal too wild to be honest.

He might then think of looking in the original agreement between the states. Here, at last, he would expect to solve the puzzle. "No doubt," he would say, "the current Confederation must contain the clearest rules against military forces in peacetime. The new plan has left this favorite model, and that must explain the anger of these political champions."

Now suppose he made a careful, critical study of the Articles of Confederation. His surprise would grow, and it would be mixed with anger. Instead of the ban he was looking for, he would find that the Articles had carefully limited the *state* legislatures on this point. But they had not placed a single limit on the power of the United States.

If he were a man of strong feelings or a hot temper, he could no longer help seeing these outcries as dishonest tricks. He would see them as part of a sinister and unprincipled opposition to a plan that deserves at least a fair and honest review from everyone who truly loves their country. "Why else," he would ask, "would these writers attack the plan so loudly on this point? On this point, the plan agrees with the general opinion of America as stated in its various constitutions. It even adds a new and powerful safeguard that none of them has!"

If, on the other hand, he were a calm and unemotional man, he would sigh at the weakness of human nature. He would regret that, on a question so important to the happiness of millions, the real issues were being tangled up by methods so hostile to a fair and correct decision. Even such a man could hardly avoid noticing something. Conduct like this looks too much like an attempt to mislead the people by alarming their emotions, rather than to convince them by arguments aimed at their understanding.

:::quote
...a conduct of this kind has too much the appearance of an intention to mislead the people by alarming their passions, rather than to convince them by arguments addressed to their understandings.

Meaning: Hamilton accuses the opponents of trying to scare people instead of reasoning with them.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point about the Articles is accurate. Article VI of the Articles said no state could keep troops in peacetime beyond what Congress judged necessary. But nothing in the Articles limited Congress itself. So the new Constitution actually *added* a limit, the two-year funding rule, that the old system lacked.

This passage is also a good example of Hamilton as a persuader. His imaginary stranger is a clever device. It lets him call his opponents dishonest without quite saying so himself. Anti-Federalists, such as the writer called "Brutus," would have replied that the danger was new. Under the Articles, Congress couldn't raise money or troops on its own, so it didn't need limits. A Congress that could tax people directly and enlist soldiers was a different matter.
:::

## Why a ban would be unwise: the dangers around us {orig=9-10}

The objection gets little support even from our own practice. But it may still be worth looking closely at its real merits. A careful look will show that it would be unwise to limit the legislature's choices about military forces in peacetime, and that if such limits were imposed, the needs of society would probably cause them to be ignored.

A wide ocean separates the United States from Europe. Even so, several facts warn us against being too confident that we are safe.

- On one side of us, stretching far behind our settlements, are growing settlements ruled by Britain.
- On the other side, reaching out to meet the British ones, are colonies and posts ruled by Spain.
- These two powers also own islands in the West Indies near us. All this gives them a shared interest, both in their American lands and in how they deal with us.
- The Native tribes on our western frontier should be seen as our natural enemies and their natural allies. The tribes have the most to fear from us and the most to hope for from them.
- Better ships and sailing have made distant nations, in a sense, our neighbors. Britain and Spain are among the leading sea powers of Europe. It would not be unlikely for them to act together in the future.
- The "Family Compact," the alliance between the related royal families of France and Spain, grows weaker as the family ties become more distant. Politicians have always, and with good reason, seen blood ties as weak and unreliable links between nations.

Taken together, these facts warn us not to be too hopeful in thinking we are completely out of danger.

:::commentary
In 1787, Britain held Canada and still occupied forts on American soil around the Great Lakes, which it had promised to give up in the 1783 peace treaty. Spain held Florida and the vast Louisiana territory west of the Mississippi, including New Orleans. Both empires really did trade with and arm Native nations along the frontier.

Hamilton's original words call the Native peoples "savage tribes" and "natural enemies." From the point of view of the Shawnee, Miami, Creek, and other nations, the situation looked very different. They were defending their homelands against American settlers moving west, and they turned to Britain and Spain for help. Hamilton's own reason, that the tribes had "most to fear from us," admits this. Fighting in the Ohio country went on for years after this essay, until the Battle of Fallen Timbers in 1794.
:::

:::vocab
Dominion: Rule or control over a territory.
Maritime: Having to do with the sea and ships.
Family Compact: A series of alliances between the kings of France and Spain, who belonged to the same royal family, the Bourbons.
:::

## The frontier forts need full-time soldiers {orig=11}

Before the Revolution, and ever since the peace, we have constantly needed small garrisons on our western frontier. No one can doubt that they will still be necessary, if only to protect against Indian raids and attacks.

These garrisons must be supplied in one of two ways. They can come from groups of militia called up from time to time, or from permanent units paid by the government.

The first way is unworkable, and even if it could work, it would be harmful. The militia would not long put up with being dragged from their jobs and families to do that most unpleasant duty in times of complete peace, if they would do it at all. Suppose they could be persuaded or forced to do it. Then the extra cost of frequent rotation, the lost work, and the disruption of people's businesses would be strong reasons against the plan. It would be as heavy and harmful to the public as it would be ruinous to private citizens.

The second way, permanent units paid by the government, amounts to a standing army in time of peace. It is a small one, to be sure, but it is no less real for being small.

This simple view of the subject shows at once that a constitutional ban on such forces would be improper, and that the matter must be left to the judgment and good sense of the legislature.

:::quote
The latter resource of permanent corps in the pay of the government amounts to a standing army in time of peace; a small one, indeed, but not the less real for being small.

Meaning: Even a few hundred full-time soldiers guarding frontier forts count as a peacetime standing army. So a total ban would make even that small force illegal.
:::

:::vocab
Garrison: A group of troops stationed in a fort or town to defend it.
Militia: A military force made up of ordinary citizens, rather than professional soldiers, who train part-time and can be called up in emergencies.
Frontier: The edge of settled territory, where American settlements met lands held by Native nations or foreign powers.
:::

:::commentary
This is the heart of Hamilton's practical case. Even a tiny frontier force is technically a "standing army in time of peace." So a strict constitutional ban would either leave the frontier unprotected or be broken from the start. In fact, the Confederation Congress had already kept a small regiment, called the First American Regiment, on the frontier in the 1780s. The "standing army" Hamilton was defending was a few hundred soldiers in log forts, not a European-style force of tens of thousands.
:::

## Stronger neighbors and key posts {orig=12}

As we grow stronger, it is likely, and we might even say certain, that Britain and Spain will increase their military forces near us. We won't want to be left naked and defenseless against their insults and attacks. So we will find it wise to increase our frontier garrisons in some proportion to the forces that could threaten our western settlements.

There are, and will be, certain posts whose possession gives control of large areas of territory and makes future invasions of the rest much easier. Some of these posts will also be keys to trade with the Indian nations. Can anyone think it would be wise to leave such posts where either of two strong neighboring powers could seize them at any moment? Doing that would mean giving up all the usual rules of caution and good policy.

## A navy will need protection {orig=13}

If we mean to be a trading people, or even to be safe along our Atlantic coast, we must try to have a navy as soon as possible. For that we need dock-yards and arsenals, and to defend these we need forts and probably garrisons.

When a nation becomes so strong at sea that its fleets can protect its dock-yards, it no longer needs garrisons for that purpose. But where a navy is still in its infancy, modest garrisons will very likely be necessary to protect against raids from the sea that aim to destroy the arsenals and dock-yards, and sometimes the fleet itself.

:::vocab
Arsenal: A place where weapons and military supplies are made or stored.
Dock-yard: A place where ships are built and repaired.
:::

:::commentary
When Hamilton wrote, the United States had no navy at all; the Continental Navy had been broken up after the Revolution. The country began building one in the 1790s, after pirates from North Africa captured American merchant ships. The Naval Act of 1794 authorized six frigates, including the USS *Constitution*, which still floats in Boston Harbor today.
:::

:::today
The United States now keeps one of the largest standing armies in the world, something many founders would have found alarming. Yet Hamilton's main safeguard is still in place. The Army must be funded by Congress, and the Constitution still forbids army funding for more than two years at a time. Each year, Congress debates and passes a defense budget. Americans still argue about how large the military should be, how much it should cost, and how much control Congress has over how presidents use it. Those debates go back to the questions Hamilton addresses here.
:::
