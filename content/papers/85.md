:::summary
In the final essay, Hamilton wraps up the series. He points out that many supposed flaws in the Constitution also exist in New York's own constitution, which its critics praise. He lists the extra security the Union will provide for liberty and property. He admits he may sometimes have written with too much heat. Then he answers the last big argument: why adopt an imperfect plan instead of fixing it first? Because "I never expect to see a perfect work from imperfect man." Amending the Constitution after it's adopted will be far easier than renegotiating the whole thing. And time and experience, not reasoning alone, will perfect it. "A nation, without a national government, is, in my view, an awful spectacle."
:::

:::keypoints
- The last two planned topics, the plan's **similarity to New York's constitution** and the **extra security** it gives, were already covered along the way.
- Many "defects" critics attack, like no bill of rights or no executive council, are **also in New York's constitution**.
- The Union will protect liberty and property by restraining **local factions**, **ambitious individuals**, **foreign meddling**, and **large armies**, and by banning nobility and unjust state laws.
- Hamilton admits he may have sometimes written with too much feeling, provoked by false charges of a conspiracy.
- Every citizen must decide **by conscience**, without blind loyalty to party.
- The plan isn't perfect, but it's **the best possible** under the circumstances.
- **"I never expect to see a perfect work from imperfect man."**
- Amendments **after** ratification will be much easier than changes **before**, since each amendment can be voted on by itself, and Congress must call a convention if two-thirds of the states ask.
- Quoting David Hume: **experience and time** must perfect any government.
- **"A NATION, without a NATIONAL GOVERNMENT, is, in my view, an awful spectacle."**
:::

:::context Background
This essay first appeared in May 1788 in the book edition of *The Federalist*. When Hamilton wrote it, seven states had ratified the Constitution. South Carolina became the eighth on May 23, 1788, just days before the book came out. Only one more was needed for the Constitution to take effect. But New York and Virginia, two large and important states, were still undecided, and without them the new government might fail.

New York's ratifying convention met in June 1788. Most delegates were Anti-Federalists at first. Hamilton led the fight for the Constitution there. After news arrived that New Hampshire and Virginia had ratified, New York approved the Constitution on July 26, 1788, by just 30 votes to 27.
:::

## The last two topics are already covered {orig=1-2}

According to the plan I announced in my first essay, two topics would seem to remain: "the analogy of the proposed government to your own State constitution," and "the additional security which its adoption will afford to republican government, to liberty, and to property." But these topics have already been so fully covered along the way that I could do little more than repeat, at greater length, what has already been said. The late stage of the debate and the time already spent on it both rule that out.

It's remarkable that the Convention's plan resembles New York's own constitution not only in its real strengths, but also in many of its supposed flaws. Among the claimed defects are:

- the president's ability to be re-elected;
- the lack of an executive council;
- the lack of a formal bill of rights;
- the lack of a provision about freedom of the press.

These, and several others noted along the way, apply as much to New York's current constitution as to the one proposed for the Union. A person would have little claim to consistency if he attacked the national plan for flaws he has no trouble excusing in his own state's. In fact, there could be no better proof of the insincerity and pretense of some of the plan's most zealous opponents among us, who claim to be devoted admirers of our state government, than the fury with which they have attacked the plan for things where our own constitution is equally or even more open to criticism.

:::commentary
Hamilton's point is about consistency. If New York's constitution has no bill of rights and New Yorkers still feel free, why is the lack of one in the national Constitution a disaster? Anti-Federalists could answer that the national government would be far more powerful and far away, so it needed more limits. Hamilton made his full argument about a bill of rights in [No. 84](/papers/84/).
:::

## The extra security the Union provides {orig=3}

The added protections for republican government, liberty, and property that would come from adopting this plan are mainly these:

- restraints on **local factions and rebellions**, which preserving the Union will impose;
- restraints on the **ambition of powerful individuals** in single states, who might gain enough support from their followers and favorites to become tyrants over the people;
- fewer opportunities for **foreign scheming**, which breaking up the Union would invite and make easier;
- preventing the **large military forces** that would surely grow out of wars between the states if they were divided;
- the explicit **guarantee of a republican government** to every state;
- the complete and universal **ban on titles of nobility**;
- precautions against **state laws that have undermined property and credit**, planted distrust among all classes of citizens, and caused a nearly universal collapse of morals.

:::commentary
This list summarizes much of the series. Union restrains faction (Nos. [9](/papers/9/) and [10](/papers/10/)), prevents foreign meddling (Nos. 2–5), and avoids wars between the states and the large armies they would require (Nos. 6–8). The last item refers to Article I, Section 10, which forbids states from printing paper money or passing laws that break contracts. Hamilton saw the debt-relief and paper-money laws of the 1780s as both unjust and a moral disaster.
:::

## How Publius conducted himself {orig=4}

So, fellow citizens, I have carried out the task I set for myself. Whether I've succeeded is for your decisions to show. I hope you'll at least agree that I kept my promise about the spirit in which I would write. I have spoken only to your judgment. I have carefully avoided the harshness that too often disgraces political arguers of every party, though the language and conduct of the Constitution's opponents have provoked it quite a bit.

The charge that supporters of the plan are part of a conspiracy against the people's liberties, which has been thrown at all of them without distinction, is too reckless and too malicious not to anger every man who knows in his own heart that the accusation is false. The endless repetition of attacks on "the wealthy, the well-born, and the great" has disgusted all sensible people. And the unjustified hiding and twisting of facts, used in various ways to keep the truth from the public, deserves the condemnation of all honest people.

It's possible these things may have sometimes led me into language more heated than I intended. I have certainly often felt a struggle between my feelings and my desire to stay moderate. If feelings have sometimes won, my excuse must be that it hasn't happened often or by much.

:::commentary
Hamilton promised in [No. 1](/papers/1/) to make his arguments "in a spirit which will not disgrace the cause of truth." Here he grades his own performance and admits he sometimes got carried away. That's a rare and honest moment in persuasive writing.

The phrase "the wealthy, the well-born, and the great" was a common Anti-Federalist attack. Critics argued that the Constitution was designed by and for a wealthy elite. Delegate John Adams had used the phrase "the rich, the well-born, and the able" in his writing about government, and Anti-Federalists turned similar phrases against the Federalists. Whether the Constitution mainly served the wealthy is still debated by historians.
:::

## Every citizen must decide by conscience {orig=5-6}

Let's pause and ask ourselves two questions. In these essays, hasn't the proposed Constitution been satisfactorily defended against the attacks on it? And hasn't it been shown to deserve public approval, and to be necessary for public safety and prosperity?

Every person must answer these questions for himself, according to his best conscience and understanding, and act on the honest and sober conclusions of his judgment. Nothing can excuse him from this duty. He is called on, and in fact bound by all the obligations that hold society together, to carry it out sincerely and honestly.

No narrow motive, no special interest, no pride in his own opinion, no passing passion or prejudice will justify, to himself, to his country, or to future generations, a wrong choice of the side he takes. Let him beware of stubborn loyalty to a party. Let him remember that what he is deciding is not some particular interest of the community, but the very existence of the nation. And let him remember that a majority of America has already approved the plan he is to accept or reject.

I won't pretend that I'm not completely confident in the arguments for adopting the proposed system. I can't see any real force in the arguments against it. I'm convinced it is the best that our political situation, habits, and opinions will allow, and better than any the Revolution has produced.

:::quote
Let him beware of an obstinate adherence to party; let him reflect that the object upon which he is to decide is not a particular interest of the community, but the very existence of the nation...

Meaning: Don't vote just because your side tells you to. This decision is too important. It's about whether the nation survives.
:::

## Why adopt an imperfect plan? {orig=7-9}

Supporters of the plan have admitted that it isn't perfect, and its enemies have made a lot of this. "Why," they ask, "should we adopt an imperfect thing? Why not amend it and make it perfect before it is permanently established?" This may sound reasonable, but it only sounds that way.

First, the extent of these admissions has been greatly exaggerated. They've been described as admitting that the plan is fundamentally flawed, and that without major changes the rights and interests of the community can't be safely trusted to it. As far as I understand what the people making these admissions mean, this completely twists their meaning. You can't find a single supporter of the plan who won't say that, although it may not be perfect in every part, it is a good one overall. It's the best the country's present views and circumstances allow. And it promises every kind of security a reasonable people could want.

Second, I would consider it the height of foolishness to drag out the shaky state of our national affairs, and to put the Union at risk through one experiment after another, in a fantasy search for a perfect plan. I never expect to see a perfect work from imperfect man.

The result of any group's discussions must be a mixture of the errors and prejudices as well as the good sense and wisdom of the individuals in it. An agreement meant to join thirteen separate states in a common bond of friendship and union must also be a compromise among that many different interests and wishes. How could perfection come from such materials?

An excellent little pamphlet recently published in this city gives unanswerable reasons why it would be extremely unlikely to assemble a new convention under conditions anywhere near as favorable as those under which the last one met, discussed, and reached agreement. I won't repeat its arguments, since I assume it has been widely read. It certainly deserves to be read by every friend of his country. But there is one way of looking at the question of amendments that hasn't been presented to the public yet. I can't finish without examining it.

:::quote
I never expect to see a perfect work from imperfect man.

Meaning: Humans are imperfect, so anything humans make will be imperfect too. Waiting for a perfect constitution means waiting forever.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the most famous lines in the Federalist Papers. It echoes Madison's [No. 37](/papers/37/), which explained why the Convention couldn't produce a flawless plan, and Benjamin Franklin's speech on the Convention's last day, when he said he agreed to the Constitution "with all its faults."

The "excellent little pamphlet" was *An Address to the People of the State of New York*, published in 1788 by "A Citizen of New-York." The author was John Jay.
:::

## Amendments will be easier after adoption {orig=10-12}

It seems to me that it can be proven absolutely that it will be far easier to get amendments after the Constitution is adopted than before.

The moment any change is made to the current plan, it becomes, for purposes of adoption, a new plan. It must go through a new decision by every state. To be fully established throughout the Union, it would need the agreement of all thirteen states. But if the Constitution is first ratified by all the states as it stands, changes can be made at any time with nine states. So the odds are thirteen to nine in favor of amending later, rather than adopting a whole new system now.

That's not all. Any constitution for the United States must include a great number of details, in which thirteen independent states must have their interests, or what they think are their interests, satisfied. So in any group charged with writing it from scratch, we can expect to see very different alliances on different points. Many who are in the majority on one question may be in the minority on a second. And a different group again may make up the majority on a third. That's why all the details have to be shaped and arranged to satisfy every party to the agreement. And that's why getting everyone to agree to a final plan becomes enormously more difficult and uncertain. The more details and the more parties there are, the worse it gets.

But every amendment to the Constitution, once it exists, would be a single proposal and could be brought forward by itself. There would be no need for deal-making or compromise on any other point, no giving and taking. The will of the required number of states would settle the matter at once. So whenever nine, or rather ten, states agreed on a particular amendment, it would certainly be adopted. There's simply no comparison between how easy it is to pass one amendment and how hard it is to establish a complete Constitution in the first place.

:::note Hamilton's footnote
Hamilton corrected himself in a footnote: it would really take ten states, not nine. Two-thirds of thirteen states (nine) could start the amendment process, but three-fourths (ten) would have to ratify an amendment.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's argument here is about how negotiations work. Writing a whole constitution requires a giant package deal where everyone has to give something up. An amendment, by contrast, is a single question with a yes-or-no answer.

He turned out to be right that amendments would come quickly. The first Congress proposed the Bill of Rights in 1789, and the states ratified ten amendments by 1791. Federalists had promised these amendments to win ratification in states like Massachusetts, Virginia, and New York.
:::

## Congress can't block amendments {orig=13-14}

Against the likelihood of later amendments, some argue that the people running the national government will always be unwilling to give up any power they have.

For my part, I am fully convinced that any amendments thought useful after careful consideration will concern the *organization* of the government, not the *amount* of its powers. For that reason alone, I think this objection has no weight.

I also think it has little weight for another reason. The basic difficulty of governing THIRTEEN STATES will constantly force the national leaders to adjust to the reasonable expectations of the people, even setting aside any ordinary amount of public spirit and honesty.

But there is a further point that proves beyond any doubt that the objection is useless. Whenever nine states agree, the national leaders will have no choice. Under Article V of the plan, Congress will be required, "on the application of the legislatures of two thirds of the States (which at present amount to nine), to call a convention for proposing amendments, which shall be valid, to all intents and purposes, as part of the Constitution, when ratified by the legislatures of three fourths of the States, or by conventions in three fourths thereof."

The words of this article are commanding. Congress "shall call a convention." Nothing here is left to its discretion. So all the speeches about Congress's unwillingness to change simply vanish into thin air.

However hard it may be to unite two-thirds or three-fourths of the state legislatures on amendments that affect local interests, there's no reason to expect any such difficulty when it comes to points that concern only the general liberty or safety of the people. We can safely count on the state legislatures being eager to put up barriers against the national government's overreach.

If this argument is mistaken, then I'm certainly fooled by it myself. In my view, it is one of those rare cases where a political truth can be tested with mathematical proof. Those who see it the same way I do, however eager they are for amendments, must agree that adopting the Constitution first is the most direct path to their own goal.

:::vocab
Article V: The part of the Constitution that explains how it can be amended.
Convention for proposing amendments: A gathering of the states that Congress must call if two-thirds of state legislatures request it. It has never been used. All 27 amendments so far have been proposed by Congress.
:::

:::commentary
Article V offers two ways to propose amendments: by a two-thirds vote of both houses of Congress, or by a convention called at the request of two-thirds of the states. Either way, three-fourths of the states must ratify. Hamilton stresses the convention route because it lets the states go around a Congress that refuses to act. Even though it has never been used, the threat of a convention has sometimes pushed Congress to act, as with the 17th Amendment (direct election of senators) in 1912.
:::

## Time and experience will perfect it {orig=15}

The eagerness to try amending the Constitution before it is established should fade in anyone who accepts these observations by a writer as sound as he is clever:

> "To balance a large state or society, whether monarchical or republican, on general laws, is a work of so great difficulty, that no human genius, however comprehensive, is able, by the mere dint of reason and reflection, to effect it. The judgments of many must unite in the work; EXPERIENCE must guide their labor; TIME must bring it to perfection, and the FEELING of inconveniences must correct the mistakes which they inevitably fall into in their first trials and experiments."

These wise reflections teach a lesson of moderation to all sincere lovers of the Union. They should warn them against risking anarchy, civil war, a permanent hostility between the states, and perhaps the military tyranny of a victorious demagogue, in pursuit of what they can only get from TIME and EXPERIENCE.

It may be a lack of political courage on my part, but I admit I can't feel as calm as those who pretend that the dangers of staying in our current situation any longer are imaginary. A NATION, without a NATIONAL GOVERNMENT, is, in my view, an awful spectacle. The establishment of a Constitution, in a time of deep peace, by the free consent of a whole people, is a WONDER, and I look forward to its completion with trembling anxiety.

I can't reconcile it with any rule of good sense to let go of the hold we now have on seven of the thirteen states, in such a difficult undertaking, and to start over after covering so much ground. I fear the results of new attempts all the more because I know that POWERFUL INDIVIDUALS, in this state and in others, are enemies of a national government in any form whatsoever.

:::quote
A NATION, without a NATIONAL GOVERNMENT, is, in my view, an awful spectacle.

Meaning: A country without a working national government is a terrible thing to see. "Awful" here means both "terrible" and "awe-inspiring."
:::

:::quote
The establishment of a Constitution, in time of profound peace, by the voluntary consent of a whole people, is a PRODIGY, to the completion of which I look forward with trembling anxiety.

Meaning: A whole people peacefully choosing their own government is something almost miraculous. A "prodigy" is an amazing, rare event. Hamilton is nervous because he knows how easily it could fail.
:::

:::vocab
David Hume: A Scottish philosopher and historian (1711–1776) whose essays were widely read in America. Hamilton quotes his essay "The Rise of Arts and Sciences."
Demagogue: A leader who gains power by appealing to people's emotions and prejudices.
Prodigy: Something extraordinary or marvelous.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton ends the series where it began. [No. 1](/papers/1/) asked whether people can create good government "from reflection and choice" rather than "accident and force." Here he calls the peaceful adoption of a constitution by a whole people a "prodigy," a near-miracle.

His quotation from Hume makes a humble point: no one, however brilliant, can design a perfect government on paper. Experience must reveal the problems, and time must fix them. That's exactly how the Constitution has developed, through 27 amendments, court decisions, and practice.

His last sentence is a warning aimed at home. The "POWERFUL INDIVIDUALS" in New York who opposed any national government meant Governor George Clinton and his allies. Hamilton would face them at New York's ratifying convention a few weeks later and, against the odds, help win.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's closing argument shaped how Americans think about their Constitution: not as a perfect, finished document, but as a framework that experience would improve. The 27 amendments include some of the most important changes in American history, such as the Bill of Rights, the abolition of slavery, equal protection of the laws, and voting rights for women. Americans still debate whether the amendment process is too hard to use today, and how much the Constitution's meaning should change through interpretation rather than amendment. But Hamilton's basic idea, that "time and experience" must correct the mistakes of any human design, is one most Americans share.
:::
