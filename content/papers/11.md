:::summary
Hamilton argues that the Union is essential to American trade. European powers with colonies in America fear the energy of American merchants and would like to keep the states divided and weak. United, the states could use their large and growing market as a bargaining chip, closing their ports to nations that treat them unfairly. A united America could also build a navy strong enough to tip the balance in European wars fought in the Caribbean, so that both sides would pay for its friendship or neutrality. Divided, the states would be pushed around, lose their shipping, and be left selling raw goods at low prices. Hamilton ends with a bold call: Americans should refuse to serve "European greatness" and build "one great American system" of their own.
:::

:::keypoints
- European sea powers fear American shipping and would try to **keep the states divided**.
- A united government could **close American ports** to a country like Britain and force it to offer better trade terms.
- A national **navy**, even a modest one, could **decide battles in the West Indies**, so Europeans would pay for American friendship or **neutrality**.
- A weak nation can't even stay neutral: "**A nation, despicable by its weakness, forfeits even the privilege of being neutral.**"
- Divided, the states would be left with a **passive commerce**, with foreign ships carrying their goods and taking the profits.
- Important rights belong to the whole Union: the **fisheries**, the **Great Lakes**, and the **Mississippi River**.
- Each region could add something to a navy: **Southern timber and naval stores**, **iron** from the South and Middle states, and **Northern sailors**.
- **Free trade among the states** would make the whole country richer.
- Americans should refuse to be "**the instruments of European greatness**" and build an American system free of European control.
:::

:::context Background
After the Revolution, American trade was in trouble. Britain, no longer bound by the old colonial ties, closed its islands in the West Indies (the Caribbean) to American ships, which had long carried food and lumber there. The two countries had no trade treaty. Spain had closed the Mississippi River to Americans. And under the Articles of Confederation, Congress had no power to regulate trade, so it couldn't strike back. Each state set its own trade rules, and when one state closed its ports to British ships, the British could simply use a neighboring state's ports instead.

The new Constitution would give Congress the power to "regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States." In this essay, Hamilton explains why that power, and a navy, mattered so much.
:::

## Europe is watching American trade {orig=1-2}

The value of the Union to our trade is one of the points on which there is the least room for disagreement. In fact, nearly everyone who knows anything about the subject agrees on it. This is true of our trade with foreign countries, and of our trade with each other.

There are signs that the bold spirit of American merchants has already made several of the sea powers of Europe uneasy. They seem afraid that we will take too large a share of the carrying trade. That trade supports their shipping and is the foundation of their naval strength.

Those nations that have colonies in America look ahead to what this country could become, and they are worried. They foresee danger to their American colonies from having, next door, states that have every desire, and would have every means, to build a powerful navy.

Feelings like these will naturally lead them toward a policy of encouraging divisions among us. They will want to keep us, as far as possible, from having an ACTIVE COMMERCE in our own ships. This would serve three purposes for them: it would keep us from competing with their shipping, it would let them take all the profits of our trade, and it would clip the wings we might use to soar to a greatness that would be dangerous to them. If caution did not forbid the details, it would not be hard to trace this policy, through facts, all the way to the offices of the ministers of European governments.

:::vocab
Carrying trade: The business of shipping goods by sea, including other nations' goods, for a fee.
Maritime: Having to do with the sea, shipping, or naval power.
Active commerce: Trade in which a nation carries its own goods in its own ships. Hamilton contrasts it with "passive commerce," where foreign ships do the carrying.
Bottoms: An old word for ships. Trading "in our own bottoms" means in American ships.
:::

:::commentary
In the 1700s, shipping was a source of national power. Merchant ships trained sailors, and those sailors could be called on to man warships. That's why European powers cared so much about who carried trade across the Atlantic. Britain's Navigation Acts, for example, required that much of its colonial trade travel in British ships.

Hamilton hints that he could name names but won't, out of "prudence." Many Americans believed Britain was deliberately trying to keep the states weak and divided, but Hamilton chooses not to offer evidence here.
:::

## Our markets as a bargaining chip {orig=3-4}

If we stay united, we can fight this unfriendly policy in many ways. We could pass trade restrictions that apply in every state at once. Then foreign countries would have to bid against each other for the right to sell in our markets.

This idea won't seem far-fetched to anyone who understands how valuable our market would be to any manufacturing nation. We are a market of three million people, growing rapidly. Most of us are farmers, and because of our situation we are likely to stay that way, so we will need to buy manufactured goods. It also makes a huge difference to a manufacturing nation's trade and shipping whether it can sell to America directly in its own ships, or only indirectly in the ships of another country.

Suppose, for example, that we had a government in America that could close all our ports to Great Britain, a country we currently have no trade treaty with. What would that do to British policy? Wouldn't it give us an excellent chance to negotiate for the most valuable and wide-ranging trade privileges in Britain's territories?

When people have asked these questions before, they have received an answer that sounds reasonable but doesn't hold up. It goes like this: our ban would not change British policy, because Britain could keep trading with us through the Dutch. The Dutch would buy British goods and then sell them in our markets, acting as Britain's direct customers and paying her for the goods.

But wouldn't British shipping be badly hurt by losing the important advantage of carrying its own goods? Wouldn't the Dutch take most of the profits, as payment for their work and risk? Wouldn't the cost of shipping alone take a large bite out of the profits? And wouldn't such a roundabout trade help other nations compete with Britain? It would raise the price of British goods in our markets, and it would hand control of this valuable part of British trade to others.

If we think these questions through, we can reasonably believe that Britain would loosen its present policy. Several forces would push it that way: the real losses Britain would suffer, the support for American trade among a large part of the British people, and the pressure from the British islands in the West Indies. Britain would let us into the markets of those islands and other places, and our trade would gain great benefits.

Of course, we could not expect to win this from the British government without giving something equal in return, such as tax breaks and special privileges in our own markets. But once we won it, it would probably have a similar effect on other nations. They would not want to see themselves pushed out of our trade entirely.

:::vocab
Prohibitory regulations: Laws that ban certain imports or ban certain countries' ships from a nation's ports.
Chimerical: Imaginary; wildly unrealistic.
West Indies: The islands of the Caribbean Sea. In 1787, Britain, France, Spain, the Netherlands, and Denmark all had colonies there.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's argument depends on the Union acting as one. Under the Articles, some states did try to punish Britain by taxing or banning British ships. But as long as a neighboring state kept its ports open, British goods simply flowed through there instead. Only a national government could make a ban stick.

The British West Indies mattered because the sugar islands depended on food, lumber, and other supplies from North America. Hamilton expected the planters there to pressure their government to reopen trade with the Americans. Later, as Secretary of the Treasury, Hamilton generally opposed fighting Britain with trade restrictions, because the new government depended on taxes from British imports. Madison and Jefferson favored using trade pressure. So this essay shows an idea that the two sides would later disagree about.
:::

## A navy that could tip the balance {orig=5}

Building a federal navy would give us another way to influence how European nations treat us. There is no doubt that if the Union continues under an effective government, we could build a navy before long. It might not be able to compete with the navies of the great sea powers. But it would at least carry real weight if we added it to either side in a war between two of them.

This would be especially true for fighting in the West Indies. A few ships of the line, sent at the right moment to help either side, would often be enough to decide the outcome of a campaign, with enormous interests riding on the result. Our location gives us a commanding position for this.

Add to this how useful our supplies would be to armies and navies fighting in the West Indies. Then it is easy to see that our favorable position would let us bargain for valuable trade privileges. Nations would pay a price not just for our friendship, but even for our neutrality. If we hold firmly to the Union, we can hope soon to become the referee of Europe in America. We would be able to tip the balance of European rivalries in this part of the world in whatever direction our interests call for.

:::vocab
Ships of the line: The largest warships of the time, carrying many cannons. They fought in a line of battle.
Neutrality: Not taking sides in a war between other nations.
Arbiter: A judge or referee who has the final say in a dispute.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's readers knew how this could work, because it had just happened. French naval support was crucial to the American victory over Britain. At Yorktown in 1781, a French fleet kept British ships from rescuing General Cornwallis's army, which then surrendered.

When Hamilton wrote, the United States had no navy at all. The Continental Navy had been disbanded after the war. In 1794, Congress finally authorized six frigates, partly to fight pirates from North Africa who were seizing American ships and sailors. One of them, the USS *Constitution*, is still afloat today.
:::

## A weak nation can't even stay neutral {orig=6-8}

But if we are divided, we will find the opposite, because the rival parts of the country would block each other, and we would lose all the tempting advantages that nature has so kindly placed within our reach.

In such a weak condition, our trade would be at the mercy of every nation at war. Since they would have nothing to fear from us, they would feel little guilt or regret about meeting their needs by seizing our property whenever it came their way. The rights of neutral nations are respected only when an adequate power defends them. A nation that is despised for its weakness loses even the right to be neutral.

Under an energetic national government, the natural strength and resources of the country, directed toward a common interest, would defeat every plan that European jealousy could invent to hold back our growth. In fact, it would remove the reason for such plans, because they would have no chance of success. An active commerce, a large shipping industry, and a thriving navy would then grow naturally, out of both need and circumstance. We could ignore the petty tricks of petty politicians who try to control or change the unstoppable course of nature.

But if the states were disunited, those plans might exist and might succeed. The sea powers of Europe could take advantage of our total weakness to dictate the conditions of our national life. They all share an interest in carrying our goods for us. And they share an even stronger interest in keeping us from carrying theirs. So they would probably join together to interfere with our shipping so much that they would in effect destroy it. In the end, they would leave us with only a PASSIVE COMMERCE.

We would then have to settle for the first price of our goods, the low price paid at our own docks. We would watch the profits of our trade snatched away to enrich our enemies and persecutors. American merchants and sailors have an unmatched spirit of enterprise, which is itself an endless mine of national wealth. That spirit would be smothered and lost, and poverty and disgrace would spread over a country that, if it acted wisely, could make itself the admiration and envy of the world.

:::quote
The rights of neutrality will only be respected when they are defended by an adequate power. A nation, despicable by its weakness, forfeits even the privilege of being neutral.

Meaning: Staying out of other nations' wars only works if you are strong enough to make them respect your choice. A weak country gets trampled no matter what it says.
:::

:::vocab
Passive commerce: Trade in which foreign ships carry a nation's goods, so foreigners take most of the shipping profits.
Depredations: Acts of robbery or plunder.
:::

:::commentary
This prediction came true even under the Constitution. During the long wars between Britain and France that began in the 1790s, both countries seized American merchant ships, and Britain forced thousands of American sailors into its navy. These conflicts led to an undeclared naval war with France (1798–1800) and the War of 1812 with Britain. Hamilton would have said this proved his point about needing naval strength. Others would say it showed that even a united America was not yet strong enough to make its neutrality respected.
:::

## Rights that belong to the whole Union {orig=9-10}

Some rights of great importance to American trade belong to the Union as a whole. I mean the fisheries, the right to sail the western lakes, and the right to sail the Mississippi River. If the Confederacy broke up, there would be delicate questions about whether these rights would survive. And the interests of more powerful nations would almost surely settle those questions against us.

Spain's attitude toward the Mississippi needs no comment. France and Britain share the fisheries with us, and they consider them extremely important to their shipping. Experience has shown that we have a clear mastery of this valuable trade, so that we can undersell those nations in their own markets. They can hardly stay unconcerned about that for long. What would be more natural than for them to try to push such dangerous competitors out of the contest?

This trade should not be seen as benefiting only one part of the country. All the shipping states can take part in it, to different degrees, and they would be likely to do so as more money becomes available for trade. As a training ground for sailors, the fishing industry already is, or will become, a resource for the whole country, once time has made the states' shipping practices more alike. And it must be absolutely essential to the establishment of a navy.

:::vocab
Fisheries: Fishing grounds, especially the rich cod-fishing waters off Newfoundland and Nova Scotia.
Western lakes: The Great Lakes.
Nursery of seamen: A place where sailors are trained. Fishing crews learned skills that could later serve in a navy.
:::

:::commentary
Each of these rights was a live issue in 1787:

- **The fisheries:** The 1783 Treaty of Paris recognized Americans' right to fish in the waters off Newfoundland. New England fishermen depended on it, and American negotiators, especially John Adams, had fought hard for it.
- **The Great Lakes:** Britain still held forts on the American side of the lakes, despite promising in the treaty to leave them. See [No. 15](/papers/15/).
- **The Mississippi:** Spain closed the river to American shipping in 1784. In 1786, John Jay, then in charge of foreign affairs for Congress, proposed giving up American use of the river for many years in exchange for a trade treaty with Spain. Southern and western Americans were furious, and the plan failed. That is why Spain's "disposition... needs no comment."
:::

## Every region can help build a navy {orig=11}

The Union will help us build that great national goal, a NAVY, in several ways. Every institution grows and thrives in proportion to the amount of resources gathered to create and support it. A navy of the United States would draw on the resources of all the states. So it is a far more reachable goal than a navy of any single state or partial confederacy, which could draw only on the resources of one part.

It happens that different parts of America each have some special advantage for building a navy:

- The more southern states have greater supplies of certain naval stores, such as tar, pitch, and turpentine. Their wood for building ships is also stronger and longer-lasting. If the navy's ships were built mostly of Southern wood, they would last much longer. That would make a major difference, both for naval strength and for saving money.
- Some of the Southern and Middle states produce more iron, and of better quality.
- Sailors must come mainly from the Northern hive, the busy seaports of New England.

It hardly needs to be explained that trade by sea needs naval protection. Nor does it need to be explained that this kind of trade helps a navy grow.

:::vocab
Naval stores: Products from pine trees, such as tar, pitch, and turpentine, used to seal and maintain wooden ships.
Northern hive: Hamilton's image for New England, whose busy fishing and shipping towns produced many sailors.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point about Southern wood proved true. The USS *Constitution* was built partly of live oak from Georgia, an extremely dense wood. It helped the ship survive cannon fire so well in the War of 1812 that it earned the nickname "Old Ironsides."

Hamilton's larger point is that the regions **complement** each other. Each has something the others lack, so together they can do what none could do alone.
:::

## Free trade among the states {orig=12-13}

Unrestricted trade among the states will help each state's commerce. They will trade their different products with each other, both to supply each other's needs at home and to sell in foreign markets. The veins of commerce in every part of the country will be refilled, and they will gain more energy and motion from the free flow of goods from every part.

Because the states produce such different goods, merchants will have much more room to do business. When one state's main crop fails because of a bad harvest, it can turn to another state's main crop for help. The variety of goods for export, as well as their value, helps keep foreign trade active.

Trade works much better with many kinds of goods of a certain total value than with just a few kinds of goods worth the same amount. This is because of competition and the ups and downs of markets. Some goods may be in great demand at one time and impossible to sell at another. But with a wide variety of goods, it can hardly happen that all of them are unsellable at once. So a merchant's business is less likely to be blocked or to stall. Experienced traders will see the force of these points right away, and they will agree that the overall balance of trade of the United States would likely be much more favorable than that of thirteen states without a union, or with partial unions.

Someone may answer that, whether the states are united or not, they will still trade closely with each other, and that will serve the same purposes. But that trade would be chained, interrupted, and narrowed by many causes, which these papers have described in detail. A unity of trade interests, like a unity of political interests, can only come from a unity of government.

:::vocab
Staple: A main product that a region grows or makes, like tobacco in Virginia or rice in South Carolina.
Balance of trade: The difference between the value of what a nation sells abroad and what it buys from abroad.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is making an early argument for what we would now call a national common market. The Constitution's Commerce Clause gave Congress power over trade "among the several States," and courts have long read it to keep states from setting up trade barriers against each other. This helped make the United States one of the largest free-trade zones in the world. His point about variety is also an early version of a familiar idea in investing: **diversification**, or spreading your risk so that one failure doesn't ruin you.
:::

## One great American system {orig=14-15}

This subject could be seen from other angles that are striking and inspiring. But they would carry us too far into the future, and they involve topics that are not suitable for a newspaper. I will only say briefly that our situation invites us, and our interests urge us, to aim for a leading position in the affairs of the Americas.

The world may be divided politically, as well as geographically, into four parts, each with its own set of interests. Unfortunately for the other three, Europe has extended its rule over all of them, to different degrees, by its armies and its diplomacy, by force and by fraud. Africa, Asia, and America have each in turn felt its domination.

The power Europe has held for so long has tempted it to praise itself as the Mistress of the World, and to think of the rest of humanity as created for its benefit. Men admired as deep philosophers have flatly claimed that Europeans are physically superior. They have seriously argued that all animals, including human beings, decline in America. They have even said that dogs stop barking after breathing our air for a while.

For too long, the facts have seemed to support these arrogant European claims. It is up to us to defend the honor of the human race, and to teach that arrogant brother some moderation. Union will let us do it, while disunion will add another victim to his triumphs.

Let Americans disdain to be the instruments of European greatness! Let the thirteen states, bound together in a strict and unbreakable Union, join in building one great American system. Let it be beyond the control of any force or influence from across the Atlantic, and able to set the terms of the relationship between the old world and the new!

PUBLIUS

Meaning: Americans should refuse to be tools that European powers use to make themselves richer and stronger.
:::

:::note
Hamilton's footnote cites *Recherches philosophiques sur les Américains* ("Philosophical Inquiries on the Americans") by Cornelius de Pauw, a Dutch writer. De Pauw was one of the best-known writers claiming that life in the Americas was weaker and "degenerate."
:::

:::vocab
Ascendant: Controlling influence or power.
Transatlantic: From across the Atlantic Ocean; here, from Europe.
Indissoluble: Impossible to break apart or dissolve.
:::

:::commentary
The claim that nature "degenerates" in America was a real scientific theory in the 1700s. The famous French naturalist Buffon argued that American animals were smaller and weaker than those of Europe, and writers like de Pauw extended the idea to people. Americans took it as an insult. Thomas Jefferson spent part of his book *Notes on the State of Virginia* refuting Buffon with measurements of American animals.

Hamilton's call for "one great American system" looks ahead to later American policy. In 1823, the Monroe Doctrine warned European powers not to start new colonies in the Western Hemisphere. Readers have taken Hamilton's words in different ways. Some see them as a proud declaration of independence from European power. Others see an early sign of the American ambition to dominate the hemisphere, which later led to conflicts with Latin American nations.

Hamilton condemns Europe for dominating Africa, Asia, and America "by force and by fraud," and he rejects claims that some people are naturally inferior. But in 1787 the United States itself held hundreds of thousands of people of African descent in slavery, and it was expanding onto the lands of Native nations. Hamilton's words about defending "the honor of the human race" can be read against that background.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's ideas about trade and power still shape debates today. Nations still use access to their markets as a bargaining chip, through tariffs, trade deals, and sanctions. People still argue about whether those tools work. Supporters say a big market gives a country real leverage, just as Hamilton claimed. Critics say trade wars often hurt a country's own consumers and farmers as much as the target. The U.S. Navy still protects shipping lanes around the world, an idea that goes back to Hamilton's claim that a nation must be strong enough to defend its trade. And the "great American system" he hoped for raises a question people still debate: what kind of role should the United States play in the world?
:::
