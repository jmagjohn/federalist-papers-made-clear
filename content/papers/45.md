:::summary
Critics feared the new national government would slowly swallow up the states. Madison answers in two steps. First, he says the question is backward: the purpose of government is the happiness of the people, not the power of the states, so if a strong Union is needed for the people's good, state power has to give way. Second, he argues the states are not actually in danger. History shows that in unions of states, the members usually overpower the center, not the other way around. The states will have more officials, more influence, and more of the people's loyalty. And the national government's powers are "few and defined," while the states' are "numerous and indefinite."
:::

:::keypoints
- The **public good** is the supreme goal. No form of government, state or national, has value except as a means to that goal.
- "We have heard of the impious doctrine in the Old World, that the people were made for kings, not kings for the people." The same mistake shouldn't be made about state governments.
- History shows that members of confederacies tend to **strip power from the central government**, not the reverse.
- The national government will **depend on the states** (for choosing the president and senators), but the states won't depend on it.
- The states will employ **far more people**, giving them much more influence.
- National powers are **"few and defined"** and mostly concern war, peace, diplomacy, and foreign trade. State powers are **"numerous and indefinite"** and cover daily life.
- The Constitution mainly **strengthens powers Congress already had** under the Articles, rather than adding new ones.
:::

:::context Background
In Nos. 41–44, Madison went through the powers the Constitution gives the national government and argued each one was necessary. Now he asks whether all those powers *together* would overwhelm the states. This was one of the Anti-Federalists' deepest fears. Writers like "Brutus" predicted that the national government, with its power to tax and its supremacy clause, would gradually absorb the states until nothing was left of them but names.
:::

## The wrong question {orig=1-2}

I have shown that none of the powers given to the national government is unnecessary or improper. The next question is whether all of those powers together would endanger the share of authority left to the states.

Instead of first asking how much power the national government absolutely needs to do its job, the Convention's opponents have worn themselves out on a secondary question: what the proposed powers might do to the state governments.

But suppose, as I have shown, that the Union is essential to protect the American people against foreign danger. Suppose it is essential to protect them against conflicts and wars between the states. Suppose it is essential to guard them against the violent, oppressive factions that spoil the blessings of liberty, and against the large military forces that would gradually poison liberty at its source. In short, suppose the Union is essential to the happiness of the American people. Then isn't it ridiculous to object to a government that the Union can't do without, just because it might make the state governments less important?

Was the American Revolution fought, was the Confederacy formed, was the precious blood of thousands spilled, and were the hard-earned savings of millions spent, so that the American people could enjoy peace, liberty, and safety? Or so that the state governments could enjoy a certain amount of power and be dressed up with certain honors and signs of sovereignty?

We have heard of the wicked idea in the Old World that the people were made for kings, not kings for the people. Is the same idea to be brought back in the New World in another form, so that the real happiness of the people is sacrificed to the interests of a different kind of political institution?

It is too soon for politicians to assume we have forgotten this: the public good, the real welfare of the great body of the people, is the highest goal to pursue. No form of government has any value except as a tool for achieving it.

If the Convention's plan went against the public happiness, I would say: Reject the plan. If the Union itself went against the public happiness, I would say: Abolish the Union. In the same way, to the extent that the sovereignty of the states can't be reconciled with the happiness of the people, every good citizen should say: Let state sovereignty be sacrificed to the people's happiness.

I have already shown how much of a sacrifice is necessary. The question now is how much danger the remaining, unsacrificed part of state power will face.

:::quote
We have heard of the impious doctrine in the Old World, that the people were made for kings, not kings for the people.

Meaning: Governments exist to serve people, not the other way around. Madison says this is just as true of state governments as of kings.
:::

:::quote
...the public good, the real welfare of the great body of the people, is the supreme object to be pursued; and that no form of government whatever has any other value than as it may be fitted for the attainment of this object.

Meaning: A government, whether national or state, is only a tool. It is valuable only if it makes people's lives better.
:::

:::vocab
Sovereignty: Supreme, independent political authority.
Municipal: Local. Madison uses it here to mean the state governments, as opposed to the national one.
Impious: Disrespectful toward what is sacred; wicked.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the boldest passages in the series. Many Americans in 1787 felt their first loyalty was to their state. Virginians and New Yorkers often called their state "my country." Madison, a Virginian, tells them that states are just instruments for the people's happiness, and if the instrument doesn't work, it should be changed.

Notice how he uses the language of the Revolution against the Anti-Federalists. Americans had fought against the idea that people exist to serve a king. Madison says the Anti-Federalists are making the same error when they put state power ahead of the people's welfare.
:::

## History shows the states are more likely to win {orig=3-5}

Several important points made in earlier essays argue against the idea that the national government will gradually destroy the state governments. The more I think about it, the more convinced I am that the balance is much more likely to be upset by the states becoming too powerful than by the national government becoming too powerful.

In every example of ancient and modern confederacies, we have seen a strong and constant tendency for the members to strip the central government of its powers. The central government has had very little ability to defend itself against these attacks. Most of those systems were so different from the one proposed here that we can't draw strong conclusions from their fate. But since the states will keep a very large share of active sovereignty under the new Constitution, the lesson shouldn't be ignored completely.

In the Achaean League of ancient Greece, the central government probably had a degree and kind of power that made it quite similar to the government the Convention has designed. The Lycian Confederacy, as far as we know its principles and form, must have been even more similar. Yet history doesn't tell us that either of them ever turned into, or started turning into, a single consolidated government. On the contrary, we know that one of them was ruined because the central authority couldn't prevent the disputes, and finally the breakup, of its members.

These cases deserve special attention because outside pressures pushing their members together were much more numerous and powerful than in our case. That means weaker ties on the inside would have been enough to hold them together. Yet even so, they fell apart rather than merging.

We have seen the same tendency in the feudal system. The local lords often lacked the people's affection, and in some cases the king had it. Even so, the local lords usually won their power struggles with the king. If outside dangers hadn't forced unity and obedience, and especially if the local lords had won the people's loyalty, the great kingdoms of Europe would today be made up of as many independent princes as there once were feudal barons.

:::vocab
Achaean League: An alliance of Greek city-states (about 280–146 BC) that was eventually conquered by Rome.
Lycian Confederacy: An ancient union of cities in what is now southern Turkey. Montesquieu praised it as a model, partly because larger cities got more votes.
Feudal system: The way medieval Europe was organized. Kings granted land to lords (barons) in exchange for loyalty and military service. The lords often became more powerful than the kings.
Consolidated government: A single, unified government that has absorbed all local governments.
:::

:::commentary
Madison is making the same argument Hamilton made in [No. 17](/papers/17/): in history, local powers usually beat central ones. The history of medieval Europe supported this. Kings spent centuries struggling to control powerful nobles.

But Madison's prediction turned out to be only half right. For the first part of American history, the states were very powerful, and states' rights arguments led eventually to secession and civil war. After the Civil War, and especially in the 1900s, the national government grew far beyond anything Madison imagined. Today it is much more powerful relative to the states than in 1788.
:::

## Why the states have the advantage {orig=6-7}

The state governments will have the advantage over the national government in every way we can compare them: how much each depends on the other, how much personal influence each has, what powers each holds, which one the people prefer and will likely support, and the will and ability of each to resist and block the other's actions.

The state governments can be seen as essential building blocks of the national government, but the national government is in no way essential to the operation or organization of the states.

Without the state legislatures, the President of the United States can't be elected at all. They will always have a large role in choosing him, and in most cases they will probably decide it themselves. The Senate will be elected entirely by the state legislatures. Even the House of Representatives, though elected directly by the people, will be chosen largely under the influence of the same kind of people whose influence gets them elected to the state legislatures.

So each of the main branches of the national government will owe its existence, more or less, to the favor of the state governments. It will naturally feel dependent on them, which is much more likely to make it too eager to please them than too overbearing. On the other side, no part of the state governments will owe its appointment directly to the national government, and very little, if at all, to the local influence of national officials.

:::commentary
Some of these advantages no longer exist. The **17th Amendment** (1913) took the election of senators away from state legislatures and gave it to the voters. Today, every state legislature lets the people choose the state's presidential electors, so legislatures no longer pick electors themselves, as several did in early elections. Both changes reduced the states' influence over the national government. Some people argue this weakened federalism, because senators no longer answer directly to state governments. Others say it made the national government more democratic.
:::

## The states will have more people and more influence {orig=8}

Far fewer people will work for the United States government than for the states. So the national government will have less personal influence than the states.

Think of all the members of the legislative, executive, and judicial branches of thirteen or more states. Add the justices of the peace, militia officers, court officials, and all the county, city, and town officials serving more than three million people. They are spread among the people and personally know every class and group. They must outnumber, by an enormous margin, everyone who will work for the national government, and they will have far more influence.

Compare the members of the three branches in the thirteen states, not even counting justices of the peace, with the members of the three branches of the single national government. Compare the militia officers of three million people with the officers of any army and navy that the national government could possibly have. In this respect alone, we can say the states' advantage is decisive.

If the national government has tax collectors, so will the states. The national collectors will mostly be at the seaports and not very numerous, while the state collectors will be spread throughout the country in large numbers. So the advantage is on the states' side here too.

It's true that the national government will have, and may use, the power to collect internal taxes as well as import taxes throughout the states. But it will probably use this power only to raise extra revenue when needed. When it does, the states will probably be given the option to pay their share by collecting it themselves first. And when the national government does collect directly, it will usually use state officers and follow state rules.

In fact, it's very likely that in other areas too, especially in organizing the courts, state officials will be given the matching national authority. But even if the national government did appoint its own internal tax collectors, their influence wouldn't compare with that of the crowd of state officials on the other side. In every district assigned to a national collector, there would be at least thirty or forty state officials of various kinds, many of them respected and influential people, whose influence would be on the side of the state.

:::commentary
In 1788, Madison was clearly right about numbers. The first national government had only a few hundred employees outside the military. Today, the federal government employs about three million civilians, while state and local governments employ around twenty million people. So by Madison's measure, the states still have more personnel. But the national government's money gives it enormous influence: it sends hundreds of billions of dollars to the states each year, often with conditions attached.

His prediction that the national government would rely on state officials to collect taxes didn't come true. The national government built its own tax system. But his idea of using state officials to carry out national programs did come true in other ways. Today, states run many national programs, like Medicaid and unemployment insurance, using federal money.
:::

## "Few and defined" vs. "numerous and indefinite" {orig=9-10}

The powers the proposed Constitution gives to the national government are few and defined. The powers that will remain with the state governments are numerous and indefinite.

The national powers will be used mainly on outside matters, such as war, peace, negotiations with other countries, and foreign trade. The power to tax will mostly be connected with foreign trade. The powers kept by the states will cover everything that, in the ordinary course of life, concerns the lives, liberties, and property of the people, and the internal order, improvement, and prosperity of the state.

The national government's work will be most extensive and important in times of war and danger. The state governments' work will matter most in times of peace and safety. Since times of war will probably be a small fraction of the whole, the states will have another advantage here. In fact, the better the national government is equipped for national defense, the less often there will be dangerous times that might let it gain the upper hand over the states.

:::quote
The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government, are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite.

Meaning: The national government gets only a short list of specific powers. The states keep a long, open-ended list covering most of everyday life.
:::

:::vocab
Delegated powers: Powers given (delegated) to the national government by the Constitution.
Reserved powers: Powers kept by the states or the people. The 10th Amendment describes them.
:::

:::commentary
This is the most quoted line in the essay, and it is still cited in Supreme Court cases about the limits of national power. In *United States v. Lopez* (1995), for example, the Court quoted it when it struck down a federal law banning guns near schools, ruling that Congress had gone beyond its power to regulate interstate commerce.

Madison's observation that national power grows in wartime proved very true. The Civil War, World War I, World War II, and the Cold War each greatly expanded the national government, and it never shrank back to its earlier size. Madison hoped strong national defense would make wars *rarer*, so the national government would rarely grow. History didn't fully cooperate.
:::

## Mostly old powers, made to work {orig=11}

If the new Constitution is examined carefully and honestly, we'll find that the change it proposes is much less about adding NEW POWERS to the Union than about strengthening its ORIGINAL POWERS.

The power to regulate trade is new, it's true. But few people oppose this addition, and no one seems worried about it. The powers relating to war and peace, armies and navies, treaties and finance, and the other major powers all already belong to the current Congress under the Articles of Confederation. The proposed change doesn't expand these powers. It only replaces them with a more effective way of carrying them out.

The change in taxation may be the most important. Yet the current Congress already has complete authority to REQUIRE the states to provide unlimited amounts of money for the common defense and general welfare, just as the future Congress will have authority to require it from individual citizens. And individual citizens will be no more obligated to pay their assigned taxes than the states have been to pay their assigned shares.

If the states had promptly obeyed the Articles of Confederation, or if their obedience could have been enforced as peacefully as it can be with individual citizens, our past experience gives no reason to think the state governments would have lost their constitutional powers and gradually been merged into one. To claim that would have happened is to say that the state governments can't survive under *any* system that accomplishes the essential purposes of the Union.

:::commentary
This is a clever argument. The Articles of Confederation already gave Congress power over war, treaties, armies, and money. The problem was that Congress had to *ask* the states to carry out its decisions, and the states often refused. The Constitution mostly fixes that problem by letting the national government act directly on citizens. So, Madison says, if you aren't afraid of the powers Congress already has, you shouldn't be afraid of them working properly.

Critics would answer that power that actually works is very different from power on paper. A Congress that can collect taxes directly is much more powerful than one that can only send requests. Madison's argument is persuasive, but it plays down how big the change really was.
:::

:::today
Americans still argue about whether national powers should be "few and defined." Over two centuries, the national government's role has grown into areas Madison assigned to the states, like education, health care, crime, and the economy. Some people see this as a necessary response to a modern, connected country. Others see it as exactly the consolidation the Anti-Federalists feared. Supreme Court cases about the limits of Congress's power, and political debates over "states' rights," keep returning to this essay.
:::
