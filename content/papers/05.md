:::summary
In his last essay on foreign dangers, John Jay looks to British history. England, Scotland, and Wales share one island, yet for centuries they were separate nations constantly at war, and foreign powers kept their rivalries burning. Queen Anne urged England and Scotland to unite in 1706 so the whole island could "resist all its enemies." Jay predicts that if America split into three or four confederacies, the same thing would happen. One would grow stronger, the others would fear and envy it, and they would distrust each other. Their trade and interests would differ, so they would seek different foreign allies, and foreign armies invited in as "allies" might never leave. Divided, Americans would be "formidable only to each other."
:::

:::keypoints
- **Queen Anne** urged England and Scotland to unite in 1706, saying union would secure "religion, liberty, and property" and help the island "resist all its enemies."
- Britain's three nations shared one island and the same real interests, yet were **almost always at war**, encouraged by foreign powers.
- Separate American confederacies would become **jealous rivals** in the same way.
- They wouldn't stay **equal** in strength for long. Whichever grew strongest would be feared and envied.
- The **North** would likely become the strongest, alarming the South.
- Separate confederacies would have **different trade interests** and **different foreign friends**, so alliances among them would be weak.
- Each would be more likely to seek **foreign allies against the others** than to unite against foreign enemies.
- Foreign armies invited in as allies are **hard to get rid of**. Rome conquered many nations while claiming to protect them.
- Divided, Americans would be **"formidable only to each other."**
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of John Jay's four essays on foreign dangers (Nos. 2–5). Opponents of the Constitution had floated the idea of splitting the country into three or four regional confederacies, such as New England, the Middle States, and the South. Jay argues that this would repeat the bloody history of the British Isles. After this essay, Jay became ill, and Hamilton took over the series. Jay returned only once, to write [No. 64](/papers/64/) on treaties.
:::

## Queen Anne's advice {orig=1}

In her letter of July 1, 1706, to the Scottish Parliament, Queen Anne made some points about the importance of the UNION then being formed between England and Scotland. They deserve our attention. Here are one or two passages from it:

> "An entire and perfect union will be the solid foundation of lasting peace: It will secure your religion, liberty, and property; remove the animosities amongst yourselves, and the jealousies and differences betwixt our two kingdoms. It must increase your strength, riches, and trade; and by this union the whole island, being joined in affection and free from all apprehensions of different interest, will be ENABLED TO RESIST ALL ITS ENEMIES."

> "We most earnestly recommend to you calmness and unanimity in this great and weighty affair, that the union may be brought to a happy conclusion, being the only EFFECTUAL way to secure our present and future happiness, and disappoint the designs of our and your enemies, who will doubtless, on this occasion, USE THEIR UTMOST ENDEAVORS TO PREVENT OR DELAY THIS UNION."

:::vocab
Queen Anne: Queen of England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1702 to 1714. Under her rule, England and Scotland united in 1707 to form the Kingdom of Great Britain.
Animosities: Strong feelings of hostility.
:::

:::commentary
Jay chose this quote carefully. Queen Anne's situation looked a lot like America's. England and Scotland had shared a monarch since 1603 but kept separate parliaments, laws, and trade rules. The 1707 Act of Union merged them into one nation. Notice her warning that enemies would "use their utmost endeavors to prevent or delay this union." Jay is hinting that foreign powers would be happy to see America's Constitution fail.

It's a little ironic for an American revolutionary to quote a British monarch with approval only a few years after the Revolution. But Jay's point is that Britain's union is exactly what made it strong enough to be such a dangerous enemy.
:::

## The lesson of British history {orig=2-4}

The last essay noted that weakness and division at home would invite danger from abroad, and that nothing would protect us better than union, strength, and good government among ourselves. This subject is rich and hard to exhaust.

British history is the one most of us know best, and it teaches us many useful lessons. We can benefit from their experience without paying the price it cost them.

Common sense says that the people of a single island like theirs should be one nation. Yet for centuries they were divided into three, and those three were almost always caught up in quarrels and wars with each other. Their real interests toward the nations of continental Europe were actually the same. But the tricks, policies, and schemes of those nations kept their jealousy of each other constantly burning. For many years, they were far more of a nuisance and a burden to each other than a help.

If the American people divided into three or four nations, wouldn't the same thing happen? Wouldn't similar jealousies arise, and be fed in the same way? Instead of being "joined in affection," free from all fear of "different interests," they would soon find that envy and jealousy had destroyed trust and affection. Each confederacy would pursue its own narrow interests instead of the interests of all America. And like most other nations that share BORDERS, they would always be either fighting over disputes and wars, or living in constant fear of them.

:::commentary
England conquered Wales in the 1280s, and England and Scotland fought on and off for centuries. France often allied with Scotland against England, an arrangement known as the "Auld Alliance." That's the kind of foreign meddling Jay means. He worries that France, Spain, or Britain would play separate American confederacies against each other in the same way.
:::

## The confederacies wouldn't stay equal {orig=5-7}

Even the most hopeful supporters of three or four confederacies can't reasonably expect them to stay exactly equal in strength for long, even if they could start out that way. And if that were possible, what human invention could keep them equal?

Besides the local circumstances that help power grow in one place and slow it in another, we must consider the effect of better policy and management. One confederacy's government would probably be run better than the others, and that would destroy their equality in strength and importance. We can't assume that each of these confederacies would show the same level of wise policy, caution, and foresight, year after year.

Whenever, and for whatever reason, one of these nations or confederacies rose well above its neighbors in political importance, and it would, its neighbors would immediately see it with envy and fear. Both of those feelings would lead them to support, if not encourage, anything that promised to weaken it. They would also hold back from anything that might help it prosper, or even keep it secure.

It wouldn't take long for that nation to notice these unfriendly attitudes. It would soon begin not only to lose trust in its neighbors, but to feel just as unfriendly toward them. Distrust naturally creates distrust. Nothing changes goodwill and kind behavior faster than spiteful jealousy and unfair accusations, whether spoken or implied.

The North is generally the region of strength. Many local circumstances make it likely that the northernmost of the proposed confederacies would, before very long, be clearly more powerful than any of the others. As soon as this became obvious, the NORTHERN HIVE would stir up the same feelings in the southern parts of America that it once stirred up in the southern parts of Europe. And it doesn't seem a wild guess that its young swarms might often be tempted to gather honey in the richer fields and milder climate of their more luxurious and delicate neighbors.

:::quote
Distrust naturally creates distrust...

Meaning: When one side becomes suspicious, the other side becomes suspicious in return, and the cycle keeps growing.
:::

:::vocab
Northern hive: Jay's image of the North as a beehive sending out "swarms." Europeans used this phrase for the northern peoples, such as the Goths and Vikings, who invaded southern Europe.
:::

:::commentary
Jay's "northern hive" image refers to the invasions of the Roman Empire by Germanic peoples from the north, and later Viking raids. He imagines a powerful Northern confederacy tempted to invade the warmer, wealthier South.

Jay's general prediction, that North and South would become rivals, came true in a way he didn't describe here. The deep division between North and South was over slavery. It grew for decades and led to the Civil War in 1861, when Southern states tried to form their own separate confederacy, just the kind of breakup Jay warned against. In that war, it was the stronger North that marched south.
:::

## Alliances between confederacies wouldn't work {orig=8-10}

Anyone who studies the history of similar divisions and confederacies will find plenty of reason to fear that these confederacies would be neighbors only in the sense of sharing borders. They wouldn't love or trust each other. Instead, they would suffer from conflict, jealousy, and mutual harm. In short, they would put us in exactly the position that some nations surely want to see us in: FORMIDABLE ONLY TO EACH OTHER.

So those gentlemen are badly mistaken who think the confederacies could form alliances for attack and defense, and that these alliances would produce the unity of will, weapons, and resources needed to keep them strongly defended against foreign enemies.

When did the independent states that Britain and Spain were once divided into ever form such an alliance, or unite their forces against a foreign enemy?

The proposed confederacies would be SEPARATE NATIONS. Each would have to regulate its trade with foreign countries through its own treaties. Since their products are different and suited to different markets, those treaties would be very different. Different trade concerns create different interests, and so different degrees of political attachment to different foreign nations.

So it might well happen, and probably would, that the foreign nation the SOUTHERN confederacy was at war with would be the very one the NORTHERN confederacy most wanted to stay friendly with. An alliance so against their immediate interests wouldn't be easy to form. And if it were formed, it wouldn't be kept in perfect good faith.

:::quote
...they would place us exactly in the situations in which some nations doubtless wish to see us, viz., FORMIDABLE ONLY TO EACH OTHER.

Meaning: Divided, the American states would be dangerous only to one another, not to any foreign power. That's exactly what America's rivals would want. "Viz." means "namely."
:::

:::commentary
Jay's point about different trade interests was well founded. Southern states exported tobacco, rice, and indigo, much of it to Britain and France. Northern states depended on shipping, fishing, and manufacturing, and competed with Britain. Their interests often pulled in opposite directions. This same economic difference later fed fierce fights over tariffs (import taxes), which Northern manufacturers wanted and Southern exporters hated.

The divided kingdoms of Spain that Jay mentions, such as Castile, Aragon, and Navarre, were united over the 1400s and 1500s, partly through the marriage of Ferdinand and Isabella.
:::

## Foreign "allies" might never leave {orig=11-12}

In fact, it's far more likely that in America, as in Europe, neighboring nations driven by opposite interests and unfriendly feelings would often end up on opposite sides. Since we are so far from Europe, it would be more natural for these confederacies to fear danger from each other than from distant nations. So each would be more eager to protect itself against the others with the help of foreign allies than to protect itself against foreign dangers by allying with the others.

And let's not forget how much easier it is to let foreign fleets into our ports and foreign armies into our country than to persuade or force them to leave. How many conquests did the Romans and others make while calling themselves allies? And what changes did they force on the governments of the people they claimed to protect?

Let fair-minded people judge, then, whether dividing America into any number of independent nations would help protect us against attacks and improper interference by foreign nations.

:::quote
And here let us not forget how much more easy it is to receive foreign fleets into our ports, and foreign armies into our country, than it is to persuade or compel them to depart.

Meaning: Once you invite a foreign army in to help you, it may never leave, and it may end up controlling you.
:::

:::commentary
This was a real fear. During the Revolution, America had depended on French troops and ships to win. That alliance worked, but Jay knew that many nations in history had invited in a powerful "protector" and ended up ruled by it. Rome often took over regions by first coming in as an ally in a local war.

Jay's argument here connects to George Washington's famous Farewell Address of 1796, which warned against "entangling" alliances and against foreign influence in American politics.
:::

:::today
Jay's warning about divided nations being played against each other by outside powers still applies around the world. When countries split apart, as Yugoslavia did in the 1990s, the new states have sometimes ended up as rivals or even at war, with outside powers taking sides. Jay's point that alliances among separate nations are fragile when their interests differ also helps explain the difficulty of keeping groups like the European Union united. Within the United States, his argument is a reminder of what was at stake when the Union nearly broke apart in the Civil War.
:::
