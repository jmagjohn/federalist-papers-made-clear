:::summary
Any new government raises hard questions, Hamilton says, and a system that combines separate sovereign states raises even more. One question about the courts is whether the federal courts alone will hear federal cases, or whether state courts will share that power. Hamilton argues that state courts keep all the power they had before, unless the Constitution clearly takes it away. They will usually be able to hear cases arising under national laws too, unless Congress says otherwise. When they do, their decisions can be appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court. The state and national courts, he says, are meant to work together as "ONE WHOLE."
:::

:::keypoints
- A new and **compound** system will raise hard questions. Only time and experience can settle their meaning.
- The states keep all the powers they had before unless the Constitution **clearly gives them exclusively** to the national government.
- Nothing in the Constitution clearly says that federal courts **alone** can hear federal cases. So state courts keep their old jurisdiction.
- State courts can also hear cases arising under **new national laws**, unless Congress specifically assigns those cases to federal courts only.
- Decisions by state courts in federal cases can be **appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court**. Otherwise, people could dodge the federal courts just by suing in state court.
- Congress might even let people **appeal from state courts to lower federal courts**. That could reduce the number of federal courts and the Supreme Court's workload.
- The state and national court systems should be seen as **parts of one whole**, not as rivals.
:::

:::context Background
Anti-Federalists feared that the new federal courts would swallow up the state courts. If federal courts had sole power over all the cases listed in Article III, state courts could lose much of their business and importance. Others asked the opposite question: if state courts could hear federal cases, could the national government make sure they applied national law correctly?

In [No. 81](/papers/81/), Hamilton promised in a footnote that he would explain how state and federal courts would share power. This short essay keeps that promise. It applies the same rule about shared powers that Hamilton laid out earlier for taxes, in [No. 32](/papers/32/).
:::

## Hard questions in a new system {orig=1-2}

When a new government is set up, no matter how carefully and wisely the work is done, it is bound to raise complicated and delicate questions. We should especially expect such questions from a constitution that is built by fully or partly combining a number of separate sovereign states. Only time can develop and perfect so complex a system. Only time can settle the meaning of all its parts and adjust them to fit together into a harmonious and consistent WHOLE.

Sure enough, such questions have come up about the Convention's plan, especially about the courts. The main ones concern how the state courts will stand in regard to the cases placed under federal jurisdiction. Will federal power over those cases be *exclusive*, or will state courts have *concurrent* jurisdiction? If they share it, how will they relate to the national courts? Sensible people are asking these questions, and they certainly deserve attention.

:::vocab
Liquidated (meaning): Made clear and settled. Madison uses this unusual word to describe how courts and practice gradually fix the meaning of unclear laws.
Exclusive jurisdiction: Power over certain cases that belongs to one court system only.
Concurrent jurisdiction: Power over the same kinds of cases that is shared by two court systems, such as state and federal courts.
Compound: Made up of several parts. Madison called the American system a "compound republic" because it combines national and state governments.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's opening is a modest admission. The Constitution's authors could not foresee every question, and its meaning would have to be worked out over time. Madison made the same point in [No. 37](/papers/37/), using the same unusual word: the meaning of new laws must be "liquidated," or settled, through use and court decisions.

This turned out to be very true of the relationship between state and federal courts. It took decades of court cases to work out, and some of its details are still argued over today.
:::

## State courts keep their powers {orig=3-4}

The principles set out in an earlier paper teach us that the states keep all the powers they had before, unless those powers are given exclusively to the national government. That exclusive transfer happens in only three cases:

- where the Constitution expressly gives an exclusive power to the Union;
- where it gives a power to the Union and forbids the states to use a similar power; or
- where it gives a power to the Union that would be completely incompatible with a similar power in the states.

These principles may not apply as strongly to judicial power as to legislative power. But I'm inclined to think they are basically correct for both. With that in mind, I will set down this rule: the state courts will keep the jurisdiction they now have, unless it appears to be taken away in one of these three ways.

The only part of the Constitution that seems to limit federal cases to federal courts is this passage: "THE JUDICIAL POWER of the United States shall be vested in one Supreme Court, and in such inferior courts as the Congress shall from time to time ordain and establish."

This could be read in two ways. It could mean that only the Supreme Court and lower federal courts may decide the cases their authority covers. Or it could simply mean that the national court system will be made up of one Supreme Court and as many lower courts as Congress decides to create. In other words, the United States will use the judicial power given to it through one supreme court and some number of lower courts that it sets up.

The first reading shuts out shared jurisdiction for the state courts. The second reading allows it. The first reading would take power away from the states by mere implication. So the second seems to me the more natural and the more defensible interpretation.

:::note
Hamilton's footnote points to "No. 31." In the standard numbering used today, the passage listing the three ways a power can become exclusive appears in [No. 32](/papers/32/).
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is applying a general rule of federalism: when in doubt, assume the states keep their old powers. The 10th Amendment, ratified in 1791, later put a version of this idea into the Constitution: powers not delegated to the United States, nor forbidden to the states, are "reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."

Hamilton's reading also connects to a compromise at the Constitutional Convention. Some delegates wanted the Constitution to require lower federal courts. Others thought state courts could handle federal cases, with appeals to the Supreme Court. The Convention settled the question by letting Congress decide whether to create lower courts at all. That only makes sense if state courts could hear federal cases too.
:::

:::vocab
Reserved powers: Powers kept by the states or the people. The 10th Amendment describes them.
Alienation (of power): Transferring a power to someone else, so the original holder loses it.
:::

## State courts can hear cases under national laws {orig=5}

But this idea of shared jurisdiction clearly applies only to the kinds of cases that state courts already heard before. It is less clear for cases that arise from, and are special to, the new Constitution. Refusing state courts power over those cases could hardly be called cutting back a power they already had.

So I don't claim that Congress, when it passes laws on the subjects given to it, can't send the cases arising under a particular law to the federal courts alone, if that seems wise. But I do hold that state courts will lose no part of their original jurisdiction, except as it relates to appeals. I even believe that state courts will naturally hear cases arising under future acts of Congress, in every case where those acts don't expressly exclude them.

I draw this conclusion from the nature of judicial power and from the general character of the system. Every government's judicial power reaches beyond its own local laws. In civil cases, it covers all disputes between parties within its jurisdiction, even if the dispute involves the laws of the most distant part of the world. The laws of Japan, as well as those of New York, may become the subject of legal arguments in our courts.

Add to this the fact that the state governments and the national government are really related systems and parts of ONE WHOLE. Then the conclusion seems certain: state courts would share jurisdiction over all cases arising under the laws of the Union, unless it was expressly forbidden.

:::quote
...the State governments and the national governments, as they truly are, in the light of kindred systems, and as parts of ONE WHOLE...

Meaning: The state and national governments are not rivals or foreign powers. They are related parts of a single system, so their courts can work together.
:::

:::commentary
This is close to how the system works today. State courts hear an enormous number of cases involving federal law and the U.S. Constitution. For example, a person on trial for a state crime can argue in state court that police violated their rights under the 4th Amendment. The Constitution itself, in the Supremacy Clause, says that state judges are bound by federal law.

As Hamilton expected, Congress has made some kinds of cases exclusively federal. Prosecutions for federal crimes, bankruptcy cases, and patent cases, for example, can be heard only in federal courts. But the general rule is the one Hamilton described: unless Congress says otherwise, state courts can hear claims under federal law. The Supreme Court has cited this essay in support of that rule.
:::

:::vocab
Plaintiff: The person who brings a lawsuit.
Prosecutor: The government lawyer who brings criminal charges against someone.
:::

## Appeals from state courts to the Supreme Court {orig=6}

This raises another question. In these cases of shared jurisdiction, how would the national courts and the state courts relate to each other? My answer is that appeals would certainly go from the state courts to the Supreme Court of the United States.

The Constitution directly gives the Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction in all the listed federal cases where it doesn't have original jurisdiction. It doesn't contain a single word limiting this to appeals from the lower federal courts. It focuses only on the *kinds of cases* that can be appealed, not on *which courts* they come from. Because of this, and because of the reasoning behind it, the power should be read to include appeals from state courts.

Only two other possibilities exist. One is that state courts must be shut out from sharing jurisdiction over national matters. The other is that the Union's judicial authority could be avoided whenever any plaintiff or prosecutor chose, simply by going to state court. Neither result should be accepted without clear necessity. The second would be completely unacceptable. It would defeat some of the most important and openly stated purposes of the proposed government, and it would seriously obstruct its work. And I see no basis for such an assumption.

As I said before, the national and state systems should be seen as ONE WHOLE. The state courts will naturally be helpers in carrying out the laws of the Union. And appeals from them will just as naturally go to the court meant to unite the principles of national justice and make the rules of national decisions consistent.

The clear aim of the Convention's plan is that, for important public reasons, all cases of the listed kinds will be decided either first or finally in the courts of the Union. So it would go against every sound rule of interpretation to limit the general words giving the Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction to appeals from lower federal courts. That would narrow the broad meaning of the words and defeat their purpose.

:::vocab
Appellate jurisdiction: The power to review a case already decided by a lower court, and to confirm, change, or reverse that decision.
Original jurisdiction: The power to hear a case first, as a trial court, rather than on appeal.
Auxiliaries: Helpers or supporting forces.
:::

:::commentary
The first Congress agreed with Hamilton. The Judiciary Act of 1789 allowed the Supreme Court to review decisions of the highest state courts in cases involving federal law. But not every state accepted this. Virginia's highest court refused, arguing that the Supreme Court had no power over a sovereign state's courts.

The Supreme Court rejected that view in two famous cases. In *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee* (1816), Justice Joseph Story ruled that the Supreme Court could review state court decisions on federal questions. Like Hamilton, he stressed that federal law needed one uniform meaning across the country. In *Cohens v. Virginia* (1821), Chief Justice John Marshall extended the same rule to criminal cases. These decisions put Hamilton's argument into practice. Today, a case can begin in a small-town state court and end up at the U.S. Supreme Court if it raises a question of federal law.
:::

## Appeals from state courts to lower federal courts? {orig=7}

But could appeals also go from state courts to the lower federal courts? This is another question that has been raised, and a harder one than the last.

The following points support a "yes." First, the Convention's plan allows Congress "to constitute tribunals inferior to the Supreme Court." Second, it declares that "the JUDICIAL POWER of the United States shall be vested in one Supreme Court, and in such inferior courts as Congress shall ordain and establish." Then it lists the cases this judicial power will cover. After that, it divides the Supreme Court's jurisdiction into original and appellate, but it doesn't define the jurisdiction of the lower courts at all. The only limits it sets for them are that they must be "inferior to the Supreme Court" and must stay within the boundaries of federal judicial power. It doesn't say whether their authority will be original, appellate, or both. All of this seems to be left to Congress's judgment.

If that is the case, I see no obstacle for now to allowing appeals from state courts to lower national courts. And we can imagine many advantages in having the power to do so. It would reduce the need to create more and more federal courts. It would also allow arrangements that shrink the Supreme Court's appellate workload. State courts could then be given fuller charge of federal cases. And in most cases where appeals seemed proper, they could go from the state courts to the federal district courts, instead of to the Supreme Court.

:::commentary
Hamilton's idea here never became law. Congress has never allowed ordinary appeals from state courts to lower federal courts. Today, the general rule is that only the U.S. Supreme Court can review a state court's judgment. A lower federal court cannot act as an appeals court over a state court.

There is one major exception in practice. Under the writ of habeas corpus, a person imprisoned by a state can ask a federal district court to decide whether the conviction violated the U.S. Constitution. Congress greatly expanded this power after the Civil War, when it worried that Southern state courts would not protect the rights of formerly enslaved people. Since then, people have debated how much federal judges should second-guess state courts. Some say federal review protects constitutional rights. Others say it disrespects state courts and drags out cases for years.
:::

:::vocab
District court: A federal trial court. Each state has at least one federal district.
Writ of habeas corpus: A court order requiring the government to bring a prisoner before a judge and prove that the imprisonment is legal. Latin for "you shall have the body."
:::

:::today
Most Americans who go to court on a federal question never set foot in a federal courthouse. State courts handle the vast majority of the country's legal cases, and they regularly apply the U.S. Constitution and federal laws. Hamilton's picture of state and federal courts as "parts of ONE WHOLE," with the Supreme Court at the top to keep federal law uniform, describes how the system works today. The questions he raised also remain alive. People still argue about when a case should be in federal rather than state court, and how much federal judges should review the work of state judges.
:::
