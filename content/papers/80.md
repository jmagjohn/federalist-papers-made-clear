:::summary
Hamilton asks which kinds of cases the federal courts should decide. He lists six types: cases under national laws, cases about enforcing the Constitution, cases involving the United States itself, cases that affect peace with foreign nations or among the states, cases at sea, and cases where state courts can't be trusted to be fair. For each one, he explains why a national court is needed. Then he checks the Constitution's actual list of federal court powers against these principles and finds that they match. Where small problems turn up, he says, Congress has the power to fix them.
:::

:::keypoints
- The Constitution's limits on the states mean nothing without a way to **enforce** them. Federal courts can overrule state laws that clearly violate the Constitution.
- National laws need **one consistent interpretation**. Thirteen separate final courts would be "a hydra," a many-headed monster producing confusion.
- **The peace of the whole should not be left to a part.** The nation answers to foreign countries, so national courts should hear cases involving foreigners.
- Federal courts should settle disputes **between states** and **between citizens of different states**, because no state court would be neutral.
- **Admiralty** cases, about ships and the sea, belong in national courts because they often involve foreigners and international law.
- No one should be **a judge in his own case**. So when two states claim the same land, neither state's courts should decide.
- Federal courts need power in **equity** as well as law, so they can deal fairly with unfair contracts and similar problems.
- If any of these powers causes problems, **Congress can make exceptions and rules** to fix them.
:::

:::context Background
Article III, Section 2 of the Constitution lists the kinds of cases federal courts can hear. Anti-Federalists, especially the writer "Brutus," attacked this list. They feared that federal courts would gradually swallow up the state courts, drag ordinary people to distant courtrooms, and leave the states with little real power.

In this essay, Hamilton answers by working from principles. First he explains what kinds of cases a national court system *should* hear. Then he shows that the Constitution's list fits those principles. The next three essays deal with the Supreme Court ([No. 81](/papers/81/)), the relationship between state and federal courts ([No. 82](/papers/82/)), and jury trials ([No. 83](/papers/83/)).
:::

## Six kinds of cases for national courts {orig=1-2}

To judge accurately how far the federal courts' power should reach, we first need to ask what their proper purposes are.

It hardly seems open to debate that the judicial power of the Union should reach the following kinds of cases:

1. Cases that arise from laws of the United States passed under Congress's proper constitutional powers.
2. Cases about carrying out the provisions written directly into the Constitution.
3. Cases in which the United States is a party.
4. Cases that involve the PEACE of the CONFEDERACY, whether they concern relations between the United States and foreign nations or relations among the states themselves.
5. Cases that begin on the high seas and fall under admiralty or maritime law.
6. Cases in which state courts can't be expected to be fair and unbiased.

:::vocab
Jurisdiction: The area of authority of a government or court. For a court, it means the kinds of cases it has the power to hear and decide.
Judiciary: The branch of government made up of courts and judges.
Admiralty: The branch of law dealing with ships, sailors, and events at sea, such as shipwrecks, cargo disputes, and ships captured in war. Also called **maritime** law.
Confederacy: A union of states. Hamilton uses it here simply to mean the United States as a whole.
:::

## Enforcing the Constitution and national law {orig=3-5}

The first point rests on a simple idea. There should always be a constitutional way to make the Constitution's rules actually work. For example, what good would limits on the state legislatures be without some constitutional way of enforcing them?

Under the Convention's plan, the states are forbidden to do a number of things. Some of these would harm the interests of the Union, and others would violate the principles of good government. A tax on imported goods is an example of the first kind, and printing paper money is an example of the second. No sensible person will believe these bans would be carefully obeyed unless the government had some effective power to stop or correct violations.

That power must take one of two forms. Either Congress gets a direct veto over state laws, or the federal courts get the authority to overrule state laws that clearly conflict with the Constitution. I can't imagine a third way. The Convention seems to have preferred the second option to the first, and I expect the states will like it better too.

As for the second point, no argument or explanation could make it clearer than it already is. If there are any political axioms, one of them is surely this: a government's judicial power should reach as far as its legislative power. The simple need for national laws to be interpreted the same way everywhere settles the question. Thirteen independent courts, each with the final say over the same kinds of cases under the same laws, would be a hydra in government. Nothing could come from it but contradiction and confusion.

Even less needs to be said about the third point. Disputes between the nation and its states or citizens can only properly be sent to national courts. Any other plan would go against reason, against precedent, and against basic decency.

:::quote
Thirteen independent courts of final jurisdiction over the same causes, arising upon the same laws, is a hydra in government, from which nothing but contradiction and confusion can proceed.

Meaning: If each state's highest court had the last word on what national laws mean, the same law could mean thirteen different things. Someone has to have the final say.
:::

:::vocab
Axiom: A statement so obvious it is accepted as true without proof.
Hydra: A monster in Greek mythology with many heads. When one head was cut off, two more grew back.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's mention of "a direct negative on the State laws" refers to a real debate at the Constitutional Convention. James Madison wanted Congress to have a veto over any state law. The Convention rejected that idea. Instead, it adopted the Supremacy Clause, which makes the Constitution and national laws "the supreme Law of the Land" and binds state judges to follow them. Courts, not Congress, would police the states.

This system started working almost at once. The Judiciary Act of 1789 allowed the Supreme Court to review state court decisions that rejected a claim based on federal law. Through this power, the Supreme Court has struck down many state laws over the years. Hamilton's concern about "uniformity," meaning one national meaning for national law, is still one of the main reasons the Supreme Court exists.
:::

## Keeping the peace with foreign nations {orig=6}

The fourth point rests on a plain principle: the peace of the WHOLE should not be left in the hands of a PART. The Union will certainly be held responsible by foreign countries for how its states behave. And whoever is responsible for an injury should also have the power to prevent it.

Unjust rulings by courts, like other kinds of injustice, are rightly considered a fair reason for war. So the federal courts should have authority over all cases involving citizens of other countries. This is just as important for keeping the nation's promises as for keeping the peace.

Someone might try to draw a line here. Cases arising under treaties and the law of nations could go to federal courts, while cases based only on a state's own local law could stay in state courts. But it's at least doubtful that this distinction makes sense. Suppose a state court gave an unjust ruling against a foreigner in a case that involved only local law, and the wrong was never corrected. That might still count as an attack on the foreigner's government, just as much as a ruling that broke a treaty or the law of nations.

An even bigger objection is that it would be extremely hard, perhaps impossible, to tell the two kinds of cases apart in practice. Such a large share of cases involving foreigners raise national questions that the safest and wisest course is to send all of them to national courts.

:::vocab
Law of nations: The rules that govern how countries treat each other and each other's citizens. Today we call it *international law*.
Municipal law: A nation's or state's own internal law, as opposed to international law. (*Lex loci*, Latin for "the law of the place," means the local law where something happened.)
Treaty: A formal agreement between nations.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton had a real problem in mind. The 1783 peace treaty with Britain promised that British creditors could collect debts owed to them by Americans. But several state legislatures and courts blocked those collections. Britain used this as a reason to keep its troops in forts on American soil in the Northwest. Hamilton's point is that the whole country paid the price when one state broke the nation's promises. In [No. 3](/papers/03/), John Jay made a similar argument about the dangers of letting states handle relations with foreign nations.
:::

## Settling disputes between states and their citizens {orig=7-9}

The power to decide cases between two states, between a state and citizens of another state, and between citizens of different states is perhaps just as essential to the peace of the Union.

History paints a horrible picture of the conflicts and private wars that tore apart and ruined Germany before Emperor Maximilian created the Imperial Chamber near the end of the 1400s. History also tells us how much that institution did to calm the disorder and bring peace to the empire. It was a court with the authority to make final decisions in all disputes among the members of the German empire.

Even our current imperfect system, the Articles of Confederation, gives the national government a way to end territorial disputes between the states. But boundary claims are not the only source of quarrels and hostility among the states. We have seen some of these other sources in our own recent experience. You can probably guess that I mean the unfair laws that too many states have passed. The proposed Constitution includes specific protections against repeating the kinds of laws we have already seen. But we can reasonably expect that the spirit behind them will take on new forms that no one could foresee or specifically guard against. Any practice that tends to disturb the harmony between the states is a proper subject for federal oversight and control.

The Constitution says that "the citizens of each State shall be entitled to all the privileges and immunities of citizens of the several States." This may be considered the foundation of the Union. It is a sound principle that every government should have the means to carry out its own rules by its own authority. So, to firmly protect the equal privileges and immunities that all citizens of the Union will have, the national courts should preside over every case in which one state or its citizens are on the opposite side from another state or its citizens.

To make sure this basic rule takes full effect, without being dodged or evaded, its interpretation should be given to a court that has no local loyalties. Such a court will likely be impartial between the different states and their citizens. And since it owes its existence to the Union, it will never be likely to feel any bias against the principles the Union is built on.

:::vocab
Privileges and immunities: Basic rights and protections of citizens. The Constitution's Privileges and Immunities Clause (Article IV) means a state can't treat citizens of other states as second-class.
Impartial: Fair; not favoring either side.
:::

:::commentary
The "Imperial Chamber" was a high court created in 1495 for the Holy Roman Empire, a loose union of German states. Publius often uses European confederacies as warnings or examples (see [No. 19](/papers/19/)).

The Articles of Confederation did let Congress set up special courts to settle land disputes between states. The best-known case was a fight between Pennsylvania and Connecticut over the Wyoming Valley, which such a court decided in Pennsylvania's favor in 1782. By "fraudulent laws," Hamilton means state laws that helped debtors at the expense of creditors, such as paper money that lost value and laws delaying debt payments. Article I, Section 10 of the Constitution bans states from printing paper money and from passing laws "impairing the Obligation of Contracts."

Cases between citizens of different states are now called **diversity cases**. Today, Congress lets federal courts hear them only when more than $75,000 is at stake. The idea is still Hamilton's: a person sued in another state's courts might not get a fair hearing.
:::

## Cases at sea, and cases needing a neutral judge {orig=10-11}

The fifth point needs little comment. Even the most devoted worshipers of state power have not so far tried to deny the national courts power over maritime cases. These cases depend so much on the law of nations, and so often affect the rights of foreigners, that they fall under the same reasoning about keeping the public peace. Under the present Confederation, the most important of them already go to the national government.

The sixth point, about using national courts when state courts can't be expected to be fair, speaks for itself. Certainly no one should be the judge in his own case, or in any case where he has the slightest interest or bias. This principle is a strong reason for making federal courts the place to settle disputes between different states and their citizens.

It should also apply to some cases between citizens of the *same* state. Claims to land based on grants from different states, where the states disagree about their borders, are of this kind. The courts of neither state could be expected to be unbiased. The state's laws might even have decided the question in advance, forcing its courts to rule in favor of the state's own land grants. And even where that hadn't happened, the judges, being human, would naturally lean strongly toward their own government's claims.

:::commentary
Under the Articles, Congress set up a Court of Appeals in Cases of Capture. It handled appeals about ships captured at sea during the Revolutionary War. This is the "most important part" of maritime cases that Hamilton says the Confederation already handled.

Hamilton's readers in New York would have understood his land-grant example right away. For years, both New York and New Hampshire had granted land in the area that became Vermont. People holding grants from different states claimed the same land, and the conflict had turned violent. Vermont joined the Union as the 14th state in 1791.
:::

## Testing the Constitution's list {orig=12-14}

Having laid out and discussed the principles that should shape the federal court system, we will now test the specific powers the Convention's plan gives it. According to the Constitution, the federal judicial power will cover:

> "all cases in law and equity arising under the Constitution, the laws of the United States, and treaties made, or which shall be made, under their authority; to all cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers, and consuls; to all cases of admiralty and maritime jurisdiction; to controversies to which the United States shall be a party; to controversies between two or more States; between a State and citizens of another State; between citizens of different States; between citizens of the same State claiming lands and grants of different States; and between a State or the citizens thereof and foreign states, citizens, and subjects."

This is the entire body of the Union's judicial power. Let's look at it piece by piece. It will extend:

**First, to all cases in law and equity arising under the Constitution and the laws of the United States.** This matches the first two kinds of cases I listed as proper for federal courts.

Some have asked what the difference is between "cases arising under the Constitution" and cases "arising under the laws of the United States." The difference has already been explained. All the limits on state legislatures provide examples. For instance, states may not print paper money. That ban comes from the Constitution itself and has nothing to do with any law passed by Congress. If a state printed paper money anyway, the disputes about it would be cases arising under the Constitution, not under the laws of the United States, in the ordinary meaning of those words. This one example stands for all the rest.

Others have asked why the word "equity" is needed. What cases of equity could grow out of the Constitution and national laws? In fact, almost any lawsuit between individuals may involve fraud, accident, trust, or hardship. Those elements would make it a matter for equity rather than ordinary law, as the distinction is known and practiced in several states.

For example, it is the special job of a court of equity to give relief against what are called hard bargains. These are contracts that may involve no direct fraud or lying, so a regular court of law would not cancel them. Yet one side may have taken an unfair and shameless advantage of the other side's desperate need or bad luck, which a court of equity would not allow. In cases like these involving foreigners on either side, the federal courts couldn't do justice without equity power as well as legal power. Agreements to transfer land claimed under grants from different states are another example of why federal courts need equity power.

This reasoning may not be as obvious in states that don't keep a formal, technical line between LAW and EQUITY. But it is obvious in New York, where we see that line in practice every day.

:::vocab
Equity: A kind of justice, separate from ordinary law, that lets judges do what is fair when strict legal rules would lead to an unfair result. A court of equity can order someone to do or stop doing something, or cancel an unfair deal. Equity cases were decided by a judge without a jury.
Law (as opposed to equity): The ordinary system of courts and legal rules, where cases are usually decided by a jury and the usual remedy is money damages.
Ambassador: An official who represents a country in a foreign nation.
Consul: An official sent by a country to a foreign city to help its citizens and protect its trade there.
Unconscionable: So unfair that it shocks the conscience.
:::

:::commentary
The difference between "law" and "equity" confuses most modern readers, so it is worth explaining. In England, two separate court systems grew up. Ordinary courts of law followed strict rules, used juries, and usually awarded money. Courts of equity, led by a judge called the Chancellor, were created to fix the unfairness that strict rules sometimes caused. An equity court could, for example, order a person to keep a promise to sell land, or stop someone from doing harm.

New York kept separate courts of law and equity in 1788, which is why Hamilton says New Yorkers see the difference "every day." Most states, including New York in the mid-1800s, later combined the two systems. Federal courts merged law and equity in 1938. The difference still matters in one important way: it helps decide when a person has a right to a jury, which is the subject of [No. 83](/papers/83/).
:::

## The rest of the list {orig=15-21}

The judicial power of the Union will also extend:

- **Second, to treaties made under the authority of the United States, and to all cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers, and consuls.** These belong to the fourth kind of case, since they clearly relate to keeping the national peace.
- **Third, to cases of admiralty and maritime jurisdiction.** These make up the whole of the fifth kind of case proper for national courts.
- **Fourth, to controversies in which the United States is a party.** These are the third kind.
- **Fifth, to controversies between two or more states, between a state and citizens of another state, and between citizens of different states.** These belong to the fourth kind, and share in part the nature of the last kind.
- **Sixth, to cases between citizens of the same state claiming lands under grants from different states.** These fall under the last kind. They are the only cases in which the Constitution directly provides for federal courts to hear disputes between citizens of the same state.
- **Seventh, to cases between a state or its citizens and foreign states, citizens, or subjects.** I have already explained that these belong to the fourth kind, and that they are especially proper for national courts.

:::commentary
One item on this list soon caused a major controversy. Hamilton lists cases "between a State and citizens of another State" without discussing whether a private citizen could *sue* a state. In 1793, the Supreme Court said yes, in *Chisholm v. Georgia*. The decision alarmed many states, and the 11th Amendment, adopted in 1795, overturned it. Hamilton addresses this question directly in [No. 81](/papers/81/).
:::

## Congress can fix the problems {orig=22}

This review of the federal courts' specific powers, as the Constitution sets them out, shows that they all fit the principles that should have guided the design of that branch. They were all needed to make the system complete.

Some of these powers may bring minor inconveniences. But remember that Congress will have plenty of authority to make exceptions and set rules that will prevent or remove such problems. A well-informed mind will never treat the possibility of a few specific harms as a solid objection to a general principle that is designed to avoid general harms and gain general benefits.

:::commentary
Hamilton relies on Congress to shape the federal courts. That is how it worked out. The Constitution created only "one supreme Court" and left Congress to set up lower federal courts. Starting with the Judiciary Act of 1789, Congress has decided how many courts there are and which cases they hear. For most of the country's early history, federal courts heard far fewer kinds of cases than the Constitution allowed. For example, Congress did not give lower federal courts general power over cases arising under federal law until 1875.

Critics then and now point out that this leaves a lot of power with Congress. Supporters reply that the flexibility lets the system adjust over time, just as Hamilton argued.
:::

:::today
This essay explains why federal courts hear the kinds of cases they do today. Lawsuits about the Constitution, federal laws, treaties, and disputes between states all go to federal court, along with cases between citizens of different states when enough money is at stake. When two states fight over water rights or borders, they still take the case straight to the Supreme Court. Hamilton's main reason, that national law needs one national meaning, explains why the Supreme Court has the final word on federal law. But the debate he was answering continues. People still argue about how much federal courts should be able to overrule state laws and state courts.
:::
