:::summary
John Jay returns to the series to defend the treaty power: the president makes treaties "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate," with two-thirds of senators present agreeing. Jay argues that the way presidents and senators are chosen will produce capable, trustworthy people, and that senators' long, overlapping terms give them the knowledge and stability foreign affairs require. The president can handle negotiations with the **secrecy** and **speed** diplomacy often needs. Jay then answers objections. Treaties need not be made by the legislature. They must be binding as supreme law, because a treaty is a bargain that one side can't cancel alone. And the fear of corrupt or unfair treaties is exaggerated.
:::

:::keypoints
- The **treaty power** is especially important because it affects **war, peace, and trade**.
- Presidents (chosen by electors) and senators (originally chosen by state legislatures) are likely to be **able and respected** people.
- Age requirements (35 for president, 30 for senators) give the people **time to judge** candidates' character.
- Senators' **six-year, staggered terms** give them experience and keep knowledge in place as members change.
- Requiring the Senate for both **treaties and laws** keeps trade policy consistent.
- Diplomacy often needs **secrecy and speed**, which the president can provide. "There are moments to be seized as they pass."
- Treaties can be made by a body other than the legislature and still bind everyone, just like court rulings.
- A treaty is **a bargain**: since both sides agreed to it, one side can't cancel it at will. So treaties must be **supreme law**.
- All states are **equally represented** in the Senate, and the president and senators are bound by treaties like everyone else.
- Corruption is unlikely, a fraudulent treaty would be **void**, and **impeachment** is available.
:::

:::context Background
John Jay wrote Nos. 2–5 at the start of the series, then fell ill. He returned for this one essay because treaties were his specialty. As Secretary for Foreign Affairs since 1784, he had handled all of America's diplomacy under the Articles of Confederation, and he had helped negotiate the 1783 peace treaty with Britain.

Treaties were a sore subject in 1788. In 1786, Jay had negotiated with Spain over trade, and he had proposed giving up American use of the Mississippi River for 25 years. Southern and western states were outraged and blocked the deal. Some Anti-Federalists now feared that a president and a two-thirds Senate could sign away the interests of one region. Jay doesn't mention that episode directly, but it hangs over the essay.
:::

## Critics attack even the best parts {orig=1-2}

It's a true and familiar observation that people who oppose a particular person, or a particular measure, rarely limit their criticism to the parts that deserve blame. Except on this principle, it's hard to explain why some people condemn the proposed Constitution as a whole and harshly attack even some of its most unobjectionable parts.

Article II, Section 2 gives power to the president, "BY AND WITH THE ADVICE AND CONSENT OF THE SENATE, TO MAKE TREATIES, PROVIDED TWO THIRDS OF THE SENATORS PRESENT CONCUR."

:::vocab
Treaty: A formal written agreement between nations, for example about peace, trade, or borders.
Advice and consent: The Senate's role in approving treaties and appointments. For treaties, two-thirds of the senators present must agree.
:::

## Who will make treaties: chosen with care {orig=3-4}

The power to make treaties is important, especially since it relates to war, peace, and trade. It should only be given out in a way, and with precautions, that best guarantee it will be used by the most qualified people, in the way most helpful to the public good.

The Convention seems to have paid attention to both points. It has arranged for the president to be chosen by select groups of electors, sent by the people for that specific purpose. And it has given the appointment of senators to the state legislatures. In such cases, this method has a huge advantage over elections by the people as a whole. In those elections, eager party workers often take advantage of voters who are lazy, uninformed, hopeful, or afraid, and put people in office with the votes of only a small share of the electorate.

The select groups that choose the president, and the state legislatures that appoint senators, will generally be made up of the most informed and respected citizens. So we can expect them to direct their attention and votes only to people who have become the most distinguished for ability and good character, and in whom the people see good reasons for trust.

The Constitution pays close attention to this goal. It excludes people under thirty-five from the presidency, and under thirty from the Senate. That limits the choice to people the public has had time to judge. The public is less likely to be fooled by flashy displays of genius and patriotism, which, like passing meteors, sometimes mislead as well as dazzle.

People say that wise kings will always be served by capable ministers. If that's true, then a group of select electors, which has far better means than any king of getting broad and accurate information about people and their characters, will make choices at least as wise and careful.

The natural conclusion is this. The president and senators chosen this way will always be among those who best understand our national interests, both among the states and toward foreign nations. They will be best able to advance those interests, and their reputation for honesty will inspire and deserve trust. The power to make treaties can safely be placed with such people.

:::quote
...those brilliant appearances of genius and patriotism, which, like transient meteors, sometimes mislead as well as dazzle.

Meaning: Some politicians seem brilliant and patriotic at first but turn out to be all flash. Age requirements give voters time to see a candidate's real character.
:::

:::commentary
Jay's argument depends on a system that no longer exists. Senators are no longer chosen by state legislatures; the 17th Amendment (1913) gave that choice to voters. And presidential electors no longer use their own judgment; they vote for the candidate their party nominated. Jay's distrust of "elections by the people in their collective capacity" reflects a common view among the founders that ordinary voters could be easily misled by party organizers. Many Americans today would disagree strongly with that view.
:::

## Experience and stability {orig=5-6}

Everyone knows that any kind of business needs a system. But the great importance of a system in national affairs hasn't yet sunk in enough with the public.

Some people want to give the treaty power to a large popular assembly whose members come and go in quick succession. They seem to forget that such a body can't achieve great goals that must be studied steadily in all their connections and circumstances. Those goals can only be reached through measures that require not only talent but exact information, and often a lot of time, to plan and carry out.

So it was wise of the Convention to provide not only that the treaty power should go to capable and honest people, but also that they should stay in office long enough to become fully familiar with our national affairs, and to create and put in place a system for managing them. The length of the term will let them greatly expand their political knowledge and make their growing experience more and more useful to the country.

The Convention was just as wise in arranging frequent Senate elections in a way that avoids handing these great affairs over to entirely new people every so often. By leaving a large share of the old senators in place, it preserves consistency and order, along with a steady flow of official knowledge.

Few people will deny that trade and shipping should be regulated by a system that is carefully designed and steadily followed, and that both our treaties and our laws should fit with it and support it. It's very important to keep them consistent. Anyone who agrees will see that this is well provided for by requiring the Senate's agreement for both treaties and laws.

:::commentary
Senators serve six-year terms, and only about one-third of the Senate is up for election every two years. Jay is pointing to that staggered schedule, which means the Senate never turns over all at once. Madison makes a similar argument for the Senate's stability in [No. 62](/papers/62/) and [No. 63](/papers/63/).
:::

## The need for secrecy and speed {orig=7-8}

In negotiating treaties of any kind, perfect SECRECY and quick ACTION are almost always needed at times. There are cases where very useful information can be obtained, if the people who have it don't have to fear being discovered. That fear affects them whether they act for money or out of friendship. There are surely many of both kinds who would trust the president to keep a secret, but wouldn't trust the Senate, and would trust a large popular assembly even less.

So the Convention did well to arrange the treaty power so that, although the president must act with the Senate's advice and consent in making treaties, he can handle the gathering of information as good sense suggests.

Anyone who has paid attention to human affairs must have noticed that there are tides in them. These tides vary greatly in how long they last, how strong they are, and which way they run, and they rarely run the same way twice. It is the job of those in charge of national affairs to see these tides and take advantage of them. People with long experience tell us that there are often times when days, or even hours, are precious.

A lost battle, the death of a ruler, the removal of a minister, or other events that change the situation can turn the most favorable tide against us. As on the battlefield, so in the government: there are moments to be seized as they pass, and those in charge of either should be able to use them.

We have suffered so often and so seriously from a lack of secrecy and speed that the Constitution would have been inexcusably flawed if it had ignored them. The parts of negotiations that usually need the most secrecy and speed are the preparatory and supporting steps. These matter nationally only because they help achieve the goals of the negotiation. The president will have no trouble handling these. And if anything comes up that needs the Senate's advice and consent, he can call the Senate into session at any time.

So the Constitution gives our treaty negotiations every advantage. On one side, they benefit from talent, information, honesty, and careful study. On the other, they benefit from secrecy and speed.

:::quote
As in the field, so in the cabinet, there are moments to be seized as they pass, and they who preside in either should be left in capacity to improve them.

Meaning: In diplomacy, as in war, opportunities appear suddenly and disappear quickly. Leaders need to be able to act fast.
:::

:::commentary
Jay knew this from experience. During the 1782–83 peace talks in Paris, he and John Adams decided to negotiate directly with Britain, without first consulting France, America's ally, even though Congress had instructed them to follow France's lead. Speed and secrecy helped win generous terms, including the Mississippi River as America's western border.

Jay's argument here is one source of a long-standing practice: presidents negotiate treaties on their own and then send the finished treaty to the Senate for approval. George Washington tried consulting the Senate in person about a treaty with the Creek Nation in 1789, but the meeting went so badly that he never did it again.
:::

:::vocab
Despatch: Speed and efficiency in getting something done. Today it's spelled "dispatch."
Intelligence: Secret information, especially about foreign governments.
:::

## Objection 1: only lawmakers should make laws {orig=9-10}

But as with almost every plan ever proposed, people have come up with objections to this one.

Some are unhappy with it, not because of any mistakes or flaws in it, but because treaties, once made, will have the force of law. So, they say, treaties should only be made by people with legislative power.

These gentlemen seem to forget that the judgments of our courts, and the commissions the governor issues under the constitution, are just as valid and binding on everyone they affect as the laws passed by our legislature. All constitutional uses of power, whether by the executive or the courts, are just as legally valid and binding as if they came from the legislature.

So whatever we call the treaty power, and however binding treaties are once made, the people can quite properly give that power to a body separate from the legislature, the executive, or the courts. Just because the people gave the power to make laws to the legislature, it surely doesn't follow that they must also give it every other sovereign power that binds and affects citizens.

## Objection 2: treaties should be easy to cancel {orig=11}

Others are content with the way treaties will be made, but object to treaties being the SUPREME law of the land. They insist, and say they believe, that treaties should be repealable at will, like ordinary laws. This idea seems to be new and unique to this country. But new errors, as well as new truths, often appear.

These gentlemen should remember that a treaty is just another name for a bargain. It would be impossible to find any nation willing to make a bargain with us that bound them ABSOLUTELY, but bound us only as long and as far as we felt like being bound.

People who make laws can certainly amend or repeal them. And no one will dispute that people who make treaties can change or cancel them. But let's not forget that treaties are made not by one side but by both. So just as both sides had to agree to make them in the first place, both must always agree to change or cancel them.

So the proposed Constitution hasn't increased the binding force of treaties at all. They are just as binding, and just as far beyond the reach of ordinary laws, now as they will be at any future time or under any form of government.

:::quote
These gentlemen would do well to reflect that a treaty is only another name for a bargain...

Meaning: A treaty is a deal between two countries. No country would make a deal with us if we could break it whenever we wanted.
:::

:::commentary
Jay's view, that a treaty can't be canceled by one side alone, was the standard view of international law in his time. But American practice has turned out to be more complicated. The Supreme Court has ruled that Congress can pass a later law that overrides a treaty within the United States, even though the U.S. may still be bound by it internationally. Presidents have also withdrawn from treaties on their own, sometimes following the treaty's own withdrawal rules. Examples include Jimmy Carter ending a defense treaty with Taiwan in 1979 and George W. Bush withdrawing from the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty in 2002. Who has the power to end a treaty is still debated.

Making treaties "the supreme Law of the Land" in Article VI was meant to solve a real problem. Under the Articles, states had openly violated the 1783 peace treaty, which gave Britain an excuse to keep its forts on American soil.
:::

## Objection 3: unfair or corrupt treaties {orig=12-15}

Suspicion can be useful in republics. But when there is too much of it in a nation, as when there is too much bile in the body, it distorts how everything looks. That probably explains the fears of some people that the president and Senate might make treaties without caring equally about the interests of all the states. Others suspect that two-thirds of the Senate will oppress the remaining third. They ask whether these officials are responsible enough for their conduct: if they act corruptly, can they be punished? And if they make bad treaties, how can we get rid of them?

**Fairness to all states.** All the states are equally represented in the Senate, by people who are the most capable and the most willing to advance their constituents' interests. So all the states will have equal influence there, especially as long as they keep choosing good senators and insist that they show up. As the United States takes on a more national form and character, the good of the whole will receive more and more attention. A government would have to be very weak indeed to forget that the good of the whole can only be advanced by advancing the good of each of its parts.

The president and Senate can't make any treaty that won't bind and affect them, their families, and their property just as much as everyone else. Since they have no private interests separate from the nation's, they won't be tempted to neglect the nation's interests.

**Corruption.** As for corruption, it's not a realistic concern. Someone must have been very unlucky in his dealings with the world, or have a heart very open to such ideas, to think it likely that the president and two-thirds of the Senate would ever be capable of such shameful conduct. The idea is too crude and too insulting to take seriously. But if it ever happened, a treaty obtained that way would be null and void under the law of nations, like any other fraudulent contract.

**Responsibility.** As for their responsibility, it's hard to see how it could be increased. Every influence that can move the human mind, including honor, oaths, reputation, conscience, love of country, and family ties, helps guarantee their loyalty. In short, since the Constitution has taken the greatest care to ensure they will be people of talent and integrity, we can be confident that the treaties they make will be as good as could be made under the circumstances. And to the extent that fear of punishment and disgrace can encourage good behavior, the impeachment clause provides plenty of it.

:::quote
However useful jealousy may be in republics, yet when like bile in the natural, it abounds too much in the body politic, the eyes of both become very liable to be deceived...

Meaning: Some suspicion of government is healthy. But too much distorts your view, the way doctors in Jay's time believed too much bile made a person see things wrongly.
:::

:::commentary
Jay's answer to the fear of an unfair treaty was that every state has an equal voice in the Senate. That would have reassured the Southern and western states that had blocked his Mississippi deal with Spain. A two-thirds requirement meant any treaty needed broad support across regions.

His dismissal of corruption as "not supposable" is one of the weaker parts of the essay. He relies heavily on the good character of officials, while Madison, in [No. 51](/papers/51/), warns that "enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm" and the system must work anyway. The two-thirds rule has made the Senate a real check. It famously rejected the Treaty of Versailles after World War I, keeping the U.S. out of the League of Nations.
:::

:::vocab
Body politic: A nation or state thought of as a single body made up of its people.
Null and void: Having no legal force at all.
:::

:::today
The treaty power still works roughly as Jay described, but with major changes in practice. Today, most international agreements are not formal treaties at all. Presidents often make "executive agreements" that don't go to the Senate, or "congressional-executive agreements" approved by a simple majority of both houses. Critics say this sidesteps the two-thirds rule the framers built in. Supporters say modern foreign affairs require more flexibility than the founders imagined. The tension Jay described, between careful deliberation and the need for secrecy and speed, is still at the heart of how America makes deals with the world.
:::
