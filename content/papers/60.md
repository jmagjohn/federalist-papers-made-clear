:::summary
In [No. 59](/papers/59/), Hamilton argued that the states should not have total control over federal elections. Now he takes up the opposite danger: that Congress might use its power over the "times, places and manner" of elections to favor one class of people, such as the rich, by holding elections only in places where they live. Hamilton calls this the most fanciful fear of all. The country is too diverse, and the House, Senate, and president are chosen in such different ways, that they would never unite behind such a scheme. Farmers and merchants each have a fair share of influence, and the wealthy don't all live in one place. Most importantly, Congress has no power to set property requirements for voters or candidates. A government bent on tyranny, he adds, would seize power outright instead of fiddling with election rules.
:::

:::keypoints
- No one claims Congress would use this power to **shut a whole state out** of representation.
- The fear is that Congress might **hold elections only in certain places**, to help a favored class win.
- Hamilton says such a plan would cause an **immediate revolt** of the people, led by the state governments.
- The House, Senate, and president are **chosen in different ways**, so they are unlikely to share a bias toward one class.
- The country's **farming and trading interests** will both be represented, and neither is likely to dominate the national government.
- The **"wealthy and the well-born"** are spread across the country, so limiting where people vote wouldn't help them.
- The only way to favor the rich would be **property requirements** for voters or candidates, and Congress **has no power** to set them.
- Leaders willing to destroy liberty would **seize power directly**, not rely on clever election laws.
:::

:::context Background
Anti-Federalists often warned that the new government would be run by an aristocracy. One of their favorite phrases was "the wealthy and the well-born." Some argued that the House was too small to include ordinary citizens (Madison answered this in Nos. 55–58). Others argued that Congress could use the Elections Clause to make voting hard for ordinary people, for example by holding all of a state's elections in one city, far from most farmers.

This is the second of Hamilton's three papers on the Elections Clause. Here he answers the charge that Congress would abuse the power to help one class of people.
:::

## The fear: rigging elections for a favored class {orig=1-2}

We have seen that unlimited power over federal elections could not safely be given to the state legislatures. Now let's look at the danger on the other side, from giving the final right to regulate its own elections to the national government itself.

No one claims that the Union would ever use this right to shut any state out of its share of representation. On this point, at least, everyone's interest would protect everyone. But some say it might be used to help a favored class of people win elections and keep out others. Congress could do this, they say, by holding elections only in certain districts, so that most citizens couldn't take part in the choice.

Of all the fanciful fears that have been raised, this seems to be the most fanciful. On the one hand, no reasonable estimate of the odds would lead us to think that the attitude behind such violent and unusual conduct could ever find its way into Congress. On the other hand, we can be certain that if so improper a spirit ever did get in, it would show itself in a completely different and far more decisive way.

That such an attempt is unlikely can be seen from this single fact. It could never be made without causing an immediate revolt by most of the people, with the state governments at their head and directing them. We can imagine that, in certain stormy and divided times, a victorious and overbearing majority might violate this basic right of freedom for one particular group of citizens. But in a country so well placed and so well informed, it is completely impossible to believe that the government would deliberately attack so basic a right of most of the people without setting off a popular revolution.

:::vocab
Chimerical: Imaginary; wildly unrealistic. A chimera was a monster from Greek myth.
Factious: Divided into quarreling groups, or tending to stir up such quarrels.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's first answer is that the people would never stand for it. If Congress tried to keep most voters away from the polls, the states would lead the resistance. This connects to his argument in [No. 59](/papers/59/): the states and the national government are meant to watch each other.

Notice his admission that a "victorious and overbearing majority" might take away the voting rights of "a particular class of citizens." He is right that this has happened. In the decades after the Civil War, many Southern states used poll taxes, literacy tests, and violence to keep Black citizens from voting for nearly a century. Those rules were mostly made by states, not by Congress. Congress later used its powers, including the Voting Rights Act of 1965, to fight them.
:::

## The branches are chosen in different ways {orig=3-4}

Beyond this general point, there are more specific reasons that rule out any fear on this subject.

The parts of the national government will be made up of very different elements. They will also be put into action in very different ways. This must be a strong obstacle to any agreement on a one-sided plan for elections.

There is enough variety in the wealth, character, customs, and habits of the people in different parts of the Union to create real differences in how their representatives feel about the various ranks and classes of society. Living closely together under the same government will gradually make people more alike in some of these ways. But there are causes, both physical and social, that may keep different leanings and attitudes alive, more or less, forever.

But the fact that will probably matter most is that each part of the government is chosen in a different way. The House of Representatives will be elected directly by the people. The Senate will be chosen by the state legislatures. The president will be chosen by electors whom the people pick for that purpose. So there is little chance that a shared interest would unite these different branches in favoring any particular class of voters.

As for the Senate, the national government would only have power over the "time and manner" of choosing senators. No rule about those things could change the spirit that guides the choice of senators. The judgment of the state legislatures as a whole could never be swayed by outside details like these. That fact alone should convince us that the feared favoritism would never be tried.

After all, why would the Senate agree to a preference that didn't include itself? And what would be the point of setting up a preference for one branch of Congress, if it couldn't be extended to the other? The makeup of one branch would cancel out the makeup of the other. And we can never suppose the scheme would reach the choice of senators unless we also suppose that the state legislatures willingly went along with it. If we suppose that, then it makes no difference where the power over elections is placed, whether with the states or with the Union.

:::vocab
Genius (of a people): The character or spirit of a people, not intelligence.
Predilection: A preference or special liking for something.
:::

:::commentary
This is a classic Federalist argument. Because the House, Senate, and president were chosen by different groups of people, they would answer to different pressures. A plot to rig elections would need all three to agree, and Hamilton thinks that is very unlikely. Madison makes a similar point about the branches having different sources of power in [No. 51](/papers/51/).

Remember that when Hamilton wrote, state legislatures chose senators. That is why he says Congress could affect only the "time and manner" of choosing senators. The places were left to the states.
:::

## Which class would be favored? {orig=5-7}

But what is this strange favoritism in Congress supposed to aim at? Would it favor one kind of work over another, one kind of property over another, or people with more property over those with less? Would it lean toward the landed interest, the moneyed interest, the merchants, or the manufacturers? Or, to use the fashionable language of the Constitution's opponents, will it try to raise up "the wealthy and the well-born," while shutting out and looking down on everyone else?

Suppose the favoritism were meant to help people in some particular kind of work or property. I think everyone will agree that the contest would be between landowners and merchants. And I don't hesitate to say that it is far less likely that either of them would gain control of the national government than that one or the other would dominate all the state governments. So we should fear unfair favoritism toward either one much less from the national government than from the states.

The states depend on farming and trade in different degrees. In most, if not all, of them, farming is the main business. In a few, though, trade nearly equals it, and in most, trade has a good deal of influence. Whichever one is stronger in a state will be carried into that state's representation in Congress. And because Congress will draw on a much greater variety of interests, in much more varied proportions, than any single state, it will be much less likely to take either side strongly than the representatives of any one state would be.

:::vocab
Landed interest: People whose wealth comes from owning land, mostly farmers and planters.
Moneyed interest: People whose wealth comes from lending money, banking, or investing.
Mercantile interest: The interests of merchants and people in trade.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is applying Madison's famous argument from [No. 10](/papers/10/). A large republic includes so many different interests that it is hard for any one of them to take over. A single state, with fewer interests, is more likely to be dominated by one group. So, Hamilton says, if you fear that one class will rig the rules, you should fear the states more than Congress.
:::

## Farmers, merchants, and the Senate {orig=8-9}

In a country made up mainly of farmers, where representation is fair and equal, the landed interest must usually have the most weight in the government. As long as this interest controls most of the state legislatures, it must have the same advantage in the national Senate, which will generally be a faithful copy of the majorities in those legislatures. So we can't assume that the Senate would ever want to sacrifice the farming class to the merchants.

I have applied this general point to the Senate in particular for a reason. The trusting fans of state power can't, by their own principles, suspect that the state legislatures would be pulled away from their duty by any outside influence. But in reality, the same conditions must have the same effect on the House of Representatives, at least when it is first formed. An improper bias toward merchants is no more likely from the House than from the Senate.

Perhaps to keep the objection alive somehow, someone may ask the opposite question. Isn't there a danger that the national government might be biased in favor of landowners, and try to give them a monopoly on running the national government? Such a bias is not likely to frighten the people who would be hurt by it, so I won't give a long answer. It will be enough to say three things:

- **First,** for reasons given elsewhere, it is less likely that strong favoritism would win out in the national government than in any of the states.
- **Second,** there would be no reason to violate the Constitution to help landowners. In the natural course of things, they would already have as much weight as they could want.
- **Third,** people who are used to studying the sources of national prosperity on a large scale must be too convinced of the usefulness of trade to want to wound it so deeply. That is what would happen if those who best understand trade were completely shut out of managing it. The importance of trade for the government's revenue alone will protect it. Congress will be constantly pushed to support trade by the pressing needs of the public treasury.

:::commentary
Hamilton's third point reflects a basic fact of the early republic. The national government expected to get most of its money from taxes on imported goods. So it had a strong reason to keep trade healthy. In fact, taxes on imports were the national government's largest source of money for most of the 1800s.

Hamilton also takes a quiet jab at the "credulous votaries of State power," which we have translated as "the trusting fans of state power." He means opponents who trusted the states completely but suspected the national government of every evil.
:::

:::vocab
Votary: A devoted follower or fan.
Revenue: Money a government collects, mainly through taxes.
:::

## "The wealthy and the well-born" {orig=10-11}

I have kept my discussion of favoritism between kinds of work and property short for a reason. As far as I understand the objectors, they have a different kind of favoritism in mind. The group they warn us about is the one they call "the wealthy and the well-born." These people, it seems, are to be raised to a hateful position above the rest of their fellow citizens. But the objectors can't agree on how. Sometimes they say this will happen because the House is so small. At other times they say it will happen by taking away most people's chance to use their right to vote.

But how are the places of election supposed to be chosen to produce this preference? Do "the wealthy and the well-born," as they are called, live only in certain spots in each state? Have they, by some miraculous instinct or foresight, set aside a common neighborhood in each state to live in? Are they found only in the towns and cities? Or are they, on the contrary, scattered across the countryside, wherever greed or chance happened to place them or their ancestors?

If the last is true, as every intelligent person knows it is, then isn't it clear that limiting elections to certain districts would defeat its own purpose, as well as being objectionable in every other way?

The truth is that there is only one way to give the rich the feared advantage. That is to require voters, or candidates, to own a certain amount of property. But that is no part of the power given to the national government. Its authority would be expressly limited to regulating the TIMES, the PLACES, and the MANNER of elections. As I have noted before, the qualifications of the people who may vote or be elected are defined and fixed in the Constitution, and Congress can't change them.

:::note Hamilton's footnote
Hamilton added a footnote saying that wealthy people are scattered across the countryside "particularly in the Southern States and in this State." He likely had in mind the South's large plantations and New York's great landed estates along the Hudson River.
:::

:::vocab
Suffrage: The right to vote.
Qualifications: The requirements a person must meet to vote or to hold an office, such as age or citizenship.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's argument is clever but needs a careful reading. The Constitution does fix the qualifications for members of Congress: age, years of citizenship, and living in the state. Congress can't add to them. The Supreme Court later held that states can't add to them either, in *U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton* (1995).

But voters were a different story. The Constitution said that anyone allowed to vote for the larger house of a state's legislature could vote for the U.S. House. So Congress couldn't set property requirements for voters, but the *states* could, and in 1788 most of them did. Women generally could not vote, and neither could enslaved people. Over time, amendments limited what states can do. The 15th Amendment (1870) barred denying the vote based on race, the 19th (1920) based on sex, the 24th (1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections, and the 26th (1971) lowered the voting age to 18.
:::

## A tyrant wouldn't bother with election rules {orig=12}

Still, let's admit, for the sake of argument, that the suggested trick might work. And let's also assume that the national rulers had overcome every doubt that a sense of duty, or fear of the danger, might give them. Even so, I don't think anyone will claim they could ever hope to carry out such a plan without an army strong enough to crush the resistance of most of the people.

I have discussed and shown in other papers how unlikely it is that such a force would exist. But to show how empty this objection is, let's grant for a moment that such a force might exist, and that the national government had it. What follows?

People who wanted to attack the community's basic rights, and who had the means to do it, wouldn't waste their time on the silly job of writing election laws to help a favored class. Wouldn't they prefer a course more suited to increasing their own power directly? Wouldn't they boldly decide to keep themselves in office forever with one decisive seizure of power? That would be better for them than relying on shaky tricks that, despite every precaution, might end in their own removal, disgrace, and ruin. Wouldn't they fear that citizens who know their rights, and hold onto them firmly, would rush from the farthest corners of their states to the polling places? There the citizens would overthrow their tyrants and replace them with people ready to avenge the insulted dignity of the people.

:::quote
Would they not rather boldly resolve to perpetuate themselves in office by one decisive act of usurpation, than to trust to precarious expedients which, in spite of all the precautions that might accompany them, might terminate in the dismission, disgrace, and ruin of their authors?

Meaning: Leaders who wanted to become tyrants would just seize power. They wouldn't gamble on tricky election rules that voters could still defeat.
:::

:::vocab
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Expedient: A quick, convenient way of getting something done, often not the most honest one.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's last argument is a kind of "reductio," a way of showing that an idea leads to something absurd. If Congress were evil enough to rig elections and strong enough to put down a revolt, it wouldn't need rigged elections at all. It could simply take power.

The argument is persuasive, but some readers find it too neat. History shows that would-be strongmen often *do* prefer to keep elections going while quietly tilting the rules, because a show of popular support makes their power look legitimate. Hamilton's earlier discussions of whether the national government could ever raise an army big enough to oppress the people are in [No. 26](/papers/26/), [No. 28](/papers/28/), and [No. 29](/papers/29/), and Madison's in [No. 46](/papers/46/).
:::

:::today
Fears that election rules can be designed to help one group over another are still very much alive. Today's debates are about things like voter ID laws, early and mail voting, the number and location of polling places, and how district lines are drawn. Supporters of stricter rules say they protect the honesty of elections and public trust in the results. Critics say they make voting harder, especially for poorer, younger, or minority voters. Hamilton's argument that a large, diverse country makes it hard to rig the rules is often contrasted with the fact that most voting rules are still made state by state, where one party or group may have more control.
:::
