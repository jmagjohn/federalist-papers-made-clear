:::summary
Critics charged that the Philadelphia Convention had overstepped its authority. It was called only to *revise* the Articles of Confederation, they said, but it wrote a whole new Constitution instead. Madison answers in three steps. First, he reads the documents that called the Convention and shows that its main goal was a "firm national government" adequate to the country's needs. When the means (changing the Articles) and the goal (an adequate government) clash, the goal should win. And the new plan actually expands principles already found in the Articles. Second, even if the delegates did go beyond their instructions, the crisis and their duty to the country justified it. Third, and most important, the Constitution is only a proposal. It means nothing unless the people approve it, and good advice should be accepted no matter who gives it.
:::

:::keypoints
- The Convention's powers came from two documents: the **Annapolis report** of September 1786 and the **resolution of Congress** of February 1787.
- Together they told the Convention to create a **firm national government** adequate to the needs of government and the preservation of the Union, by altering the Articles.
- Two rules of interpretation: give every part of an instruction some meaning, and if parts conflict, the **means should give way to the end**.
- The Convention was allowed to make **alterations and new provisions**. The critics can't say where "alteration" ends and "transformation" begins.
- The new Constitution mostly **expands principles already in the Articles**, which were too weak to work.
- The one real departure: ratification by **the people** and by **nine states**, instead of by every state legislature. Critics rarely press this point, because one small state shouldn't be able to block the other twelve.
- The Convention's powers were only **advisory**. Its plan is "of no more consequence than the paper on which it is written" unless the people approve it.
- In great changes of government, **forms must give way to substance**. The people's right to "abolish or alter their governments" requires someone to propose a plan first.
- Even if the Convention had no authority at all, the real question is not **who** gave the advice but whether the advice is **good**.
:::

:::context Background
In September 1786, delegates from five states met at Annapolis, Maryland, to discuss trade problems among the states. Too few states came to do any business. Instead, the delegates issued a report, drafted by Hamilton, calling for a broader convention in Philadelphia the next May. In February 1787, Congress endorsed the idea, but it described the meeting's purpose as "the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation."

The Convention instead wrote an entirely new Constitution. It also said the Constitution would take effect once nine states ratified it through special conventions. The Articles, by contrast, required every state legislature to approve any change. Opponents seized on this. In [No. 39](/papers/39/), Madison promised to answer three questions about the plan. This essay answers the second and third: Was the Convention authorized to propose it? And if not, did its duty to the country make up for the lack of authority?
:::

## The documents that called the Convention {orig=1-5}

The second question to examine is whether the Convention was authorized to write and propose this mixed Constitution.

Strictly speaking, the Convention's powers should be judged by looking at the commissions each delegate received from his own state. But all of these referred either to the recommendation of the meeting at Annapolis in September 1786, or to the recommendation of Congress in February 1787. So it will be enough to look at those two documents.

The Annapolis report recommends the:

> "appointment of commissioners to take into consideration the situation of the United States; to devise SUCH FURTHER PROVISIONS as shall appear to them necessary to render the Constitution of the federal government ADEQUATE TO THE EXIGENCIES OF THE UNION; and to report such an act for that purpose, to the United States in Congress assembled, as when agreed to by them, and afterwards confirmed by the legislature of every State, will effectually provide for the same."

The recommendation of Congress reads as follows:

> "WHEREAS, There is provision in the articles of Confederation and perpetual Union, for making alterations therein, by the assent of a Congress of the United States, and of the legislatures of the several States; and whereas experience hath evinced, that there are defects in the present Confederation; as a mean to remedy which, several of the States, and PARTICULARLY THE STATE OF NEW YORK, by express instructions to their delegates in Congress, have suggested a convention for the purposes expressed in the following resolution; and such convention appearing to be the most probable mean of establishing in these States A FIRM NATIONAL GOVERNMENT:

> "Resolved, That in the opinion of Congress it is expedient, that on the second Monday of May next a convention of delegates, who shall have been appointed by the several States, be held at Philadelphia, for the sole and express purpose OF REVISING THE ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION, and reporting to Congress and the several legislatures such ALTERATIONS AND PROVISIONS THEREIN, as shall, when agreed to in Congress, and confirmed by the States, render the federal Constitution ADEQUATE TO THE EXIGENCIES OF GOVERNMENT AND THE PRESERVATION OF THE UNION."

In plain terms, the Annapolis report asked the states to send delegates who would study the country's situation and propose whatever further changes they thought necessary to make the national government adequate to the Union's needs. Congress and every state legislature would then have to approve it. Congress's resolution noted that the Articles already allowed changes approved by Congress and all the state legislatures. It said experience had shown the Articles had defects, and that several states, especially New York, had suggested a convention. Such a convention seemed the most likely way to establish a firm national government. So Congress recommended that delegates meet in Philadelphia in May to revise the Articles and propose changes that would make them adequate to the needs of government and the preservation of the Union.

:::vocab
Commission: An official document that gives someone the authority of an office.
Exigency: An urgent need or emergency.
Annapolis Convention: A meeting of delegates from five states in Annapolis, Maryland, in September 1786. It called for the Constitutional Convention.
:::

:::commentary
Notice which words Madison puts in capital letters. He stresses "A FIRM NATIONAL GOVERNMENT" and "ADEQUATE TO THE EXIGENCIES." His opponents stressed a different phrase in the same resolution: "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation." Both sides were quoting the same document. They just emphasized different parts of it.

Madison also points out that New York, the state whose voters he is addressing, had asked for the Convention. New York's legislature had instructed its delegates in Congress to propose one. That makes it awkward for New Yorkers to complain about the Convention's existence.
:::

## What the two documents required {orig=6-7}

From these two documents, four things are clear:

1. The goal of the Convention was to establish in these states **A FIRM NATIONAL GOVERNMENT**.
2. This government was to be **ADEQUATE TO THE EXIGENCIES OF GOVERNMENT** and **THE PRESERVATION OF THE UNION**.
3. These goals were to be reached by **ALTERATIONS AND PROVISIONS IN THE ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION**, as Congress put it, or by **SUCH FURTHER PROVISIONS AS SHOULD APPEAR NECESSARY**, as the Annapolis report put it.
4. The changes were to be reported to Congress and to the states, to be agreed to by Congress and confirmed by the states.

Comparing these phrases and reading them fairly, we can work out the authority the Convention acted under. It was to design a NATIONAL GOVERNMENT, adequate to the EXIGENCIES OF GOVERNMENT and OF THE UNION, and to reshape the Articles of Confederation into whatever form would achieve these purposes.

## Two rules for reading instructions {orig=8-9}

There are two rules of interpretation that plain reason teaches and that are also based on established legal principles.

The first is that every part of the wording should, if possible, be given some meaning, and all the parts should be made to work toward a common goal.

The second is that where the parts can't be made to fit together, the less important part should give way to the more important part. The means should be sacrificed to the end, not the end to the means.

Suppose, then, that the words defining the Convention's authority truly contradicted each other. Suppose that, in the Convention's judgment, a NATIONAL and ADEQUATE GOVERNMENT could not possibly be created through ALTERATIONS and PROVISIONS in the ARTICLES OF CONFEDERATION. Which part of the instructions should the Convention have followed, and which should it have rejected? Which part was more important, and which less? Which was the end, and which the means?

Let the strictest interpreters of delegated powers answer these questions. Let the most stubborn critics of the Convention's actions answer them. Let them tell us what mattered more to the happiness of the American people:

- that the Articles of Confederation be set aside, so that an adequate government could be provided and the Union preserved; or
- that an adequate government be given up, so that the Articles of Confederation could be preserved.

Let them tell us this, too. Was preserving the Articles the *goal*, with reform of the government as the *means* of reaching it? Or was establishing a government adequate to the nation's happiness the goal that the Articles themselves were originally meant to serve? And shouldn't the Articles, as a means that had failed, have been sacrificed to that goal?

:::quote
...the means should be sacrificed to the end, rather than the end to the means.

Meaning: When instructions conflict, follow the purpose, not the method. The Articles were just a tool for good government. If the tool doesn't work, replace it.
:::

:::vocab
Construction (of a law): Interpretation; the way the meaning of a law or document is worked out.
Axiom: A basic principle accepted as true without needing proof.
Delegated powers: Powers given to an official or body by someone else, who keeps the final authority.
:::

:::commentary
Here Madison argues like a lawyer. He uses two real rules of legal interpretation. Courts still use versions of them today, for example by trying to read a law so that every word has some meaning, and by reading a law in light of its purpose.

Critics would reply that Madison has the instruction backward. Congress didn't just state a goal. It named a *specific* method, revising the Articles, and called it the "sole and express purpose." On this view, if the Convention thought the Articles couldn't be fixed, it should have reported that and asked for new instructions. Some delegates felt this way at the time. Robert Yates and John Lansing, two of New York's three delegates, left the Convention partly because they believed it was going beyond its authority.
:::

## Was the change really so radical? {orig=10-11}

But do we even need to suppose that these phrases flatly contradict each other? Do we have to assume that no ALTERATIONS or PROVISIONS in the Articles could possibly reshape them into a national and adequate government, like the one the Convention proposed?

I assume no one will make a fuss about the change of TITLE. Changing a name could never count as using a power that wasn't granted. ALTERATIONS in the body of the document are expressly allowed. NEW PROVISIONS in it are also expressly allowed. So here is a power to change the title, to add new articles, and to alter old ones.

Must we admit that this power has been exceeded as long as part of the old Articles remains? Those who say yes should at least mark the boundary between allowed changes and seized ones. They should show where change within the limits of ALTERATIONS AND FURTHER PROVISIONS ends, and where a complete TRANSFORMATION of the government begins.

Will they say the changes should not have touched the substance of the Confederation? The states would never have called a convention with so much ceremony, or described its goals so broadly, unless they had some SUBSTANTIAL reform in mind.

Will they say the FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES of the Confederation were outside the Convention's authority, and should not have been changed? I ask, what are these principles? Let's go through them.

**Do they require that the states be treated as distinct and independent sovereigns when the Constitution is established?** The proposed Constitution treats them that way.

**Do they require that members of the government be appointed by the state legislatures, not by the people of the states?** One branch of the new government will be appointed by those legislatures. And under the Confederation, delegates to Congress MAY ALL be chosen directly by the people. In two states, they actually are.

**Do they require that the government's powers act on the states, not directly on individuals?** In some cases, as I have shown, the new government's powers will act on the states as political bodies. And in some cases, the existing government's powers already act directly on individuals. This is true in cases of:

- ships captured at sea;
- piracy;
- the post office;
- coins, weights, and measures;
- trade with the Indians;
- disputes over land granted by different states;
- and, above all, trials by courts-martial in the army and navy, where a person can be sentenced to death without a jury or even a civilian judge.

In all these cases, the Confederation's powers act directly on the persons and interests of individual citizens.

**Do these principles require that no tax be collected except through the states?** The Confederation itself allows a direct tax, to some extent, through the post office. Congress has interpreted its power over coinage as a way of collecting revenue directly from that source as well. But setting these examples aside, wasn't it an admitted goal of the Convention, and the general expectation of the people, that regulating trade should be given to the national government in a way that would make trade a direct source of national revenue? Hadn't Congress repeatedly recommended this as consistent with the basic principles of the Confederation? Hadn't every state but one, and hadn't New York itself, gone far enough along with Congress's plan to accept the PRINCIPLE of this new idea?

**Finally, do these principles require that the national government's powers be limited, and that beyond this limit the states keep their sovereignty and independence?** We have seen that in the new government, as in the old, the national powers are limited. And in all matters not listed, the states keep their sovereign and independent authority.

:::note
Publius's footnote names the two states whose delegates to Congress were chosen directly by the people: Connecticut and Rhode Island.
:::

:::vocab
Court-martial: A military court that tries members of the armed forces.
Unenumerated: Not listed. Here, the powers not listed in the Constitution, which stay with the states.
Usurped: Taken without the right to it.
Impost: A tax on imported goods.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's list of "fundamental principles" is clever. For each one, he shows either that the new Constitution keeps it, or that the old Articles had already broken it. So, he argues, the change is a matter of degree, not of kind.

The tax point refers to a real history. In 1781 and again in 1783, Congress asked the states for the power to collect a tax on imports, called an **impost**. Because any change to the Articles required all thirteen states, the plan failed. Rhode Island refused the first time, and the second plan never won the approval of every state. New York was a key holdout, which is why Madison pointedly says New York had accepted the "principle" of the idea.

A critic could answer that Madison is downplaying how much changed. Under the Articles, acting directly on individuals was a rare exception. Under the Constitution, it would be the normal rule, backed by a power to tax and a system of federal courts. As Madison admitted in [No. 39](/papers/39/), the Constitution is national in how its powers operate.
:::

## Old principles, made stronger {orig=12}

The truth is that the great principles of the proposed Constitution are not so much completely new as an expansion of principles already found in the Articles of Confederation. The trouble with the old system is that these principles are so weak and limited that they justify every charge of ineffectiveness made against it. They need to be enlarged so much that the new system looks like a complete transformation of the old.

:::commentary
This is the heart of Madison's first answer. He doesn't deny that the Constitution looks very different from the Articles. He says the difference comes from *strengthening* ideas the Articles already contained. Whether you find this convincing may depend on how much weight you give to looks versus principles. Many historians would say the Constitution was a genuine new start, whatever its roots.
:::

## The one real departure {orig=13}

In one respect, it is admitted that the Convention departed from the terms of its commission. Instead of reporting a plan that needed approval by THE LEGISLATURES OF ALL THE STATES, it reported a plan to be approved by THE PEOPLE, which can take effect with only NINE STATES.

It is worth noticing that this objection, though the most believable one, has been raised the least in the flood of publications attacking the Convention. This restraint can only come from an unavoidable sense of how absurd it would be to let the fate of twelve states depend on the stubbornness or corruption of a thirteenth.

Everyone remembers an example of this. A MAJORITY in a state holding one sixtieth of the American people stubbornly opposed a measure that twelve states, holding fifty-nine sixtieths of the people, had approved and called for. That example is still fresh in the memory, and still stirs the anger, of every citizen who has felt the damage to his country's honor and prosperity.

Since those who criticize the Convention's powers have more or less dropped this objection, I will set it aside without further comment.

:::commentary
This is Madison's weakest point, and he knows it. He admits it and then moves on quickly. Article XIII of the Articles said no change could be made unless Congress agreed and the legislature of every state confirmed it. Article VII of the Constitution instead said the Constitution would take effect among the ratifying states once conventions in nine states approved it. That was clearly not what the Articles allowed.

The state with "one sixtieth" of the people is Rhode Island, the smallest state. Its refusal alone had been enough to kill the 1781 impost, and it refused to send any delegates to the Convention. Madison's argument is practical: requiring unanimity had already let one small state block reforms the rest of the country wanted. Rhode Island did not ratify the Constitution until 1790, after the new government was already running.

Madison's claim that critics rarely raised this objection is open to question. Some Anti-Federalists did attack the nine-state rule, though many focused more on the content of the plan than on how it was adopted.
:::

## The Convention's powers were only advisory {orig=14-15}

The THIRD question is how far the duties arising from the situation itself could make up for any lack of formal authority.

So far, we have examined the Convention's powers as strictly as if they had been real and final powers to establish a Constitution for the United States. We have seen how well they hold up even under that assumption.

Now it is time to remember that the Convention's powers were only advisory. They were only the power to recommend. The states meant them that way, and the Convention understood them that way. Accordingly, the Convention designed and proposed a Constitution that is worth no more than the paper it is written on, unless it is stamped with the approval of those to whom it is addressed. This point puts the whole subject in a completely different light. It lets us judge the Convention's course fairly.

:::quote
...a Constitution which is to be of no more consequence than the paper on which it is written, unless it be stamped with the approbation of those to whom it is addressed.

Meaning: The Convention didn't impose anything. It only made a proposal. The people decide whether it becomes law.
:::

:::vocab
Approbation: Approval.
Recommendatory: Having only the power to recommend, not to command.
:::

## The situation the Convention faced {orig=16}

Let's look at the ground the Convention stood on. From its proceedings, we can gather several things.

The delegates were deeply and unanimously struck by the crisis. That crisis had led the country, almost with one voice, to make this unusual and serious attempt to correct the errors of the system that had caused it. They were just as deeply and unanimously convinced that a reform like the one they proposed was absolutely necessary to achieve the purposes they were appointed for.

They could not have missed that the hopes of the great body of citizens throughout this great empire were turned, with the sharpest anxiety, to the outcome of their work. They had every reason to believe that every enemy of American liberty and prosperity, foreign and domestic, was hoping for the opposite.

They had seen how eagerly the PROPOSAL of a single state, Virginia, for a partial amendment of the Confederation had been taken up and supported. They had seen that a VERY FEW delegates from a VERY FEW states, meeting at Annapolis, had TAKEN THE LIBERTY of recommending a great and critical step that was completely outside their commission. And they had seen that this step was not only approved by public opinion, but actually carried out by twelve of the thirteen states. They had seen Congress, in many cases, take on powers to act, not just to recommend. The public had accepted this, even when the occasions were far less urgent than the one before the Convention.

They must have reflected that in all great changes of established governments, forms should give way to substance. Clinging rigidly to forms in such cases would make empty and useless the supreme and precious right of the people to "abolish or alter their governments as to them shall seem most likely to effect their safety and happiness." The people can't all spontaneously move together toward their goal. So it is essential that such changes be started by some INFORMAL AND UNAUTHORIZED PROPOSALS, made by some patriotic and respected citizen or group of citizens.

They must have remembered that this same irregular, self-assumed right of proposing plans for the people's safety and happiness was how the states first united against the danger from their old government. That is how committees and congresses were formed to combine their efforts and defend their rights. And that is how CONVENTIONS were ELECTED in THE SEVERAL STATES to write the constitutions they are now governed by. Nor could they have forgotten that petty, ill-timed objections and an anxious devotion to ordinary forms appeared nowhere, except among those who wanted to hide their secret hostility to the cause itself behind those masks.

They must have kept in mind that their plan would be submitted TO THE PEOPLE THEMSELVES. If this supreme authority rejected it, it would be destroyed forever. If the people approved it, that approval would wipe out any earlier errors and irregularities.

It may even have occurred to them that, where people were eager to find fault, the Convention would be criticized no matter what. Failing to use the full power they had been given would draw criticism. So would recommending any measure not covered by their commission. And neither would draw less criticism than recommending, all at once, a measure fully equal to the nation's needs.

:::quote
...in all great changes of established governments, forms ought to give way to substance...

Meaning: When a country is deciding its basic government, getting the result right matters more than following every procedural rule.
:::

:::note
Publius's second footnote identifies the source of the quotation about the right "to abolish or alter their governments": the Declaration of Independence.
:::

:::vocab
Empire: In 1787, often just a large country or territory, not necessarily one ruled by an emperor.
Nugatory: Having no force or effect; useless.
Cavil: To raise petty, unnecessary objections.
:::

:::commentary
Madison makes a bold argument here. The American Revolution itself was "irregular." The Continental Congress, the committees of correspondence, and the state conventions that wrote the first state constitutions had no legal authority under British rule. Americans had already accepted that the people could act outside the usual forms when their safety required it. Why should the Convention be held to a stricter standard?

Madison's quotation from the Declaration of Independence is a paraphrase. The Declaration says that when a government becomes destructive of the people's rights, "it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it, and to institute new Government... as to them shall seem most likely to effect their Safety and Happiness."

Critics saw a danger in this reasoning. If a group of respected leaders can ignore their instructions whenever they think the cause is important enough, what limits them? Madison's answer is the final vote of the people, which comes in the next section.
:::

## What else should the Convention have done? {orig=17}

Imagine that, with all these impressions and considerations in mind, the Convention had acted differently. Instead of showing a manly confidence in their country, which had trusted them so especially, and instead of pointing out a system they believed could secure its happiness, suppose they had made the cold and sullen decision to disappoint its eager hopes. Suppose they had sacrificed substance to forms, and left their country's dearest interests to the uncertainties of delay and the risks of events.

Then let me ask any man who can lift his mind to one noble thought, or feel one patriotic emotion: what judgment should the fair-minded world, the friends of humanity, and every good citizen have passed on the conduct and character of this assembly?

Or, if there is a man whose urge to condemn can't be controlled, let me ask him what sentence he has saved for others. What about the twelve states that USURPED THE POWER of sending delegates to the Convention, a body completely unknown to their constitutions? What about Congress, which recommended creating this body, which was just as unknown to the Confederation? And what about the State of New York in particular, which first urged this unauthorized step and then went along with it?

:::commentary
Madison turns the critics' logic against them. If the Convention was illegal for going beyond the Articles, then the states that sent delegates and the Congress that recommended the meeting were also acting outside the Articles, since the Articles never mentioned a convention. And New York, whose readers he is addressing, had led the way.
:::

## Good advice is good, whoever gives it {orig=18-19}

But to take away every excuse from the critics, let's grant for a moment that the Convention was neither authorized by its commission nor justified by the circumstances in proposing a Constitution for the country. Does it follow that the Constitution should be rejected for that reason alone?

There is a noble saying that it is right to accept good advice even from an enemy. Shall we then set the shameful example of refusing good advice even when it comes from our friends? Surely the wise question, in every case, is not so much WHO gives the advice, as whether the advice is GOOD.

To sum up, here is what I have argued and proved:

- The charge that the Convention exceeded its powers has no foundation, except in one case that the critics have hardly pressed.
- If the Convention had exceeded its powers, the circumstances not only justified but required it, as the trusted servants of their country, to take the liberty it did.
- Finally, even if the Convention had violated both its powers and its duties in proposing a Constitution, the Constitution should still be accepted, if it is designed to achieve the goals and happiness of the American people.

How far the Constitution deserves that description is the subject of this investigation.

:::quote
The prudent inquiry, in all cases, ought surely to be, not so much FROM WHOM the advice comes, as whether the advice be GOOD.

Meaning: Judge the Constitution on its merits, not on whether the people who wrote it followed every rule.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's final argument is the strongest one. Whatever the Convention's authority, it couldn't make anything law by itself. The Constitution became law only because ratifying conventions in the states, elected for that purpose, approved it. So the real decision belonged to the people, not to the delegates in Philadelphia.

With this essay, Madison finishes the introduction to his review of the Constitution. Starting in [No. 41](/papers/41/), he examines the actual powers the Constitution gives the national government.

:::today
The question of whether the Convention exceeded its authority still matters. Article V of the Constitution allows two-thirds of the states to call a convention to propose amendments, but it has never been used. One worry people raise about calling such a convention is that it might become a "runaway convention" and go beyond its instructions, as some say the 1787 Convention did. Supporters of a new convention say any proposals would still need approval by three-fourths of the states. Critics say no one can be sure what such a meeting would do. Madison's argument, that the people's final approval cures any irregularity, sits at the center of this debate.
:::

- Why does Madison think it would have been absurd to let one state block a plan that twelve states wanted?
- Do you agree that the people's approval "blots out" any errors in how the Constitution was written? Why or why not?
