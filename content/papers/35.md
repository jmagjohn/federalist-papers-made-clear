:::summary
Hamilton makes two arguments in this essay. First, he warns that limiting the national government to taxes on imports would be harmful. It would push those taxes too high, encouraging smuggling, hurting merchants, and placing an unfair share of the burden on importing states like New York. Second, he answers a popular objection: that the House of Representatives is too small to include members of every class and occupation. Hamilton says that idea is unworkable and unnecessary. In free elections, voters will mostly choose merchants, landowners, and members of the "learned professions" such as lawyers. These representatives share interests with the people they represent, depend on their votes, and must live under the laws they pass. That, not a mirror of every job, is what ties representatives to the people.
:::

:::keypoints
- If the national government could tax only a few things, those things would bear **too much of the burden**. That would hurt certain industries and spread taxes unfairly.
- Very high import duties would encourage **smuggling**, favor manufacturers over everyone else, push business into less natural channels, and often fall on **merchants** rather than consumers.
- Relying only on import duties would be unfair to **importing states** like New York and would let **manufacturing states** pay less than their share.
- Critics said the House was too small to include **every class of citizens**. Hamilton calls this idea "altogether visionary."
- Craftsmen and manufacturers usually vote for **merchants**, whom they see as their natural allies. Landowners, rich and poor, share an interest in **low land taxes**. The **learned professions** can act as fair judges between other interests.
- What really ties a representative to the people is that he **depends on their votes** and must **obey the same laws** he makes.
- Taxation requires **wide knowledge**. Those who understand the economy best are the least likely to tax oppressively.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues Hamilton's defense of the national taxing power (Nos. 30–36). Its second half takes up a different kind of objection, about **representation**. Many Anti-Federalists argued that a good legislature should be a small picture of the whole people, with farmers, craftsmen, and people of modest means, not just the rich and educated. They worried that the House of Representatives, which would start with only 65 members, would be too small for that, and would fill up with wealthy elites who didn't understand ordinary people's lives. That worry made the taxing power seem even more dangerous.

Hamilton promises to discuss the size of the House "in another place." Madison does so in Nos. 55–58.
:::

## Limited taxes fall too heavily on a few things {orig=1}

Before looking at any other objections to an unlimited national taxing power, I will make one general remark. If the national government's power over revenue were limited to certain particular things, too large a share of the public burden would naturally fall on those things. Two evils would come from this. Certain branches of industry would be oppressed. And taxes would be divided unequally, both among the states and among citizens of the same state.

:::vocab
Revenue: Money a government collects, mainly through taxes, to pay for its work.
:::

## The harm of very high import duties {orig=2}

Suppose that, as some have urged, the national taxing power were limited to duties on imports. Because the government couldn't reach other sources of money, it would often be tempted to raise these duties to a harmful level.

Some people imagine that import duties can never be too high. They say the higher the duties, the more they will discourage wasteful buying, produce a favorable balance of trade, and encourage American manufacturing. But all extremes are harmful in various ways. Very high duties on imports would:

- encourage a general spirit of smuggling, which always hurts the honest trader, and in the end the revenue itself;
- make other classes of the community pay an improper tribute to the manufacturers, by giving them control of the market before they are ready for it;
- sometimes force business out of its more natural channels into others where it does less well;
- and finally, burden the merchant, who often has to pay the duties himself without getting the money back from the consumer.

When demand equals the supply of goods for sale, the consumer usually pays the duty. But when the market has more goods than buyers, much of the duty falls on the merchant. Sometimes it not only eats up his profits but cuts into his capital, the money he needs to stay in business. I tend to think that the duty is split between seller and buyer more often than people imagine. It isn't always possible to raise the price of a product by the exact amount of every new tax on it. A merchant, especially in a country with little money invested in trade, often has to keep prices down to sell his goods faster.

:::vocab
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
Balance of trade: The difference between the value of what a country sells to other countries and what it buys from them. A "favorable" balance means it sells more than it buys.
Capital: Money or property used to run a business.
Monopoly: Complete control of something by one party, with no one else allowed to share it.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is making an early argument about what economists now call **tax incidence**: who really ends up paying a tax. A tax on imports is collected from the merchant at the port, but he may pass it on to buyers through higher prices, or he may have to absorb some of it himself. Modern economists agree with Hamilton that the burden is often shared, depending on supply and demand.

Hamilton wasn't against all protective duties. In his *Report on Manufactures* (1791), written as Treasury Secretary, he argued for using duties and other measures to help young American industries grow. But he warns here against extremes. Later fights over tariffs showed how divisive they could be. The high Tariff of 1828, which critics called the "Tariff of Abominations," angered Southern states that bought manufactured goods and sold cotton abroad. It led to the Nullification Crisis of 1832, when South Carolina declared federal tariffs void within the state.
:::

## Why import duties alone would be unfair {orig=3}

The rule that the consumer is the one who pays is true far more often than not. That is why it is much fairer for import duties to go into a common national fund, instead of benefiting only the states where the goods come in. But the rule isn't true often enough to make it fair for those duties to be the *only* national fund.

When the merchant pays the duties, they act as an extra tax on the importing state, whose citizens also pay their share as consumers. In this way, the duties create inequality among the states, and the higher the duties, the greater the inequality.

Limiting national revenue to this one kind of tax would also create inequality, for a different reason, between manufacturing and non-manufacturing states. The states that can best supply their own needs with their own manufactured goods will buy fewer imported goods, compared to their population or wealth, than states that aren't so well placed. So under this system alone, they wouldn't pay into the national treasury in proportion to their ability. To make them pay their share, the government must be able to use excises, which properly fall on certain kinds of manufactured goods.

New York has more at stake here than those of her citizens who want to limit the Union to external taxes may realize. New York is an importing state and is not likely to become a major manufacturing state any time soon. So she would suffer in two ways if the Union were limited to taxes on trade.

:::vocab
Excise: A tax on goods made or sold within a country, such as liquor. Collecting it often required inspectors to enter homes and businesses.
Impost: A tax on imported goods; a duty.
External tax: A tax on trade with other countries, mainly duties on imports.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton aims this point straight at his New York readers. New York City was one of the busiest ports in the country, and the state collected its own import duties on goods passing through it. Many of those goods were then sold in neighboring states, like Connecticut and New Jersey, whose people ended up paying part of New York's taxes. This income is one reason many New York leaders, including Governor George Clinton, opposed giving Congress the import tax. Hamilton argues that, under the new system, New York would actually lose out if the Union could tax *only* imports.
:::

## Necessity breeds bad experiments {orig=4}

As for the danger that import duties would be pushed to a harmful extreme, someone may point out, as I noted in another of these papers, that concern for the revenue itself would prevent this. Too-high duties bring in less money, so the government would avoid them.

I readily admit this would be true as long as other sources of revenue were open. But if the roads to them were closed, HOPE, driven by necessity, would lead to experiments. These would be backed by strict precautions and extra penalties. For a while, they would work, until people had time to invent ways to get around the new precautions. The first success would tend to create false beliefs that might take a long period of later experience to correct. Necessity, especially in politics, often produces false hopes, false reasoning, and a matching set of mistaken policies.

But even if this excess did not result from limiting the national taxing power, the inequalities I have described would still follow from the other causes, though not as severely. Let us now return to examining the objections.

## The objection: Congress should include every class {orig=5}

One objection, judging by how often it is repeated, seems to be the critics' favorite. It says the House of Representatives is too small to include all the different classes of citizens. Without them, it can't combine the interests and feelings of every part of the community, or create a proper bond of sympathy between the representatives and the people who elect them.

This argument looks very attractive and appealing. It is well designed to win over the prejudices of the people it is aimed at. But when we take it apart carefully, it turns out to be nothing but nice-sounding words. Its goal, first of all, is impossible. And in the sense its supporters mean, it is unnecessary.

I will leave for another place the question of whether the House has enough members. Here I will only look at how the opposite view has been used against the taxing power.

:::vocab
Constituents: The people a representative is elected to serve.
Specious: Seeming true or reasonable at first, but actually false.
:::

## Free voters won't choose by occupation {orig=6-7}

The idea that every class of people should actually be represented by people of the same class is completely unrealistic. Unless the Constitution specifically required each occupation to send one or more members, it would never happen in practice.

With few exceptions, mechanics and manufacturers will always tend to vote for merchants rather than for people of their own trades. These sensible citizens know that their crafts and manufacturing supply the goods that merchants trade. Many of them are directly connected to the business of commerce. They know the merchant is their natural supporter and friend. However much they may rightly trust their own good sense, they know the merchant can promote their interests better than they can themselves.

They also know that their way of life hasn't given them the learning and skills without which even the greatest natural talents are mostly useless in a lawmaking body. And they know that merchants have the influence, weight, and education to stand up better against any mood in the government that might turn against manufacturing and trade.

These reasons, and many others that could be mentioned, prove that craftsmen and manufacturers will usually vote for merchants and for those the merchants recommend. Experience confirms it. So we must think of merchants as the natural representatives of all these classes of the community.

As for the learned professions, little needs to be said. They don't really form a separate interest in society. Depending on their situation and talents, members of these professions will be trusted and chosen by one another and by other parts of the community alike.

:::vocab
Mechanic: In Hamilton's time, a skilled craftsman or artisan, such as a carpenter, blacksmith, or shoemaker.
Learned professions: Jobs that required advanced education, traditionally law, medicine, and the ministry.
Deliberative assembly: A group, such as a legislature, that discusses and debates before making decisions.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's view of craftsmen is not flattering. He suggests that ordinary working people will, and should, look to their "betters" to represent them. To many modern readers, this sounds snobbish. It reflects an 18th-century belief that political leadership belonged to men of education and property.

The Anti-Federalists saw it differently. They argued that representatives should be like the people who elect them, sharing their circumstances and feelings. At New York's ratifying convention later in 1788, the Anti-Federalist Melancton Smith argued that the small House would be filled by a "natural aristocracy" of the wealthy and well-known, and that people of the "middling class" would rarely be elected. This debate, between representatives who *resemble* voters and representatives who *serve* them, has never really ended.
:::

## Landowners rich and poor share one interest {orig=8}

That leaves only the landed interest. In politics, and especially regarding taxes, I believe it is completely united, from the richest landlord down to the poorest tenant. No tax can be put on land that won't affect the owner of millions of acres as well as the owner of a single acre. So every landholder will have a common interest in keeping land taxes as low as possible. And common interest can always be counted on as the surest bond of sympathy.

But even if we suppose there is a difference of interest between the wealthy landholder and the middling farmer, why would the first have a better chance than the second of being sent to the national legislature? If we take facts as our guide and look at our own state senate and assembly, we find that owners of moderate amounts of land are the majority in both. This is just as true in the senate, which is smaller, as in the assembly, which is larger.

Where the voters have the same qualifications, it doesn't matter whether they choose a small or a large number of representatives. Their votes will go to the people they trust most, whether those people have large fortunes, moderate property, or no property at all.

:::vocab
Landed interest: The people who own or farm land, thought of as one group with shared concerns.
Tenant: A person who rents land or a home from its owner.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton points to New York's own legislature as evidence. Its senate was smaller than its assembly, yet both were made up mostly of middling landowners, not just the very rich. His point is that a smaller House of Representatives wouldn't automatically be filled with the wealthy.

His claim that every landowner has the same interest in taxes was open to challenge. A tenant farmer who owned no land at all might care more about rent than about land taxes. And in 1788, many people had no vote at all. Most states required voters to own a certain amount of property. Women could not vote almost anywhere, and most enslaved and free Black Americans were shut out as well. Hamilton's "community" whose interests representatives would share was much smaller than the whole population.
:::

## What really ties representatives to the people {orig=9-10}

It is said that all classes of citizens must have some of their own members in the representative body, so that their feelings and interests will be better understood and cared for. But we have seen that this will never happen under any system that leaves the people free to vote as they choose. Where they are free, the representative body will be made up of landholders, merchants, and members of the learned professions, with too few exceptions to affect the spirit of the government.

But where is the danger that these three kinds of men won't understand or care about the interests and feelings of the different classes of citizens? Won't the landholder know and feel whatever will promote or protect landed property? And because of his own interest in that kind of property, won't he be quick to resist every attempt to harm or burden it? Won't the merchant understand, and want to promote as far as is proper, the interests of the crafts and manufacturing so closely tied to his trade? And won't the member of a learned profession, who feels neutral about the rivalries between different branches of business, likely be a fair judge between them? Won't he be ready to support either, as far as it seems to him to serve the general interests of society?

We must also consider the passing moods and attitudes that may spread through particular parts of society. A wise government will never ignore these. Is a man whose position leads him to wide study and information less able to judge their nature, extent, and causes than one whose view doesn't go beyond his own neighbors and friends?

Isn't it natural that a man who seeks the people's favor, and who depends on his fellow citizens' votes to keep his office, will make sure to learn their attitudes and wishes? Won't he be willing to let them have their proper influence on his conduct? This dependence, and the fact that he and his descendants must live under the laws he votes for, are the true bonds of sympathy between the representative and the voter. And they are strong ones.

:::quote
This dependence, and the necessity of being bound himself, and his posterity, by the laws to which he gives his assent, are the true, and they are the strong chords of sympathy between the representative and the constituent.

Meaning: A representative will care about his voters because he needs their votes to stay in office, and because he and his family have to obey the same laws he passes.
:::

:::vocab
Suffrage: The right to vote, or a vote itself.
Posterity: Future generations.
Arbiter: A person who settles a dispute between others.
:::

:::commentary
This is the heart of Hamilton's answer, and it is a strong one. A representative doesn't need to *be* a farmer to protect farmers. He needs to *depend on* farmers' votes. And if he passes a harsh tax, he will pay it too. Madison makes the same point in [No. 57](/papers/57/), saying that members of Congress "can make no law which will not have its full operation on themselves and their friends."

Critics then and now ask whether that is enough. Lawmakers' lives can be very different from their voters' lives. Today, for example, members of Congress are much wealthier on average than most Americans, and lawyers have long been the most common occupation among them. Supporters of Hamilton's view say what matters is accountability at the ballot box. Others argue that a Congress with more varied backgrounds would better understand the whole country.
:::

## Taxation needs knowledge {orig=11}

No part of government requires wide information and a thorough knowledge of the principles of political economy as much as taxation does. The person who understands those principles best will be the least likely to use oppressive methods, or to sacrifice any particular class of citizens to raise revenue. It could be shown that the system of finance that brings in the most money will always be the one that is least burdensome.

There's no doubt that to use the taxing power wisely, the person who holds it must know the general character, habits, and ways of thinking of the people as a whole, and the resources of the country. This is all that can reasonably be meant by knowing the interests and feelings of the people. In any other sense, the idea either means nothing or means something absurd. And in that sense, let every thoughtful citizen judge for himself where the needed qualifications are most likely to be found.

:::vocab
Political economy: The old name for the study of how a nation produces and manages its wealth, including trade, money, and taxes. Today it is mostly called economics.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's claim that "the most productive system of finance will always be the least burdensome" is a bold one. His idea is that heavy, oppressive taxes discourage business and invite cheating, so they end up raising less money than moderate, well-designed taxes. Some modern economists make a similar argument about very high tax rates, though how high is "too high" is hotly debated.

Hamilton soon had the chance to test his ideas. As the first Secretary of the Treasury, he designed the national government's first tax system. One of his taxes, an excise on whiskey, led to the **Whiskey Rebellion** of 1794 in western Pennsylvania, where farmers who turned their grain into whiskey saw the tax as unfair. President Washington led militia troops to put down the uprising.
:::

:::today
The question Hamilton raises here, whether a legislature should look like the people it represents, is still debated. Congress today is more diverse than ever in race, gender, and background, but it is still wealthier and more highly educated than the country as a whole. Some people say representation works best when lawmakers share the experiences of the people they serve. Others say, as Hamilton did, that what matters most is that lawmakers answer to voters at election time and live under the laws they pass. Debates over tariffs also continue. Supporters say they protect American jobs and industries. Critics say they raise prices for consumers and invite other countries to strike back.
:::
