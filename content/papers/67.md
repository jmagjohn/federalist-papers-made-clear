:::summary
Hamilton opens his series of essays on the presidency by attacking the way opponents have described it. They have painted the president as a king, even an Eastern emperor, with a crown, a throne, guards, and a palace of servants. Hamilton says the real office has powers about equal to, or in some cases less than, those of New York's governor. To show how far critics have gone, he takes one example: a leading opponent claimed the president could fill empty seats in the Senate. In fact, the Constitution gives that power to the governors of the states. Hamilton walks through the text, clause by clause, to prove the claim false, and he accuses its author of deliberately trying to deceive the people.
:::

:::keypoints
- Designing the **executive branch** was one of the hardest tasks of the Convention, and it has been attacked with the **least fairness**.
- Opponents play on Americans' **hatred of monarchy**, describing the president as a king in all but name.
- In reality, the president's powers are in few cases **greater**, and in some cases **less**, than those of New York's governor.
- One writer claimed the president could **fill vacancies in the Senate**. Hamilton uses this as a sample of the opponents' methods.
- The president's power to **appoint officers** covers only offices "established by law," not senators, whose selection the Constitution handles separately.
- The president's power to make **recess appointments** is a temporary backup for when the Senate is not in session. It applies to the same kind of officers.
- The Constitution **expressly** gives the power to fill Senate vacancies to **state executives**, until the state legislature meets.
- Hamilton says the false claim was a **deliberate attempt to deceive**, and he defends his unusually harsh tone.
:::

:::context Background
With this paper, Hamilton begins eleven essays on the presidency (Nos. 67–77). Americans had just fought a war against King George III, and many feared that a single, powerful executive would become a new king. Anti-Federalist writers fed this fear. One of them, writing as "Cato," argued in his fifth letter that the president would become a monarch. Cato is often thought to be New York's governor, George Clinton, though historians are not certain.

Hamilton knew that fear of monarchy was the strongest weapon against the Constitution. Before defending the presidency's powers, he tries to clear away what he calls fiction.
:::

## Turning to the presidency {orig=1-2}

The design of the executive branch of the proposed government now deserves our attention.

There is hardly any part of the plan that could have been harder to arrange than this one. And there is perhaps no part that has been attacked with less fairness or criticized with less good judgment.

:::vocab
Executive: The branch of government that carries out ("executes") the laws. In the national government, this is the president.
Inveigh: To attack someone or something angrily in words.
:::

## A king in disguise? {orig=3-5}

Here the writers against the Constitution seem to have worked hard to show off their talent for misrepresentation. They are counting on the people's dislike of monarchy. So they have tried to turn all the people's suspicions and fears against the proposed President of the United States. They present him not merely as an unborn child of that hated parent, monarchy, but as its fully grown offspring.

To prove this supposed family resemblance, they haven't hesitated to borrow from pure fiction. The powers of an official whose authority is in few cases greater, and in some cases less, than that of the governor of New York have been inflated into more than royal privileges. He has been decorated with qualities grander and more splendid than those of a king of Great Britain. He has been shown to us with a jeweled crown shining on his head and royal purple robes flowing behind him. He has been seated on a throne, surrounded by flatterers and mistresses, receiving the ambassadors of foreign rulers with all the proud display of royalty. Images of Asian despotism and luxury have hardly been missing to complete this exaggerated scene. We have been taught to tremble at the terrifying faces of murderous palace guards, and to blush at the secrets of a future harem.

Attempts as wild as these to distort the presidency, or rather to transform it into something else entirely, make it necessary to look carefully at its real nature and form. We must do this both to see what it truly looks like, and to unmask the dishonesty and expose the falseness of the fake likenesses that have been spread so slyly and so busily.

In doing this, anyone would find it hard either to look calmly at, or to treat seriously, the tricks, as weak as they are wicked, that have been invented to mislead public opinion on this subject. They go so far beyond the usual (though still unjustifiable) liberties of political spin that they overpower even the most fair and tolerant mind. Such a person would normally want to judge political opponents kindly. But here that feeling must give way to open and complete anger.

It is impossible not to call it deliberate fraud and deception to pretend that a king of Great Britain resembles an official like the proposed President of the United States. It is even more impossible not to say the same about the reckless and shameless tricks that have been used to make this fraud succeed.

:::vocab
Monarchy: Government by a king or queen who usually inherits the throne.
Despotism: Rule by one person with unlimited power.
Diadem: A jeweled crown worn as a sign of royalty.
Janizaries: The elite soldiers who guarded the sultan of the Ottoman Empire, centered in modern Turkey.
Seraglio: The part of a sultan's palace where his wives lived; a harem.
Prerogative: A special right or power that belongs to an office, especially to a king.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's list of images, from crowns and purple robes to Ottoman guards and harems, mocks the opponents' warnings by exaggerating them further. Purple was the traditional color of Roman emperors. The Ottoman sultan in Constantinople (modern Istanbul) was, to many Europeans and Americans of the time, the very picture of a despot. Hamilton's descriptions also reflect stereotypes about Eastern peoples that were common in Europe and America then.

The opponents' fear was not absurd, though. Many Americans worried that a president who could be re-elected again and again, commanded the army, and had a veto might slowly grow into a king. Hamilton answers those concerns in detail in [No. 69](/papers/69/), where he compares the president with the British king and the governor of New York point by point.
:::

## A false claim about Senate vacancies {orig=6-7}

In one case, which I give as a sample of the general spirit, the boldness has gone so far as to give the President of the United States a power that the Constitution EXPRESSLY gives to the executives of the individual states. I mean the power of filling vacancies in the Senate that happen unexpectedly.

This bold test of his fellow citizens' intelligence has been tried by a writer who (whatever his real merit may be) has received no small share of praise from his party. On this false and baseless claim, he has built a series of comments that are just as false and baseless. Let him now face the evidence. And let him, if he can, justify or excuse the shameful insult he has offered to the truth and to the rules of fair dealing.

:::note Hamilton's footnote
Hamilton's footnote names the writer's essay: "Cato, No. V." Cato's fifth letter warned that the president would be dangerously powerful. According to Hamilton, it also claimed the president could fill Senate vacancies.
:::

:::vocab
Casual vacancy: A seat that becomes empty unexpectedly, through death or resignation, before the term ends.
Temerity: Reckless boldness.
:::

## What the appointment clauses say {orig=8-9}

The second clause of the second section of the second article gives the President of the United States power:

> "to nominate, and by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, to appoint ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, judges of the Supreme Court, and all other OFFICERS of United States whose appointments are NOT in the Constitution OTHERWISE PROVIDED FOR, and WHICH SHALL BE ESTABLISHED BY LAW."

Right after this clause comes another, in these words:

> "The President shall have power to fill up all VACANCIES that may happen DURING THE RECESS OF THE SENATE, by granting commissions which shall EXPIRE AT THE END OF THEIR NEXT SESSION."

The supposed power of the president to fill vacancies in the Senate has been drawn from this second clause. But a little attention to how the clauses connect, and to the plain meaning of the words, will show that this conclusion doesn't even look reasonable.

The first of these two clauses clearly only provides a way to appoint officers "whose appointments are NOT OTHERWISE PROVIDED FOR in the Constitution, and which SHALL BE ESTABLISHED BY LAW." So of course it can't apply to the appointment of senators. Their appointment IS provided for elsewhere in the Constitution. And the Senate is ESTABLISHED BY THE CONSTITUTION itself, so it won't need to be set up later by a law. Hardly anyone will dispute this.

:::vocab
Advice and consent: The Senate's role in approving treaties (by a two-thirds vote) and appointments (by a majority).
Recess: A break between sessions, when a legislature is not meeting.
Commission: An official document that gives someone the authority of an office.
Colorable: Seeming true or reasonable at first glance.
:::

## Four reasons the claim is false {orig=10}

It is just as clear that the second clause can't be read to include the power of filling vacancies in the Senate. Here are the reasons.

**First,** look at how that clause relates to the one before it, which sets out the general way of appointing officers of the United States. It is plainly nothing more than an add-on to the first. Its purpose is to set up a backup method of appointment, for cases where the general method wouldn't work. The ordinary power of appointment belongs to the president and Senate TOGETHER, so it can only be used while the Senate is in session. But it would have been unreasonable to force the Senate to stay in session all the time just to appoint officers. And vacancies might occur DURING ITS RECESS that needed to be filled right away for the public good. So the second clause is obviously meant to let the president, ALONE, make temporary appointments "during the recess of the Senate, by granting commissions which shall expire at the end of their next session."

**Second,** if this clause is an add-on to the one before it, then the VACANCIES it mentions must refer to the "officers" described in that first clause. And as we have seen, that description leaves out members of the Senate.

**Third,** the clause's timing makes its meaning even clearer. The power works "during the recess of the Senate," and the appointments last "to the end of the next session" of that body. If the clause had been meant to cover senators, it would naturally have tied the temporary power to the recess of the *state legislatures*, which make the permanent choice of senators. It would not have tied it to the recess of the national Senate, which has no part in choosing senators. And it would have made temporary senators serve until the next session of the legislature of the state whose seat was empty, not until the end of the next session of the national Senate.

Naturally, the situation of the body that makes the permanent appointments would shape the rules for the temporary ones. The national Senate is the only body whose situation this clause has in mind. So the vacancies it refers to can only be offices in which the Senate shares the power of appointment with the president.

**Last,** and most decisive of all, the first and second clauses of the third section of the first article remove any possible doubt. They also destroy any excuse for misunderstanding. The first says that "the Senate of the United States shall be composed of two Senators from each State, chosen BY THE LEGISLATURE THEREOF for six years." The second says that "if vacancies in that body should happen by resignation or otherwise, DURING THE RECESS OF THE LEGISLATURE OF ANY STATE, the Executive THEREOF may make temporary appointments until the NEXT MEETING OF THE LEGISLATURE, which shall then fill such vacancies."

Here, in clear and plain words, is an express power given to the *state executives* to fill unexpected vacancies in the Senate with temporary appointments. This doesn't just disprove the idea that the earlier clause was meant to give that power to the President of the United States. It proves that this idea, which doesn't even have the merit of sounding believable, must have come from an intention to deceive the people. That intention is too obvious to be hidden by clever arguments, and too outrageous to be excused by pretending otherwise.

:::commentary
Hamilton's reading is careful and convincing. The Constitution gave the president a power to fill vacancies "during the Recess of the Senate," but in the very article on the Senate it gave the job of filling Senate vacancies to each state's governor. As Hamilton says, the timing gives the game away: a power tied to the *Senate's* schedule makes sense for officials the Senate confirms, not for senators chosen by state legislatures.

The **17th Amendment** (1913) changed how Senate vacancies are filled. Since senators are now elected by the people, a vacancy must be filled by a special election. But a state's legislature may let the governor make a temporary appointment until that election is held, and most states do. Either way, the president has never had any role in filling Senate seats.
:::

:::commentary
The president's power to make **recess appointments** became important in its own right. Presidents have used it many times to fill posts when the Senate was away. They have also sometimes used it to install nominees the Senate had not approved, which led to clashes with the Senate. To block them, the Senate has often held brief "pro forma" sessions every few days during its breaks. In *NLRB v. Noel Canning* (2014), the Supreme Court ruled that these short sessions count, and that a break of fewer than ten days is usually too short to count as a recess.
:::

:::vocab
Supplement: Something added to complete or extend something else.
Sophistry: Clever but misleading arguments.
Palliate: To make something seem less serious or less wrong than it is.
:::

## Why I've been so harsh {orig=11}

I have taken the trouble to pick out this example of misrepresentation, and to put it in a clear and strong light. I have done so because it is undeniable proof of the unjustified tricks being used to keep people from judging the Constitution fairly and impartially on its real merits.

In so outrageous a case, I haven't hesitated to allow myself a harshness of criticism that doesn't fit the general spirit of these papers. I am happy to let any fair and honest opponent of the proposed government decide this question: could any words be too harsh for so shameless and corrupt an attempt to deceive the citizens of America?

:::quote
I hesitate not to submit it to the decision of any candid and honest adversary of the proposed government, whether language can furnish epithets of too much asperity, for so shameless and so prostitute an attempt to impose on the citizens of America.

Meaning: Hamilton dares even fair-minded opponents to say his harsh words go too far. "Epithets" are insulting names, "asperity" means harshness, and to "impose on" people means to deceive them.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the angriest passages in the Federalist Papers. Usually Publius tries to sound calm and reasonable, and Hamilton admits as much. Here he accuses an opponent not of a mistake but of a deliberate lie.

Was he fair? Hamilton is right that the Constitution clearly gives the power to the states. But it's possible Cato simply misread the clause instead of lying on purpose. The Constitution was new and long, and many readers were working through it quickly. Accusing an opponent of bad faith was a powerful debating move, and Hamilton may have chosen this example because it was the easiest to win.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's complaint about wild exaggerations in political debate still sounds familiar. Today, false or misleading claims can spread quickly online, and both sides of political debates accuse each other of spreading them. Hamilton's method is a useful model: instead of just insisting the other side is wrong, he quotes the exact words of the Constitution and shows, step by step, why the claim doesn't fit. His essay also raises a question about tone. Is it ever right to call an opponent a liar, or does that make honest debate harder? People disagree about where the line between strong criticism and personal attack should be.
:::
