:::summary
In this short essay, Hamilton answers critics who said the new national government would cost too much. He argues that breaking up the Union would cost even more. If the states split into two or three regional confederacies, each one would be about as large as Great Britain and would need a full government of its own, nearly as large as the one the Constitution proposes. Instead of paying for one national government, Americans would be paying for several. On top of that, each confederacy would need customs officers to stop smuggling across its borders, and armies to defend itself against its neighbors. Disunion, he concludes, would harm Americans' wallets as much as their peace, trade, revenue, and liberty.
:::

:::keypoints
- Saving money in one area leaves more for others, and means **lower taxes** for the people.
- One Union means **one national government to pay for**. Several confederacies would mean **several**.
- Hardly anyone proposed thirteen separate nations. The most common idea was **three confederacies**: North, Middle, and South.
- Each of those would be **larger than Great Britain** and would need a government **just as complete** as the one proposed.
- Hamilton thinks a split into **two confederacies**, North and South, is even more likely. Pennsylvania would probably side with the North to avoid becoming "**the Flanders of America**."
- Thirteen states can support a national government **more easily than any smaller group** can.
- Disunion would also mean paying for **border guards** against smuggling and **armies** against neighbors.
:::

:::context Background
One common complaint against the Constitution was its cost. The new government would have a president, a Senate, a House of Representatives, federal courts, and officials to collect taxes. Under the Articles of Confederation, there had been only a single-house Congress with a small staff. Anti-Federalists warned that the new government would be expensive and would raise taxes to pay for itself.

This essay is the last of Hamilton's essays on the economic advantages of union, following [No. 11](/papers/11/) on trade and [No. 12](/papers/12/) on revenue. It is also one of the shortest of all the Federalist Papers.
:::

## One government or several? {orig=1}

Since we have been discussing revenue, it makes sense to consider economy, meaning saving money in government, as well. Money saved in one area can be put to good use in another, and less will have to be taken from the people's pockets.

If the states are united under one government, there will be only one national civil list to support. If they are divided into several confederacies, there will be just as many national civil lists to pay for. And each of them, at least in its main departments, would have to be as large as the one needed to govern the whole country.

Few people support splitting the states into thirteen unconnected nations. That plan is too extreme and too dangerous. People who imagine breaking up the country generally think in terms of three confederacies. One would be made up of the four Northern states, another of the four Middle states, and a third of the five Southern states. It is unlikely that there would be more than that.

Under this plan, each confederacy would cover more land than the kingdom of Great Britain. No well-informed person will think that such a confederacy could be run well by a government with fewer branches and offices than the one the Convention has proposed.

Once a state reaches a certain size, it needs the same energy in its government, and the same kind of administration, as a state that is much larger. This idea can't be proven exactly, because there is no rule for measuring how much governing power is needed to govern a given number of people. But think about the island of Britain, which is about the same size as each of the imagined confederacies. It has about eight million people. Consider how much authority it takes to direct the passions of such a large society toward the public good. Then we will see no reason to doubt that the same amount of power would be enough to do the same job in a much larger society.

Governing power, if it is well organized and used properly, can spread its force over a very large area. Through a wise arrangement of lower-level institutions, it can in a sense reproduce itself in every part of a great empire.

:::vocab
Economy: Here, saving money and avoiding waste, especially in government spending.
Civil list: A British term for the cost of running a government's civilian side, such as the salaries of its officials. Hamilton uses it to mean the basic cost of a national government.
Confederacy: A loose union of independent states. Some opponents suggested splitting the thirteen states into two or three regional confederacies.
Energy (in government): Strength and the ability to act effectively.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's key point is that government costs don't simply shrink with size. A small nation still needs a head of government, a legislature, courts, a treasury, diplomats, and a military. So three regional nations would pay for three of each, instead of one. Economists today call this an **economy of scale**: some costs stay about the same whether you serve a small group or a large one, so serving a larger group costs less per person.

His last sentence answers the old idea, made famous by Montesquieu, that a republic must be small (see [No. 9](/papers/9/) and [No. 10](/papers/10/)). Hamilton suggests that a well-designed government, working through state and local institutions, can reach every part of a large country.
:::

## Two confederacies are more likely {orig=2}

Each confederacy would need a government just as complete as the one proposed. That point becomes even stronger if we consider another possibility, which is more likely than three confederacies. Look closely at geography and trade, and at the habits and prejudices of the different states. Then you will conclude that if the Union broke up, the states would most naturally join together under two governments.

The four Eastern states, because of all the ties that bind people together as a nation, can surely be expected to unite. New York, given its location, would never be foolish enough to expose a weak, unsupported side to the power of that confederacy. There are other obvious reasons that would make it easy for New York to join it. New Jersey is too small a state to think of standing on the border against this even more powerful combination, and there seems to be nothing to stop it from joining too.

Even Pennsylvania would have strong reasons to join the Northern league. Its true policy is an active foreign trade carried in its own ships, and that matches the views and character of its citizens. The more southern states, for various reasons, may not think they have much interest in encouraging American shipping. They may prefer a system that lets all nations freely carry their goods as well as buy them. Pennsylvania may not want to mix its interests with a union whose policy is so opposed to its own.

Pennsylvania must be a border state in any case. So it may decide that it is safer to have its exposed side face the weaker Southern confederacy rather than the stronger Northern one. That would give it the best chance of not becoming the Flanders of America. But whatever Pennsylvania decides, if the Northern confederacy includes New Jersey, there will probably be no more than one confederacy south of that state.

:::vocab
Eastern states: In 1787, the New England states: New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Connecticut.
Flanders: A region in what is now Belgium and northern France. Because it lay between powerful rivals, it was the scene of so many European wars that it was called a battleground of Europe.
Navigation: Here, shipping, and the business of carrying goods by sea.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction of a split between North and South was eerily close to what happened in 1861, when eleven Southern states left the Union and formed the Confederate States of America. But the cause he names here, a disagreement over **shipping policy**, was not the main cause of the Civil War. The deepest division was slavery, which Hamilton does not mention.

Still, the issue he does mention was connected to slavery. Southern planters exported crops like tobacco and rice and wanted the cheapest shipping available, from any nation. Northern merchants wanted laws favoring American ships. At the Constitutional Convention, these interests struck a deal. Congress was allowed to pass shipping laws by a simple majority, and the Constitution barred Congress from banning the importation of enslaved people before 1808.
:::

## More states can share the cost {orig=3}

Nothing could be clearer than this. Thirteen states can support a national government more easily than half of them, or a third of them, or any number less than all of them. This point should go a long way toward answering the objection to the proposed plan that is based on its cost. And when we look at that objection more closely, it will turn out to rest on mistaken grounds in every way.

## The hidden costs of disunion {orig=4}

So far, I have pointed only to the cost of several governments. Now add the many people who would have to be hired to guard the inland borders between the confederacies against smuggling. The need for revenue would surely create such jobs in time. Then add the military forces that, as I have shown, would be sure to grow out of the jealousies and conflicts among the separate nations the states would become. Once we add all this up, we will clearly see what separation would do to every part of the country. It would hurt their ability to save money just as much as their peace, trade, revenue, and liberty.

:::quote
...a separation would be not less injurious to the economy, than to the tranquillity, commerce, revenue, and liberty of every part.

Meaning: Breaking up the Union would cost Americans more money, as well as costing them their peace, their trade, their tax revenue, and their freedom.
:::

:::commentary
This final paragraph ties together the arguments of the essays before it. The border guards come from [No. 12](/papers/12/), which described France's army of 20,000 customs patrols. The military costs come from [No. 8](/papers/8/), which warned that rival states would build standing armies. Hamilton's message to cost-conscious voters is: *the Constitution may look expensive, but the alternative is more expensive.*

Critics could answer that Hamilton is comparing the Constitution with the worst possible alternative. Many Anti-Federalists did not want to break up the Union at all. They wanted to keep a single union but amend the Articles, which they believed would be cheaper than the new system. Hamilton and the other authors argue in other essays, especially [No. 15](/papers/15/) through [No. 22](/papers/22/), that the Articles could not be saved.
:::

:::connection
This essay closes the group of papers on the advantages of union. Hamilton's arguments here draw on [No. 6](/papers/6/) through [No. 8](/papers/8/) (wars between the states) and [No. 11](/papers/11/) and [No. 12](/papers/12/) (trade and revenue). Madison's [No. 14](/papers/14/) follows by arguing that a republic can work over a territory as large as the United States.
:::

:::today
Arguments about the cost of government are still central to American politics. People still debate whether some services are better and cheaper when run by the national government or by states and cities. Supporters of national programs often make Hamilton's argument: one system is more efficient than fifty separate ones. Supporters of state and local control answer that governments closer to the people can tailor services to local needs and are easier to hold accountable. The same debate shows up around the world, for example when regions talk about breaking away from a country and voters must weigh what independence would cost.
:::
