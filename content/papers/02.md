:::summary
John Jay asks the most basic question of the debate: should Americans stay **one nation** under one government, or split into several separate confederacies? Until recently, everyone agreed that union was essential. Now some politicians say otherwise. Jay argues that geography and history have made Americans one people, sharing one connected land, the same ancestry, language, religion, and principles, and a war fought side by side. The first national government was weak because it was created in the middle of a war. Now a convention of respected leaders, working calmly for months, has proposed a better one. Jay reminds readers that the Continental Congress of 1774 was attacked too, yet its advice proved wise.
:::

:::keypoints
- People must give up **some natural rights** to create a government with the powers it needs.
- The key question: **one nation** under one national government, or **several separate confederacies**?
- Until recently, everyone agreed Americans' prosperity depended on **staying united**. Now some politicians, including some former supporters of union, disagree.
- America is **one connected country**, linked by rivers and waterways, as if designed to be held together.
- Americans are **one people**, with shared ancestry, language, religion, principles of government, and a war fought together.
- The Articles were weak because they were written **in the middle of a war**.
- The Convention met **calmly in peacetime** for months and included many of the nation's most respected leaders.
- The plan is only **recommended**, not imposed. It deserves neither blind approval nor blind rejection.
- The **Congress of 1774** was attacked too, but the people wisely followed its advice.
:::

:::context Background
John Jay was a New York lawyer and diplomat who had served as president of the Continental Congress and helped negotiate the 1783 peace treaty with Britain. In 1787 he was the nation's Secretary for Foreign Affairs, so he knew better than anyone how weak America looked to other nations.

Jay was supposed to write many more essays on foreign affairs, but he became ill (possibly rheumatism) after No. 5. He returned only to write [No. 64](/papers/64/) on treaties. This essay begins the series' first major argument: why the Union is valuable.
:::

## The most important question {orig=1-3}

The people of America are now being asked to decide a question that will prove to be one of the most important they have ever faced. So it's clear they should look at it carefully and from every angle.

Nothing is more certain than that government is necessary. And it is equally certain that whenever and however a government is set up, the people must give up some of their natural rights to give it the powers it needs.

So it's worth carefully considering which would better serve the interests of the American people. Should they be one nation, for all general purposes, under one federal government? Or should they split into separate confederacies, and give each one's leaders the same kind of powers they are being advised to give one national government?

Until recently, everyone agreed without question that the prosperity of the American people depended on staying firmly united. The wishes, prayers, and efforts of our best and wisest citizens have always aimed at that goal. But now some politicians claim this view is wrong. Instead of looking for safety and happiness in union, they say, we should seek them by dividing the states into separate confederacies or nations.

However strange this new idea may seem, it has supporters. And some people who strongly opposed it before now support it. Whatever arguments or motives changed these gentlemen's views, it would surely be unwise for the people to adopt these new political ideas without being fully convinced they are based on truth and good policy.

:::vocab
Natural rights: Rights people have simply by being human, before any government exists, such as life and liberty.
Cede: To give up or hand over.
Confederacy: A loose union of states.
:::

:::commentary
Jay starts with an idea from the philosopher John Locke that most Americans in 1787 accepted. People are born with natural rights, but to live safely together, they agree to give up some of their freedom to a government. The question isn't *whether* to give up some rights. It's how best to arrange the government that receives them.

The idea of splitting into regional confederacies (for example, New England, the Middle States, and the South) was really being discussed in private, though few people said it openly. Jay and Hamilton treat it as the real alternative to the Constitution.
:::

## One country, made to be united {orig=4}

It has often given me pleasure to see that independent America is not made up of separate, distant territories. Instead, our free western people were given one connected, fertile, wide-spreading country. Providence has especially blessed it with a variety of soils and crops, and watered it with countless streams for the enjoyment and convenience of its people.

A series of navigable waters forms a kind of chain around its borders, as if to tie it together. Meanwhile, the most noble rivers in the world, flowing at convenient distances from each other, give the people highways for easily sending help to one another and for transporting and trading their various goods.

:::vocab
Providence: God's care and guidance of the world. Americans in the 1700s often spoke of Providence as shaping their history.
Navigable: Deep and wide enough for boats to travel on.
:::

## One people, made to be united {orig=5-7}

With equal pleasure, I have often noticed that Providence has chosen to give this one connected country to one united people. They are descended from the same ancestors, speak the same language, practice the same religion, and are devoted to the same principles of government. They are very similar in their manners and customs. And through their shared advice, weapons, and efforts, fighting side by side through a long and bloody war, they have nobly won liberty and independence for all.

This country and this people seem to have been made for each other. It seems that Providence intended that such a fitting inheritance, given to a band of brothers united by the strongest ties, should never be split into a number of unfriendly, jealous, and foreign nations.

Until now, people of every class and religious group among us have shared these feelings. For all general purposes, we have always been one people. Every citizen everywhere has enjoyed the same national rights, privileges, and protection. As a nation, we have made peace and war. As a nation, we have defeated our common enemies. As a nation, we have formed alliances, made treaties, and entered into agreements with foreign countries.

:::quote
...Providence has been pleased to give this one connected country to one united people--a people descended from the same ancestors, speaking the same language, professing the same religion, attached to the same principles of government, very similar in their manners and customs...

Meaning: Jay argues that Americans were already one people because they shared ancestry, language, religion, and political ideas.
:::

:::commentary
This is the most famous passage in the essay, and it deserves a careful, critical reading. Jay's picture of a single, uniform people was an exaggeration even in 1787:

- **Ancestry:** Many Americans had English roots, but there were also large communities of Scottish, Irish, German, Dutch, French, and Swedish descent. Jay's own ancestors were French Huguenots (Protestant refugees) and Dutch.
- **Language:** German was widely spoken in Pennsylvania, and Dutch was still common in parts of New York.
- **Religion:** Americans were mostly Protestant, but deeply divided among Congregationalists, Anglicans, Presbyterians, Baptists, Quakers, Methodists, and others. There were also Catholics and Jews.
- **Who is left out:** About 700,000 enslaved Africans and African Americans, nearly one in five people in the country, are not part of Jay's "one united people." Neither are Native Americans.

Jay's goal was persuasion. He wanted readers to feel they belonged together. It's worth asking whether a nation has to be based on shared ancestry and religion, or whether, as many later Americans argued, it can be based on shared ideals.
:::

## The first government was made in wartime {orig=8-9}

A strong sense of how valuable union is led the people, very early on, to set up a federal government to preserve it and make it last. They created it almost as soon as they existed as a political community. In fact, they created it at a time when their homes were burning, many of their citizens were bleeding, and the spread of war and destruction left little room for the calm, careful thought that must always come before creating a wise and well-balanced government for a free people. It's no wonder that a government created in such unfavorable times turned out, when tried, to be seriously flawed and unable to do its job.

These intelligent people saw these flaws and regretted them. They remained just as attached to union as they were in love with liberty. They saw the danger that immediately threatened union, and more distantly threatened liberty. And they were convinced that both could only be fully protected by a more wisely designed national government. So, as if with one voice, they called the recent convention at Philadelphia to take up this important subject.

:::commentary
Jay offers a kind and fair explanation for the Articles of Confederation's flaws: they were written during the Revolutionary War, when there was no time for careful thought. Congress proposed the Articles in 1777, during some of the war's darkest days, though they weren't ratified until 1781. Notice how Jay links union and liberty. Without union, he argues, liberty will eventually be lost too.
:::

## The Convention worked calmly in peacetime {orig=10-11}

This convention was made up of men the people trusted. Many had become highly respected for their patriotism, good character, and wisdom during times that tested people's minds and hearts. They took on this difficult task. In the calm season of peace, with their minds free of other concerns, they spent many months in cool, uninterrupted daily discussion. Finally, without being intimidated by power or influenced by any passion except love of country, they presented and recommended to the people the plan produced by their shared and nearly unanimous decisions.

It's true that this plan is only RECOMMENDED, not forced on anyone. But remember that it is recommended neither for BLIND approval nor for BLIND rejection. It is recommended for the calm and fair consideration that such an important subject deserves.

But as I said in the previous essay, this is more to be hoped for than expected. Past experience teaches us not to be too optimistic.

:::commentary
Jay's description of the Convention is flattering. "Many months" was about four months, from May to September 1787. And it wasn't free of passion. The delegates argued fiercely, especially over representation. But it's true that the Convention included many of the country's most respected figures, including George Washington, who presided, and Benjamin Franklin.

The point that the plan is "only recommended" is important. The Convention had no power to make the Constitution law. Only the people, through state ratifying conventions, could do that.
:::

## Remember the Congress of 1774 {orig=11-13}

Many still remember that well-founded fears of immediate danger led the American people to form the famous Congress of 1774. That body recommended certain measures to the people who sent it, and events proved those measures wise.

Yet we all remember how quickly the press filled up with pamphlets and weekly papers attacking those very measures. Some government officials attacked them out of personal interest. Others did so because they misjudged the consequences, or were too attached to their old loyalties, or had ambitions that didn't fit the public good. All of them worked tirelessly to persuade the people to reject the advice of that patriotic Congress. Many people were fooled and misled. But the great majority reasoned and decided wisely, and they are glad today that they did.

They reasoned that the Congress was made up of many wise and experienced men. Since its members came from different parts of the country, they brought with them and shared a great deal of useful information. During the time they spent together studying and discussing their country's true interests, they must have gained a very accurate understanding of them. Each of them had a personal stake in public liberty and prosperity, so it was their wish as well as their duty to recommend only measures that, after careful thought, they truly believed were wise.

These and similar reasons led the people to rely heavily on the Congress's judgment and honesty. They took its advice, despite all the tricks and efforts used to stop them.

The people had reason to trust the members of that Congress, even though few of them had been fully tested or were widely known. They have even more reason now to respect the judgment and advice of the Convention. It is well known that some of the most distinguished members of that Congress, who have since been tested and rightly praised for their patriotism and abilities, and who have grown old gaining political knowledge, were also members of this Convention. They brought all their knowledge and experience with them.

:::vocab
Congress of 1774: The First Continental Congress, which met in Philadelphia to coordinate the colonies' response to British policies. It called for a boycott of British goods.
Constituents: The people a representative serves.
:::

:::commentary
Jay compares the Constitution's opponents to the Loyalists who opposed the First Continental Congress in 1774. That was a sharp comparison, since Loyalists had sided with Britain. Jay himself had been a member of the First Continental Congress, so he was speaking from experience.

His argument is partly an appeal to authority: trust these men because of who they are and what they've done. That kind of argument can be reasonable, since experience counts for something. But it isn't proof. Experienced, respected people can still be wrong. Notice that Madison, in [No. 37](/papers/37/), asks readers to judge the plan on its merits instead.
:::

## Why would anyone want to divide us now? {orig=14}

It's worth noting that not only the first Congress, but every Congress since, and the recent Convention as well, has always agreed with the people that America's prosperity depends on its Union. Preserving and continuing the Union was the people's great goal in forming the Convention. It is also the great goal of the plan the Convention has advised them to adopt.

So why, at this moment, are some men trying to make the Union seem less important? And why are some suggesting that three or four confederacies would be better than one?

I am personally convinced that the people have always been right about this, and that their constant and universal attachment to the Union rests on great and serious reasons. I will try to explain those reasons in the essays that follow.

Those who promote the idea of replacing the Convention's plan with several separate confederacies seem to see clearly that rejecting it would put the survival of the Union in the greatest danger. That would certainly be the result. And I sincerely hope every good citizen sees just as clearly that, if the Union ever breaks apart, America will have reason to cry out in the words of the poet: "FAREWELL! A LONG FAREWELL TO ALL MY GREATNESS."

:::quote
"FAREWELL! A LONG FAREWELL TO ALL MY GREATNESS."

Meaning: Jay quotes Shakespeare's play *Henry VIII*. The line is spoken by Cardinal Wolsey, a powerful man who has just lost everything. Jay warns that America would suffer the same fall if the Union broke apart.
:::

:::today
Jay's question, "what makes us one people?", is still debated. His answer relied heavily on shared ancestry, language, and religion. Over time, as the country became far more diverse, many Americans came to a different answer: that the United States is held together by shared ideals, like those in the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, rather than by shared bloodlines. This idea is sometimes called "civic nationalism." Debates about immigration, national identity, and what it means to be American often come back to the contrast between Jay's vision and that later one.
:::
