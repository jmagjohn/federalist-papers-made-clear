:::summary
Critics of the Constitution attacked two clauses as monsters that would swallow up the states. The first lets Congress make all laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out its powers. The second makes the Constitution, and national laws made under it, the "supreme law of the land." Hamilton says both clauses only state what would be true anyway. A power to do something must include the power to use the means needed to do it. And a law that isn't supreme over the people it governs isn't really a law. He also points out a key limit: only laws made *in line with* the Constitution are supreme. A national law that tried to block a state tax would not be supreme. It would be a grab for power the Constitution never gave.
:::

:::keypoints
- The **necessary and proper clause** and the **supremacy clause** were two of the most attacked parts of the Constitution.
- Hamilton says the government would work exactly the same **with or without** these clauses. They only state what is already implied.
- A **power** to do something includes the power to use the **means** needed to do it. For a legislature, those means are laws.
- The clause was added for **caution**, to stop people from later trying to shrink the Union's lawful powers with clever arguments.
- The national government judges its own powers first, but **the people** judge last. If it oversteps, the people can act to correct it.
- National laws must be **supreme**, or the Union would be a mere treaty, not a government.
- Only laws made **"in pursuance"** of the Constitution are supreme. Laws that invade the states' remaining powers are **usurpations**.
- The states keep full power to raise money by **any kind of tax** except taxes on imports and exports.
:::

:::context Background
This paper is part of a group of essays (Nos. 30–36) in which Hamilton defends the national government's power to tax. Anti-Federalists, the opponents of the Constitution, feared that Congress would use this power to take every source of money away from the states. One of the best-known Anti-Federalist writers, who called himself "Brutus," warned that the necessary and proper clause and the supremacy clause together would let the national government swallow the states.

In No. 33, Hamilton answers that fear. Madison returns to the same two clauses in [No. 44](/papers/44/), and the argument over what "necessary and proper" means has never really ended.
:::

## The two clauses the critics attack {orig=1-2}

The rest of the argument against the Constitution's taxing power is built on two clauses. The last clause of Article 1, section 8 gives Congress the power "to make all laws which shall be NECESSARY and PROPER for carrying into execution THE POWERS by that Constitution vested in the government of the United States, or in any department or officer thereof."

The second clause of Article 6 says that "the Constitution and the laws of the United States made IN PURSUANCE THEREOF, and the treaties made by their authority shall be the SUPREME LAW of the land, any thing in the constitution or laws of any State to the contrary notwithstanding."

These two clauses have been the target of furious attacks and angry speeches. Critics have shown them to the people in the most exaggerated and false colors. They call them the deadly machines that will destroy the state governments and wipe out the people's liberties. They call them a hideous monster whose hungry jaws will spare no one: not men or women, not young or old, not rich or poor, not the holy or the common.

Yet after all this noise, I can say with complete confidence that the government would work *exactly the same* if these clauses were erased entirely, as it would if they were repeated in every article. This may surprise people who haven't looked at the clauses the way I have. But the clauses only declare a truth that follows, necessarily and unavoidably, from the very act of creating a federal government and giving it certain specific powers. This is so clear that even the calmest person can hardly listen to all the abuse heaped on this part of the plan without losing his temper.

:::vocab
Necessary and proper clause: The last clause of Article 1, section 8. It lets Congress make the laws needed to carry out its other powers. It is sometimes called the "elastic clause."
Supremacy clause: Article 6, clause 2 of the Constitution. It makes the Constitution, national laws made under it, and treaties the "supreme law of the land," above state laws.
:::

:::commentary
The capital letters are Hamilton's. In the 1700s, writers used them the way we use bold or italics. He stresses the words "necessary and proper," "the powers," "in pursuance thereof," and "supreme law" because his whole argument depends on them.

Notice how strongly Hamilton describes his opponents. They speak in "virulent invective" and "petulant declamation," meaning bitter attacks and childish rants. This is persuasion, not proof. The Anti-Federalists had a serious worry: a clause that let Congress pass any law it called "necessary" seemed to have no clear limit.
:::

## A power includes the means to use it {orig=3-4}

What is a power, except the ability to do a thing? What is the ability to do a thing, except the power to use the *means* needed to do it? What is a *legislative* power, except a power to make *laws*? What are the means of carrying out a legislative power, except *laws*? What is the power to set and collect taxes, except a legislative power, a power to *make laws* to set and collect taxes? And what are the proper means of carrying out that power, except *necessary and proper* laws?

This simple chain of questions gives us a test for judging what the clause really is. It leads us to an obvious truth. A power to set and collect taxes must be a power to pass all laws necessary and proper for using that power. So what does this unlucky and slandered clause do, except declare the same truth? It says that Congress, which has already been given the power to tax, may pass all laws necessary and proper to put that power into effect.

I have applied this reasoning to the taxing power in particular for two reasons. It is the subject we are now discussing, and it is the most important power the Union is to receive. But the same reasoning leads to the same result for every other power in the Constitution. And the "sweeping clause," as critics have taken to calling it, lets Congress pass necessary and proper laws *only* to carry out those listed powers.

So if anything is objectionable, you must look for it in the specific powers that this general statement rests on. The statement itself may be guilty of repeating the obvious. But it is at least perfectly harmless.

:::vocab
Tautology: Saying the same thing twice in different words, so that the second part adds nothing new.
Enumerated powers: The specific powers listed in the Constitution, mainly in Article 1, section 8.
:::

:::commentary
This is the heart of Hamilton's argument. The clause doesn't add any new powers, he says. It only lets Congress use the listed powers. If you think Congress has too much power, he says, complain about the list, not about this clause.

The question he leaves open is what counts as "necessary." Does it mean *absolutely* necessary, or just *useful* and *convenient*? That question became a major fight only three years later. In 1791, Hamilton, then secretary of the treasury, proposed a national bank. Thomas Jefferson and James Madison argued that a bank was not truly "necessary" for carrying out any listed power, so it was unconstitutional. Hamilton argued that "necessary" only means useful or helpful to the goal. President Washington sided with Hamilton and signed the bank bill.
:::

## Why include the clause at all? {orig=5}

But *suspicion* may ask: if the clause adds nothing, why was it put in? The only answer can be that it was added for extra caution. It guards against people who might someday want to shrink or dodge the Union's lawful powers through clever, hair-splitting arguments.

The Convention probably foresaw something that has been a main point of these papers. The greatest danger to our political well-being is that the state governments will eventually wear away the foundations of the Union. So the Convention may have thought it necessary, on such a basic point, to leave nothing open to interpretation.

Whatever the reason was, the outcry against the clause shows how wise the precaution was. That outcry reveals a desire to question the great and essential truth that the clause plainly sets out to declare.

:::commentary
Hamilton turns the critics' anger around. If the clause only states the obvious, why are people so upset about it? Because, he suggests, they want to deny the obvious and weaken the national government. That is a clever debating move. It also shows what Hamilton feared most: not a national government that was too strong, but states that would pull the Union apart, as they had under the Articles of Confederation.
:::

## Who decides what is necessary and proper? {orig=6}

But someone may ask again: who will judge whether the laws passed to carry out the Union's powers are *necessary* and *proper*?

My first answer is that this question comes up just as much from the simple grant of the powers as from this clause. My second answer is that the national government, like every other government, must judge first whether it is using its powers properly. Its constituents, the people, must judge last. Suppose the federal government goes beyond the just limits of its authority and uses its powers like a tyrant. Then the people, who created it, must appeal to the standard they set up, the Constitution. They must take whatever steps the emergency calls for and good sense allows to repair the damage done to the Constitution.

Whether a law is proper, in a constitutional sense, must always depend on the nature of the powers it is based on. Suppose Congress stretched its authority in some forced way (which is hard to imagine) and tried to change a state's law of inheritance. Wouldn't it be obvious that it had gone beyond its own authority and invaded the state's? Or suppose Congress claimed that a state land tax interfered with national revenue, and tried to cancel it. Wouldn't it be just as obvious that this invaded the states' shared authority over that kind of tax, which the Constitution plainly assumes the states have?

If there is ever any doubt about this, the credit will belong entirely to those critics who, in their reckless hatred of the Convention's plan, have worked hard to wrap it in a cloud that hides the plainest and simplest truths.

:::vocab
Constituents: The people a government or representative serves.
Law of descent: The rules for who inherits a person's property when he or she dies.
Concurrent jurisdiction: Authority shared by two governments at the same time. Both the nation and the states can tax most of the same things.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's answer here is the people, not the courts. If Congress oversteps, the people "must appeal to the standard they have formed." He doesn't spell out how. That could mean voting officials out, or, in an extreme case, resistance. A few months later, in [No. 78](/papers/78/), Hamilton gave the courts a central role: judges would declare laws void when they conflicted with the Constitution. Today, the Supreme Court is usually seen as the main judge of whether a law is "necessary and proper," though Congress, the president, and voters all play a part.

His two examples, inheritance law and state land taxes, were meant to show that some things are clearly off-limits to Congress. Whether the line is always that clear is another question.
:::

## What "supreme law" really means {orig=7}

But critics say that the laws of the Union are to be the *supreme law* of the land. What else could they be? What would they amount to if they were *not* supreme? Clearly, they would amount to nothing. A *law*, by the very meaning of the word, includes supremacy. It is a rule that the people it applies to are bound to obey.

This is true of every political association. When individuals join together into a society, that society's laws must be the supreme guide of their conduct. When a number of political societies join together into a larger one, the laws the larger society makes, using the powers its constitution gives it, must be supreme over the smaller societies and the people in them. Otherwise it would be a mere treaty, depending on the good faith of the parties. It would not be a government, which is only another word for *political power and supremacy*.

But it does *not* follow that every act of the larger society will be the supreme law of the land. Some acts may not be based on its constitutional powers. Some may invade the remaining authority of the smaller societies. These will be nothing but acts of usurpation, and they deserve to be treated as such.

So we see that the supremacy clause, like the clause we just discussed, only declares a truth that follows directly and necessarily from creating a federal government. You will have noticed that it *expressly* limits this supremacy to laws made *in pursuance of the Constitution*. I mention this only as an example of the Convention's caution. That limit would have been understood even if it hadn't been written down.

:::quote
A LAW, by the very meaning of the term, includes supremacy.

Meaning: A law that people are free to ignore isn't a law at all. If national laws weren't supreme, they would be just suggestions.
:::

:::quote
It would otherwise be a mere treaty, dependent on the good faith of the parties, and not a government, which is only another word for POLITICAL POWER AND SUPREMACY.

Meaning: The Articles of Confederation worked like a treaty: the states obeyed only when they felt like it. A real government must be able to make its laws binding.
:::

:::vocab
Usurp: To take power that doesn't rightfully belong to you.
Residuary sovereignty: The authority left over for the states after the national government's listed powers are taken out. The 10th Amendment later put this idea into words.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is making two points that pull in opposite directions. First, national laws *must* beat state laws, or the Union is just a treaty like the Articles of Confederation. Second, only laws made "in pursuance" of the Constitution get that rank. A law that goes beyond Congress's powers is not supreme at all.

Both halves are still part of American law. The Supreme Court used the supremacy clause in *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) to rule that a state could not tax a bank created by Congress. Chief Justice John Marshall famously wrote that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy." In the same case, Marshall read "necessary and proper" broadly, much as Hamilton had in 1791. But courts have also struck down national laws that went beyond Congress's powers, which is the second half of Hamilton's point.
:::

## Taxes: where national power stops {orig=8}

So a law setting a tax for the use of the United States would be supreme by its nature. No one could lawfully resist or control it. But a law that canceled or blocked the collection of a tax set by a state would *not* be the supreme law of the land. The only exception is taxes on imports and exports, which the Constitution reserves to the Union. Any other such law would be a usurpation of power the Constitution never granted.

Suppose both governments piled too many taxes on the same thing, so that collecting them became hard or uncertain. That would be a problem for both. It would not come from one side having more power than the other, or from either side lacking power. It would come from one or the other using its power unwisely, in a way that hurts both equally. But we can hope and expect that their shared interest will lead them to work together and avoid any serious trouble.

The conclusion from all this is that under the proposed Constitution, the states will keep an independent and uncontrollable power to raise as much money as they need, by every kind of tax except duties on imports and exports. In the next paper, I will show that this *concurrent jurisdiction* over taxes was the only acceptable alternative to making the states' taxing power completely subordinate to the Union's.

:::vocab
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
Subordinate: Lower in rank; under the control of something else.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's promise here is specific: the states can tax anything except imports and exports, and Congress can't stop them. Article 1, section 10 does bar states from taxing imports and exports without Congress's consent, so that exception is in the text.

The bigger promise, that national power would stay within narrow, clear limits, is the one people still argue about. Supporters of a broad reading of national power point to Hamilton's view that a power includes all the means to carry it out. Supporters of a narrow reading point to his examples of what Congress plainly *cannot* do, and to his promise that the states would keep their own independent powers.
:::

:::today
The necessary and proper clause is one of the most important sentences in the Constitution. It is the basis for many things Congress does that are not listed by name, from creating federal agencies to passing criminal laws that support its other powers. The debate Hamilton tried to settle is still alive. In 2012, in a major case about the Affordable Care Act, the Supreme Court split over whether the clause allowed Congress to require people to buy health insurance. Supporters of broad national power say Hamilton was right that a power must include the means to use it. Critics say a clause with no clear limit could justify almost anything, just as the Anti-Federalists warned. The supremacy clause is less controversial, but it is at the center of every conflict between state and national law.
:::
