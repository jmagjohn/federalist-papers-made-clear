:::summary
Hamilton begins a three-essay defense of one short clause in the Constitution. It lets each state legislature set the "times, places and manner" of elections for Congress, but allows Congress to change those rules by law. Hamilton's main principle is simple: "every government ought to contain in itself the means of its own preservation." If the states had the only say over federal elections, they could destroy the national government just by refusing to hold them. Opponents answer that the states already have this kind of power over the Senate, because state legislatures choose senators. Hamilton replies that this was a necessary price for giving the states a role in the national government, and that the Senate is much harder to shut down than the House. He warns that ambitious state leaders, or even foreign powers, might someday want to break up the Union.
:::

:::keypoints
- The Constitution lets **state legislatures** set the times, places, and manner of congressional elections, but **Congress can change** those rules.
- Hamilton's central principle: **"Every government ought to contain in itself the means of its own preservation."**
- No election law written into the Constitution could fit every future situation, so **someone** had to have ongoing power over elections.
- The Convention chose the best of three options: the states act **first**, and Congress has the **final** say.
- If the states had **sole control**, they could end the Union by simply **not holding elections**.
- The states' power to choose **senators** was a necessary exception, not a reason to add more risk.
- The **House** is more exposed than the Senate, because all its members are elected at once every two years.
- State **leaders** may not share the people's loyalty to the Union, and **foreign powers** may encourage plots against it.
:::

:::context Background
Article I, Section 4 of the Constitution is often called the **Elections Clause**. Anti-Federalists attacked it fiercely. They feared that Congress could use it to hold elections at odd times or in faraway places, so that only the wealthy or well-connected could vote. Some predicted that Congress might use it to stay in power or even to cancel elections. Later in 1788, several state ratifying conventions, including Massachusetts's, recommended amendments that would let Congress use this power only if a state failed to act.

Hamilton spends three papers on this clause. In this one, he explains why the national government needs the final word. In [No. 60](/papers/60/) and [No. 61](/papers/61/), he answers the fear that Congress would abuse it.
:::

## The clause and its critics {orig=1}

The natural order of our subject now leads us to the part of the Constitution that lets Congress regulate, as a last resort, the election of its own members. Here are its words:

> "The TIMES, PLACES, and MANNER of holding elections for senators and representatives shall be prescribed in each State by the legislature thereof; but the Congress may, at any time, by law, make or alter SUCH REGULATIONS, except as to the PLACES of choosing senators."

This provision has been attacked by those who condemn the whole Constitution. It has also been criticized by those whose objections have been narrower and more moderate. In one case, it has even been found objectionable by a gentleman who says he supports every other part of the plan.

:::commentary
In plain terms, the clause works like this. Each state decides when, where, and how its people vote for members of Congress. But Congress can pass a law that replaces or changes the state's rules.

Why the exception for "the places of choosing senators"? At the time, state legislatures chose senators. The Convention did not want Congress telling a state legislature where it had to meet. After the 17th Amendment (1913) gave the people the right to elect senators directly, this exception came to matter very little.
:::

## Every government must be able to protect itself {orig=2}

Even so, I would be greatly mistaken if there is any part of the whole plan that is easier to defend than this one. It rests on a plain truth: EVERY GOVERNMENT OUGHT TO CONTAIN IN ITSELF THE MEANS OF ITS OWN PRESERVATION.

Every fair-minded person will approve of the Convention for following this rule. He will also disapprove of every departure from it, unless the departure was forced by the need to include some particular feature that couldn't fit the rule exactly. Even then, he may accept that it was necessary. But he will still see such a departure from so basic a principle as a flaw in the system. And he will regret it, since it may become the seed of future weakness, and perhaps anarchy.

:::quote
Every government ought to contain in itself the means of its own preservation.

Meaning: A government should never have to depend on someone else's goodwill just to keep existing. It must have the tools to keep itself alive.
:::

:::vocab
Preservation: Keeping something safe and in existence.
Anarchy: A state of disorder with no effective government.
:::

## Three ways to place the power {orig=3}

No one will claim that an election law could have been written into the Constitution that would fit every likely change in the country's situation. So no one will deny that some flexible power over elections had to exist somewhere.

I assume people will just as readily agree that there were only three sensible ways to arrange this power. It could have been placed:

- entirely in Congress;
- entirely in the state legislatures; or
- first in the state legislatures, and finally in Congress.

The Convention wisely chose the last way. It has given the rules for federal elections, in the first place, to the state governments. In ordinary times, and when no one has bad intentions, this may be more convenient and more satisfying to the people. But the Convention has kept for the national government a right to step in whenever unusual circumstances make that necessary for its safety.

:::vocab
Discretionary power: Power that lets officials decide what to do as situations change, instead of following a fixed rule.
Interposition: Stepping in between. Later, some Southern leaders used "interposition" to claim states could block national laws they thought unconstitutional.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point here is practical. A country's election needs change over time, so the Constitution couldn't spell out every detail. Someone had to be able to make and update the rules. The Convention split the job: states write the everyday rules, and Congress holds a backup power.

Notice that Hamilton presents Congress's power as something for "extraordinary circumstances." In practice, Congress has used it more broadly. For example, it has set a single national Election Day and required states to offer certain ways to register to vote.
:::

## Why the states can't have the final word {orig=4-5}

Nothing could be clearer than this. If the state legislatures had the *only* power to regulate elections for the national government, the Union's existence would be entirely at their mercy. At any moment they could destroy it, simply by failing to arrange for the election of the people who run it.

It does little good to say that the states would probably never neglect this duty. The fact that the Constitution would make it *possible*, with nothing gained in return for the risk, is an objection no one can answer. And no one has yet given a good reason for taking that risk. The wild guesses of a sick, suspicious mind can never deserve to be called reasons.

If we are in the mood to expect abuses of power, it is just as fair to expect them from the state governments as from the national government. And it fits sound principles better to trust the Union with the care of its own existence than to hand that care to anyone else. So if we must risk abuse of power on one side or the other, it is more reasonable to risk it where the power naturally belongs than where it doesn't.

Suppose the Constitution had included an article letting the United States regulate the elections for the state governments. Would anyone have hesitated to condemn it? People would have called it an unjustified shift of power, and a tool deliberately designed to destroy the state governments. In that case, the violation of principle would need no explanation. To a fair observer, the violation is just as clear in a plan that would make the national government's existence depend, in the same way, on the wishes of the state governments. Anyone who looks at the question fairly must agree that each government ought, as far as possible, to depend on itself for its own survival.

:::commentary
Hamilton's argument cuts both ways, and that is its strength. Americans would never accept a rule letting Congress run *state* elections, because Congress could then wipe out the states. By the same logic, the states shouldn't hold the only key to *national* elections.

Hamilton admits the states probably wouldn't refuse to hold elections. His point is that a constitution should not leave a door open to disaster when closing it costs nothing. This way of thinking, which plans for the worst case instead of hoping for the best, runs all through the Federalist Papers.
:::

:::vocab
Annihilate: To destroy completely.
Jealousy: In 1787, this often meant suspicion or watchful distrust of power, not envy.
:::

## "But the states already choose senators" {orig=6-7}

Someone may object to my position in this way. Under the Constitution, the state legislatures will choose the members of the Senate. So the danger I describe already exists in full. By refusing to appoint senators, the states could deal a deadly blow to the Union at any time. Since the Union already depends on them in such an important matter, the objection goes, there's no harm in trusting them with elections too. Besides, each state's interest in keeping its seats in the national government would fully protect against abuse.

This argument sounds convincing, but it doesn't hold up when examined. It is certainly true that the state legislatures could destroy the national government by refusing to appoint senators. But it doesn't follow that because they have this power in one case, they ought to have it in every other case. There are cases where such a power could be far more harmful, without any reason as strong as the one that guided the Convention in designing the Senate.

To the extent that the Senate's design exposes the Union to harm from the state legislatures, it is an evil. But it is an evil that couldn't have been avoided without shutting the states, as governments, completely out of the national government. If that had been done, people would have seen it as a total abandonment of the federal principle. And it would have taken away from the state governments the complete protection this arrangement gives them.

It may have been wise to accept an inconvenience in this case to gain a necessary advantage or a greater good. But that is no argument for piling up more of the same evil, where no necessity requires it and no greater good calls for it.

:::vocab
Federal (in 1787): Describing a union of sovereign states, where the central government deals with the states rather than directly with citizens. This is close to what we would now call a "confederation."
Specious: Seeming true or reasonable at first, but actually wrong.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton concedes a real point. Under the original Constitution, a state could leave its Senate seats empty just by refusing to choose senators. But giving the states this role was part of the bargain that made the Constitution possible. Choosing senators gave the state governments a direct voice in, and a way to protect themselves within, the national government.

Hamilton's reply is a general rule worth remembering: accepting one risk because you had to doesn't mean you should accept more risks you don't have to take.
:::

## Why the House is more at risk than the Senate {orig=8-9}

It's also easy to see that the national government would face much greater danger if the states controlled the elections for the House of Representatives than it does from their power to appoint senators.

Consider how the Senate works. Senators will serve six-year terms. Their terms are staggered, so that one-third of the seats become open and are filled every two years. No state can have more than two senators. And a quorum of the Senate will be sixteen members. Together, these facts mean that if a few states joined together for a while to stop appointing senators, they could neither end the Senate's existence nor stop it from working.

We have nothing to fear except from a general and permanent agreement among the states. The first kind of plot might come from the evil plans of the leading members of a few state legislatures. The second would require a fixed and deep-rooted dislike of the Union among most of the people. That will either never happen at all, or it will probably come from experience showing that the national government can't promote their happiness. In that case, no good citizen could want it to continue.

The House of Representatives is different. Its members are all to be elected at the same time, once every two years. If the state legislatures had the only power to regulate these elections, every election would be a dangerous moment for the nation. If the leaders of a few of the most important states had secretly agreed beforehand to prevent an election, the result might be the breakup of the Union.

:::commentary
Hamilton's point is about timing. Because only a third of the Senate is chosen at once, a few states refusing to pick senators couldn't shut it down. But the entire House is elected every two years. If a few large states refused to hold elections, the House might not be able to gather a quorum, and the national government could freeze.

The fear that states might refuse to take part in the national government was not far-fetched. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress often couldn't do business because too few states had sent delegates.
:::

## State leaders and the people may disagree {orig=10}

I won't deny that there is some weight to this point: each state's interest in being represented in the national government will help prevent the state legislatures from abusing a power over elections. But this protection won't seem complete to anyone who sees an obvious difference. The *people* care about the public happiness. Their *local rulers* care about the power and importance of their own offices.

The people of America may be strongly attached to the national government at times when the rulers of certain states feel very differently. Those rulers may be driven by the natural rivalry for power and by hopes of personal gain, and backed by a strong faction in each of their states. In some states right now, on the very question of the Constitution, we can see this kind of split between most of the people and the individuals who have the most influence in their governments.

The plan of splitting the country into separate confederacies will always multiply the chances for ambitious men. So it will always be a tempting bait for those influential men in the state governments who are capable of putting their own profit and advancement ahead of the public good. Suppose they held so powerful a weapon as the sole power to regulate national elections. Then a few such men, in a few of the largest states, where the temptation will always be strongest, might destroy the Union. They could seize on some passing discontent among the people, which they may have stirred up themselves, and stop choosing members of the House of Representatives.

We should never forget that a firmly united America, under an effective government, will probably become a growing object of jealousy to more than one nation of Europe. Plots to overthrow it will sometimes begin in the schemes of foreign powers, and some of them will rarely fail to support and encourage such plots. So the Union's survival should never, if it can be avoided, be left in the care of anyone except those whose position will always give them a direct interest in guarding it faithfully and carefully.

:::vocab
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
Confederacy: A loose union of independent states. Some opponents suggested splitting the thirteen states into two or three regional confederacies.
Emolument: Salary or profit from holding an office.
:::

:::commentary
When Hamilton mentions state leaders who disagree with their own people about the Constitution, his New York readers would likely have thought of their own state. Governor George Clinton and his allies led the opposition to the Constitution there. Hamilton is hinting that such leaders might care more about keeping their own power than about what the people want.

Hamilton's warning about separate confederacies and foreign plots also echoes the early papers in the series, such as [No. 5](/papers/5/) and [No. 6](/papers/6/), which argued that a divided America would be weak and open to meddling by European powers.
:::

:::today
The Elections Clause is still one of the most important parts of the Constitution in everyday politics. Congress has used it to set a single national Election Day, to require states to elect House members from districts, and to pass laws like the National Voter Registration Act of 1993, which made it easier to register to vote. It is also at the center of debates over redistricting and voting rules. In *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019), the Supreme Court ruled that federal courts can't decide claims of partisan gerrymandering, while noting that Congress could act under this clause. In *Moore v. Harper* (2023), the Court rejected the idea that state legislatures have nearly unchecked power over federal election rules, ruling that state courts can still review them. Some people want Congress to use its power to set national voting standards. Others argue that states should keep control, as they have for most of the nation's history. Both sides can point to Hamilton's balance of state control first and national power as a backup.
:::
