:::summary
Hamilton answers four objections to making the Senate the court for impeachment trials. First, critics say it mixes legislative and judicial power. Hamilton replies that some mixing is proper and even necessary, and that New York's own constitution mixes them far more. Second, critics say it gives the Senate too much power. Hamilton argues that each power should be judged on its own merits, and that the House has strong powers of its own, including the sole right to bring impeachments. Third, critics fear senators will go easy on officials they helped appoint. But the Senate only approves the president's choices, so it has little reason to protect them. Fourth, critics ask how senators could fairly judge a corrupt treaty they themselves approved. Hamilton answers that the protection against bad treaties lies in requiring the president and two-thirds of the Senate to agree, not in punishing senators afterward.
:::

:::keypoints
- Separation of powers allows a **partial mixing** of the branches for special purposes, as with the president's veto. Impeachment is the legislature's **check on the executive**.
- The House **accuses** and the Senate **judges**, so the same people are never both accuser and judge. Conviction needs a **two-thirds** vote.
- Critics who praise New York's constitution ignore that its Senate is also the state's **highest court**.
- Instead of vague worries about the Senate's total power, we should ask, for **each power separately**, where it is best placed.
- The **House** has powerful counterweights: it starts all money bills, it alone can impeach, and it picks the president when no candidate wins a majority of electors.
- Senators won't protect officials they helped appoint, because the Senate **doesn't choose** nominees. It only approves or rejects the president's choices.
- The security against bad treaties is the need for the **president and two-thirds of the Senate** to agree. Lawmakers should not be punished for votes they cast as a body.
- The Senate will gladly punish a president who betrayed its trust, or a few **corrupt leaders** who misled it.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 65](/papers/65/), Hamilton explained why the Constitution makes the Senate the court for impeachment trials. Now he answers the main criticisms of that choice. Anti-Federalists feared the Senate more than any other part of the new government. In the original Constitution, senators were chosen by state legislatures, not by the voters. They served six-year terms, and they shared in making treaties and appointments. Critics warned that the Senate could become an aristocracy, a small, privileged group working with the president against the people. Giving it the power to try impeachments seemed to make it even stronger.
:::

## First objection: mixing legislative and judicial power {orig=1-2}

A review of the main objections to the proposed court for impeachment trials will probably remove whatever bad impressions of it still remain.

The FIRST objection is that this provision combines legislative and judicial power in the same body. Critics say this violates the important and well-established rule that the different branches of power must be kept separate. The true meaning of this rule has been discussed and explained elsewhere. I showed that it is entirely consistent with a partial mixing of the branches for special purposes, as long as they remain separate and unconnected in the main. In some cases, this partial mixing is not only proper but necessary, so that the parts of the government can defend themselves against each other.

The ablest experts in political science agree that a veto for the executive over the acts of the legislature, whether absolute or qualified, is a necessary barrier against the legislature's attacks on the executive. With just as much reason, we may argue that the powers over impeachments are an essential check in the hands of the legislature against attacks by the executive, as I suggested before.

Dividing these powers between the two houses avoids the problem of making the same people both accusers and judges. One house has the right to accuse, and the other has the right to judge. This also guards against persecution if a spirit of faction takes hold of either house. And since two-thirds of the Senate must agree to convict, innocent people will have all the protection they could want.

:::commentary
The "elsewhere" is Madison's discussion of separation of powers in [No. 47](/papers/47/) and [No. 48](/papers/48/). Madison showed that even Montesquieu, the great defender of separating powers, did not mean the branches must have *nothing* to do with each other. He meant only that one branch should not hold the *whole* power of another. The Constitution mixes the branches on purpose: the president shares in lawmaking through the veto, the Senate shares in executive work through treaties and appointments, and Congress acts as a court in impeachments.

The two-thirds rule has proved to be a very high bar. Every president impeached by the House has been acquitted by the Senate.
:::

:::vocab
Maxim: A short statement of a general rule or truth.
Separation of powers: The idea that legislative, executive, and judicial power should be held by separate branches of government.
Encroachment: Gradually pushing into someone else's territory or taking over their powers.
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
:::

## New York's constitution mixes powers far more {orig=3}

It is curious to see how fiercely this part of the plan is attacked on this principle by men who claim to admire every part of our own state's constitution. That constitution makes the state Senate, together with the chancellor and the judges of the state Supreme Court, not only a court of impeachments but also the highest court in the state, in all civil and criminal cases. The chancellor and judges are so few compared with the senators that New York's final judicial power can truly be said to belong to its Senate. If the Convention's plan departs from the famous rule that is so often mentioned and so little understood, how much more guilty is the constitution of New York?

:::note A note on the original text
Hamilton adds in a footnote that in New Jersey, too, the final judicial authority belongs to one house of the legislature. In New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, and South Carolina, one house of the legislature is the court for impeachment trials.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is aiming at New York's Anti-Federalists, who praised their state constitution while attacking the federal one. Under New York's 1777 constitution, the highest court was called the "Court for the Trial of Impeachments and the Correction of Errors," and state senators made up most of it. Hamilton's point is that, by his opponents' own standards, their favorite constitution mixes powers far more than the Constitution does. The British House of Lords also served as Britain's highest court of appeal, a role it kept until 2009.
:::

:::vocab
Chancellor: In New York at the time, the judge who headed the Court of Chancery, a court that decided cases based on fairness rather than strict legal rules.
Judiciary: The branch of government made up of courts and judges.
:::

## Second objection: too much power for the Senate {orig=4-5}

A SECOND objection is that this power adds too much to the Senate's power, and tends to make the government look too aristocratic. The critics point out that the Senate will share with the president the powers to make treaties and to appoint officers. If we add the power to decide all impeachments, they say, the Senate's influence will clearly dominate.

It is hard to give a very precise answer to such an imprecise objection. What measure or standard can we use to decide whether the Senate has too much influence, too little, or just the right amount? Wouldn't it be safer and simpler to drop such vague and uncertain calculations? We could instead examine each power by itself and decide, on general principles, where it can be placed with the most advantage and the least harm.

If we take this approach, it will lead to a clearer result, if not a more certain one. Unless I am mistaken, the Convention's handling of the treaty power will then appear fully justified. The reasons were given in an earlier paper, and more will come up when we examine the executive. The wisdom of joining the Senate with the president in making appointments will, I trust, be shown just as clearly in those same essays. And I hope my last paper went a good way toward proving that it would be hard, if not impossible, to find a better place for the power to decide impeachments. If all this is true, we should drop this imagined fear that the Senate will have too much weight.

:::commentary
Hamilton's method here is worth noticing. Instead of arguing about whether the Senate's powers *added together* are too much, he insists on judging each power separately. That is a fair way to evaluate a design, but critics could reply that the danger lies precisely in the combination. A body that makes treaties, approves appointments, *and* judges impeachments might become powerful in ways that no single power would suggest.

The "earlier paper" on treaties is John Jay's [No. 64](/papers/64/). Hamilton returns to treaties in No. 75 and to appointments in [No. 76](/papers/76/) and [No. 77](/papers/77/).
:::

:::vocab
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged upper class.
Treaty: A formal written agreement between nations.
Prerogative: A special right or power that belongs to an office, especially to a king.
:::

## The House has strong powers of its own {orig=6-7}

But this fear, such as it is, has already been answered in the remarks on the senators' length of service. Those remarks showed, both from historical examples and from reason, that in every government with a republican spirit, the most POPULAR branch is usually the people's favorite. So it will usually be a full match, if not more than a match, for every other part of the government.

Apart from this very active and powerful principle, the Convention's plan also gives the House of Representatives several important counterweights to the extra powers given to the Senate. These help keep the House in balance:

- The House alone will have the right to start **money bills**.
- The House alone will have the right to **bring impeachments**. Isn't this a complete counterbalance to the Senate's right to decide them?
- The House will be the **umpire in presidential elections** whenever no candidate wins votes from a majority of all the electors. No doubt this will sometimes happen, if not often.

The constant possibility of this last power must be a rich source of influence for the House. The more we think about it, the more important this final, though occasional, power will seem. It lets the House decide contests between the most distinguished citizens of the Union for its highest office. It might not be rash to predict that, as a source of influence, it will outweigh all the special powers of the Senate.

:::commentary
The "remarks on the senators' length of service" are in No. 63, which argued that the popular branch would always be strong enough to hold the Senate in check.

Hamilton's list matches the Constitution. "All Bills for raising Revenue shall originate in the House of Representatives" (Article I, Section 7). And the House chooses the president if no candidate wins a majority of electoral votes. Hamilton expected this to happen "sometimes, if not frequently," but it has happened only twice. In 1801, the House chose Thomas Jefferson after he and Aaron Burr tied in electoral votes. Hamilton himself urged Federalists to prefer Jefferson over Burr. That tie led to the 12th Amendment (1804), which had electors vote separately for president and vice president. In 1825, the House chose John Quincy Adams over Andrew Jackson after no candidate won a majority. Since then, the rise of two major parties has usually produced a clear winner in the Electoral College, so this power of the House has not turned out to be the great source of influence Hamilton predicted.
:::

:::vocab
Electors: The people chosen in each state to cast that state's votes for president.
Suffrages: Votes.
:::

## Third objection: senators will protect officials they helped appoint {orig=8-9}

A THIRD objection is based on the Senate's role in appointments. Critics imagine that senators would be too gentle in judging officials they had helped put in office.

The principle behind this objection would condemn a practice found in all the state governments, and perhaps in every government we know. Some officials hold office "during pleasure," meaning they can be removed whenever those who appointed them choose. With just as much reason, one could claim that in this case, the appointers' favoritism would always shelter the officials' misconduct. But that practice is based on the opposite assumption. It assumes that the people who appoint officials are responsible for their fitness and ability. It also assumes that the appointers want public affairs to be run well and respectably. Together, these will make them willing enough to remove anyone who has proved, by his conduct, unworthy of the trust placed in him.

The facts may not always match this assumption. But if it is generally true, it destroys the idea that the Senate would favor the president's appointees. The Senate will merely approve the president's choice. Its bias toward those officials could hardly be strong enough to blind it to evidence of guilt so serious that the nation's representatives decided to accuse them.

If more arguments were needed to show how unlikely such a bias is, we would find them in the nature of the Senate's role in appointments. The president will NOMINATE, and, with the advice and consent of the Senate, APPOINT. So the Senate makes no CHOICE. It may defeat one of the president's choices and force him to make another. But it cannot CHOOSE for itself. It can only approve or reject the president's choice.

The Senate might even prefer someone else at the very moment it approves the nominee, simply because it has no definite reason to oppose him. And if it refused, it could not be sure that the next nominee would be its own favorite, or anyone it considered better. So a majority of the Senate would hardly feel any special fondness for an appointee. It would feel only what his apparent merit inspired, and that feeling would vanish once there was proof that he lacked merit.

:::commentary
Hamilton's description of the Senate's role here is the same one he gives in [No. 76](/papers/76/): the president chooses, and the Senate only accepts or rejects. In practice, senators have often had more influence than this, especially over appointments in their own states through the custom known as "senatorial courtesy." Still, Hamilton's basic point holds. Senators have voted to convict judges whose appointments the Senate had once approved. In fact, every official the Senate has ever convicted in an impeachment trial has been a federal judge.
:::

:::vocab
During pleasure: Holding an office only as long as the person who appointed you wishes. The opposite of a fixed term.
Advice and consent: The Senate's constitutional role in approving the president's nominations and treaties.
:::

## Fourth objection: senators would judge their own treaties {orig=10-11}

A FOURTH objection comes from the Senate's partnership with the president in making treaties. Critics say this would make senators their own judges whenever that trust was carried out in a corrupt or treacherous way. Suppose senators joined with the president to betray the nation's interests in a ruinous treaty. What chance would there be, the critics ask, of punishing them as they deserved, when they themselves would decide the charges against them?

This objection has been spread more earnestly, and with more appearance of reason, than any other against this part of the plan. And yet, unless I am mistaken, it rests on a false foundation.

:::commentary
This objection drew on a real fear in 1788. Many Americans in the South and West worried that the new government might make a treaty giving up America's right to use the Mississippi River. In 1786, John Jay, then in charge of foreign affairs for the Confederation Congress, had been willing to give up that right for a period of years in talks with Spain. Critics feared that a few states could push through such a treaty in the Senate.
:::

## The real protection against bad treaties {orig=12-13}

The Constitution's main protection against corruption and treachery in making treaties lies in the number and character of the people who make them. The JOINT ACTION of the president and two-thirds of the members of a body chosen by the combined wisdom of the state legislatures is meant to guarantee that national leaders stay faithful in this matter.

The Convention could properly have planned to punish the president for disobeying the Senate's instructions, or for dishonesty in carrying out the negotiations given to him. It might also have planned to punish a few leading senators who had sold their influence in that body as paid agents of foreign corruption. But it could not properly plan to impeach and punish two-thirds of the Senate for agreeing to a bad treaty. That would be no more proper than punishing a majority of either house of Congress for agreeing to a harmful or unconstitutional law. I believe no government has ever accepted such a principle.

How, in fact, could a majority of the House impeach itself? No better, clearly, than two-thirds of the Senate could try itself. And yet why should a majority of the House escape punishment for sacrificing society's interests by an unjust and tyrannical law, any more than two-thirds of the Senate for sacrificing the same interests in a harmful treaty with a foreign power?

The truth is that in all such cases, a body's freedom and necessary independence in its debates require that its members be free from punishment for what they do as a group. Society's protection must come from other things:

- taking care to give the trust to the right people;
- making it in their interest to carry it out faithfully;
- and making it as hard as possible for them to join together for any interest opposed to the public good.

As for misconduct by the president, such as twisting the Senate's instructions or working against its views, we need not fear that the Senate will lack the will to punish the abuse of its trust or to defend its own authority. On this point we can count on its pride, if not on its virtue. Now suppose a few leading members used their skill and influence to trick the majority into measures the community hated. If the proof of their corruption were convincing, ordinary human nature tells us what would happen. The Senate would usually be quite willing to turn public anger away from itself by quickly sacrificing the people who caused its mismanagement and disgrace.

:::quote
We may thus far count upon their pride, if not upon their virtue.

Meaning: Even if senators aren't especially good people, their pride in their own power will lead them to punish a president who deceives them.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's last answer relies on the same idea as Madison's "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" in [No. 51](/papers/51/). Senators will defend the Senate's authority because it is *theirs*.

His point that lawmakers should not be punished for votes they cast as a body is built into the Constitution. The Speech or Debate Clause (Article I, Section 6) says members of Congress "shall not be questioned in any other Place" for their speeches and debates. Members of Congress also turned out not to be subject to impeachment at all. In 1797, the House impeached Senator William Blount for plotting to help Britain seize Spanish territory. The Senate expelled him, and it later dismissed the impeachment on the grounds that it lacked jurisdiction. Since then, Congress has handled misconduct by its own members through expulsion, censure, and other discipline instead of impeachment.
:::

:::today
Many of the objections Hamilton answered here still come up. When the Senate holds an impeachment trial, people ask whether senators can act as fair judges when they are also politicians with party loyalties. Some senators have also faced the question of whether they can fairly judge officials they voted to confirm. Supporters of the system say the Senate is still the best body available, and the two-thirds requirement protects officials from being removed for purely partisan reasons. Critics say the high bar means that removal is almost impossible when the Senate is closely divided. Hamilton's general approach, judging each power by where it is best placed, is still a useful way to think about how the Constitution divides power.
:::
