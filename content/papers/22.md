:::summary
Hamilton finishes his list of the Articles of Confederation's defects. Congress can't **regulate trade**, so foreign nations take advantage of America and the states fight trade wars with each other. It raises armies by asking states for **quotas** of soldiers, which during the Revolution led to a costly bidding war for men. Every state gets an **equal vote**, so Delaware counts as much as Virginia, and rules requiring **supermajorities** let a small minority block the majority. That invites delay, bad compromises, and foreign bribery. There is **no national court** to give the laws and treaties one meaning. A single-house Congress shouldn't be trusted with more power. And worst of all, the Articles were never approved by **the people** themselves, who are the only true source of a government's authority.
:::

:::keypoints
- Without power over **commerce**, America can't win fair trade treaties, and the states pass **unneighborly** rules against each other.
- Raising armies by **quotas** caused an "auction for men" during the war, with huge bonuses, short enlistments, and unfair burdens on the states nearest the fighting.
- **Equal votes** for every state break the rule that the **majority should rule**. A majority of states could be a small minority of the people.
- Requiring **more than a majority** gives a minority a veto. It causes delay, endless deals, and **"contemptible compromises."**
- Supermajority rules make **foreign corruption** easier, because a foreign power only has to bribe a few people to block action.
- Laws are a **"dead letter"** without courts. The nation needs **one supreme court** so that laws and treaties mean the same thing everywhere.
- A **single-house Congress** shouldn't be given more powers. Piling power into one body would create a tyranny.
- The Articles were ratified only by **state legislatures**. The new government must rest on **"THE CONSENT OF THE PEOPLE."**
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of Hamilton's essays on the failures of the Articles (Nos. 15–22). Many of the problems he lists were well known by 1787. Congress had tried twice to get the power to collect a 5 percent tax on imports, in 1781 and 1783. Because the Articles could only be changed by a **unanimous** vote, one state was enough to kill each plan. Rhode Island rejected the first in 1782. New York, Hamilton's own state, refused to accept the second on Congress's terms in 1786.

Meanwhile, Britain kept American ships out of much of its trade, and the states taxed each other's goods. These trade troubles led to a meeting at Annapolis in 1786, where Hamilton helped write the call for a convention in Philadelphia the next year.
:::

## No power over trade {orig=1-4}

Besides the defects I have already listed, the current federal system has others that are just as important. Together, they make it completely unfit to manage the affairs of the Union.

Everyone agrees that one of these is the lack of a power to regulate commerce. I discussed the value of such a power in the first part of these essays. For that reason, and because everyone is already convinced on this subject, I don't need to add much here. Even at a quick glance, it's obvious that nothing needs national oversight more than trade and finance.

The lack of this power has already blocked us from making useful treaties with foreign powers. It has also caused bad feelings among the states. No nation that understands how our union works would be foolish enough to make a treaty giving the United States any important privileges. It would know that the states could break the Union's promises at any moment. And it has found from experience that it can enjoy every advantage it wants in our markets without giving us anything in return, except what happens to suit it at the moment.

So it's no surprise what Mr. Jenkinson said when he introduced a bill in the British House of Commons to regulate trade between our two countries for the time being. He began by saying that similar laws in the past had served every purpose of British commerce. He said it would be wise to keep the same plan until it became clear whether the American government was likely to become more stable and united.

Several states have tried, by their own separate bans, limits, and exclusions, to pressure Britain to change its policy. But they have failed to act together. That's because there is no national authority, and because the states have clashing and different views. This has defeated every attempt of this kind so far. It will keep doing so as long as the same obstacles to uniform policy remain.

Some states have also passed meddling and unneighborly trade rules that go against the true spirit of the Union. In several cases, these have given other states good reason to feel offended and to complain. Unless national authority restrains them, we must fear that such examples will multiply and spread. In the end, they would become serious sources of hostility and conflict, as well as harmful barriers to trade among the different parts of the Confederacy. A description of the German Empire shows where this can lead:

> "The commerce of the German empire is in continual trammels from the multiplicity of the duties which the several princes and states exact upon the merchandises passing through their territories, by means of which the fine streams and navigable rivers with which Germany is so happily watered are rendered almost useless."

The character of the American people might never allow this description to fit us exactly. Still, as state rules gradually clash more and more, we can reasonably expect that the citizens of each state would finally be seen and treated by the others as no better than foreigners.

:::vocab
Commerce: Trade, especially between nations or states.
House of Commons: The elected, lower house of the British Parliament.
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
Navigable: Deep and wide enough for boats and ships to travel on.
Alien: A person from another country who is not a citizen.
:::

:::commentary
**Charles Jenkinson** was a leading British official on trade. After the Revolution, Britain banned American ships from much of its trade, including the valuable trade with the British West Indies, while still selling plenty of British goods in America. Britain had little reason to offer a better deal, because Congress couldn't strike back. Each state set its own trade rules, so if one state taxed British ships, they simply went to a neighboring state.

The states also fought with each other. New York, for example, charged fees on goods arriving from New Jersey and Connecticut. Madison later wrote that New Jersey, stuck between the ports of New York and Philadelphia, was like "a cask tapped at both ends," drained by both of its neighbors.

The Constitution fixed this with the **Commerce Clause**, which gives Congress power to regulate trade "with foreign Nations, and among the several States." In *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824), the Supreme Court used it to strike down a steamboat monopoly granted by New York. Today the clause is one of the main sources of Congress's power, and how far it reaches is one of the most debated questions in constitutional law.
:::

## An auction for soldiers {orig=5-6}

Under the plainest reading of the Articles, the power to raise armies is just a power to ask the states for quotas of men. During the last war, this practice was full of obstacles to a strong and economical defense.

It set off a competition among the states that created a kind of auction for men. To fill the quotas assigned to them, the states outbid each other, until the bounties, or signing bonuses, grew enormous and impossible to afford. Men who were willing to serve hoped the bounties would rise even higher. So they put off enlisting, and they didn't want to sign up for long periods.

The results were these:

- Slow and small recruiting in the most critical emergencies.
- Short enlistments at a cost never seen before.
- Constant turnover in the troops, which ruined their discipline and often put the public safety in danger, when whole armies went home at once.
- Harsh methods of raising men, used on several occasions, which nothing but the people's passion for liberty would have led them to put up with.

This way of raising troops is as bad for fairness as it is for cost and strength. The states near the fighting, driven by the need to protect themselves, worked to meet their quotas, sometimes beyond their ability. The states far from danger were mostly as careless as the others were hard-working.

In this case, unlike the case of money, there was no hope of a final settling of accounts to ease the unfairness. States that didn't pay their share of money could at least be charged with what they owed. But no record could be kept of the soldiers a state failed to supply. Still, we shouldn't regret the lack of that hope very much. There is little chance that the worst offending states will ever be able to make up for their failures to pay money anyway.

The system of quotas and requisitions, whether for men or money, is in every way a system of weakness in the Union, and of inequality and injustice among its members.

:::vocab
Quota: A fixed share that each state was supposed to contribute.
Requisition: A formal request. Under the Articles, Congress asked the states for money and soldiers but couldn't make them provide either.
Bounty: A payment offered to encourage something, such as a bonus for joining the army.
Enlistment: Signing up to serve in the military, usually for a set period of time.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton knew this problem firsthand. He served on George Washington's staff for four years during the Revolution. Washington complained constantly that short enlistments left him with an army that kept melting away. Soldiers signed up for a few months, and many went home just as they were becoming experienced. States competed for recruits with bonuses of money and land.

The Constitution solved this by giving Congress the power "to raise and support Armies" directly, without going through the states. Hamilton defends that power, and answers fears of a standing army, in Nos. [23](/papers/23/)–29.
:::

## Equal votes for unequal states {orig=7-8}

The equal vote of every state is another objectionable part of the Confederation. Every idea of proportion and every rule of fair representation condemn a principle that gives Rhode Island as much weight as Massachusetts, Connecticut, or New York. It gives Delaware an equal voice in national decisions with Pennsylvania, Virginia, or North Carolina. In practice, it goes against the basic rule of republican government: the will of the majority should win.

Clever arguers may reply that sovereign states are equal, and that a majority of the states' votes is a majority of America as a union. But this kind of logical trick will never overcome the plain sense of justice and common sense. A majority of the states could be a small minority of the people of America. (New Hampshire, Rhode Island, New Jersey, Delaware, Georgia, South Carolina, and Maryland make up a majority of the states, but they don't contain one-third of the people.) Two-thirds of the American people could not be persuaded for long, by artificial distinctions and clever reasoning, to hand control of their interests to one-third.

After a while, the larger states would rebel against the idea of taking orders from the smaller ones. To accept this loss of their rightful weight would mean not only ignoring the love of power, but even giving up the desire for equality. It isn't reasonable to expect the first, or fair to demand the second. The smaller states depend especially on the Union for their safety and well-being. So they ought to be willing to give up a claim that would destroy the Union if they held on to it.

Someone may object that the most important decisions need the agreement of not seven but nine states, two-thirds of the total. From this, they might conclude that nine states would always include a majority of the Union. But that doesn't fix the wrongness of an equal vote among states of hugely different size and population. Nor is the conclusion true in fact. We can name nine states that contain less than a majority of the people. (Add New York and Connecticut to the seven states listed above, and together they still have less than a majority.) And under the Articles, it is possible for those nine to make the decision.

Besides, some fairly important matters can be decided by a bare majority of seven states. There are other matters where it is doubtful how many votes are needed. If those doubts were settled in favor of seven votes being enough, a simple majority of states could decide questions of the greatest importance. Finally, the number of states will probably grow, and there is no rule for adjusting the votes to match.

:::vocab
Suffrage: The right to vote, or a vote itself.
Sophistry: Clever-sounding arguments that are actually misleading or false.
Legerdemain: Sleight of hand; trickery.
Maxim: A basic rule or principle.
:::

:::commentary
This was the most explosive issue at the Constitutional Convention. Large states like Virginia and Pennsylvania wanted representation based on population. Small states like Delaware and New Jersey insisted on keeping the equal vote. The fight nearly broke up the Convention in the summer of 1787. It was settled by the **Great Compromise**: representation by population in the House, and two senators per state in the Senate.

So the Constitution kept part of the principle Hamilton attacks here. Today, Wyoming and California each have two senators, even though California has far more people. Supporters say the Senate protects smaller states and reminds us that the United States is a union of states. Critics say it gives a voter in a small state much more influence than a voter in a large one, just as Hamilton complained. Madison defends the compromise as a necessary deal in [No. 62](/papers/62/).
:::

## Why a minority veto is poison {orig=9-10}

But that's not all. What may seem at first like a cure is actually a poison. Giving a minority a veto over the majority, which always happens when more than a majority is needed to decide, tends to put the will of the greater number under the will of the smaller.

Because a few states have failed to send delegates, Congress has often been in the position of a Polish diet, where a single VOTE has been enough to stop everything. A sixtieth of the Union, about the share of Delaware and Rhode Island, has several times been able to block it completely. This is one of those clever ideas that works in practice the opposite of how it is supposed to work in theory.

People have demanded that public bodies decide unanimously, or nearly so, on the assumption that this would make us safer. But what it really does is tangle up the government's work and destroy its energy. It replaces the regular debates and decisions of a respectable majority with the whims or schemes of an unimportant, unruly, or corrupt little group.

In a nation's emergencies, when the strength or weakness of its government matters most, action is usually necessary. Public business must go forward somehow. If a stubborn minority can overrule the majority's opinion about the best way to do it, then the majority must give in to the minority's views just to get something done. In that way, the will of the smaller number will overrule that of the greater and set the direction of national policy.

The results are tiresome delays, constant bargaining and scheming, and contemptible compromises of the public good. And yet in such a system, we're lucky when compromises can be made at all. Sometimes the matter can't be settled by a deal. Then the government's measures must be put off, which does harm, or defeated, which can be fatal. The government is often kept from acting because it can't get the needed number of votes. Its condition must always seem weak, and it sometimes borders on anarchy.

It's easy to see that a rule like this gives more room to foreign corruption, and to factions at home, than a rule that lets the majority decide. People have assumed the opposite. That mistake comes from not paying enough attention to the harm done by blocking the government at critical times. When the Constitution requires a large number to agree before the nation can act, we tend to feel safe, because nothing improper is likely TO BE DONE. But we forget how much good may be prevented, and how much harm may be caused, by the power to stop necessary action. That power can keep things stuck in whatever bad position they happen to be in at a given moment.

:::quote
To give a minority a negative upon the majority (which is always the case where more than a majority is requisite to a decision), is, in its tendency, to subject the sense of the greater number to that of the lesser.

Meaning: Any rule that requires more than a simple majority gives a smaller group the power to block the larger one, so in practice the few can control the many.
:::

:::vocab
Diet: An old word for a national assembly, used for the legislatures of Germany and Poland.
Junto: A small group of people who work together, often secretly, for their own political goals.
Energy (in government): Strength and the ability to act effectively.
Anarchy: A state of disorder with no effective government.
:::

:::commentary
The "Polish diet" refers to the **liberum veto** in Poland's parliament, the Sejm. Under this rule, a single member could block a decision, and even end the whole session, just by objecting. Foreign powers bribed members to use it, and it helped leave Poland weak. By the time Hamilton wrote, Poland's neighbors had already begun dividing up its territory, starting in 1772.

Hamilton's argument here is one of the strongest cases ever made against supermajority rules. But the Constitution he defends still has several. It requires two-thirds of the Senate to approve treaties, two-thirds of both houses to override a veto, and three-fourths of the states to approve amendments. The Senate's **filibuster**, which in practice makes most bills need 60 votes out of 100, isn't in the Constitution at all. Supporters of supermajority rules say they protect minorities and force broad agreement. Critics use Hamilton's words: they let "a pertinacious minority" block the will of the majority.
:::

## The danger of foreign bribery {orig=11-13}

Suppose, for example, we were fighting a war alongside one foreign nation against another. Suppose our situation demanded peace, but our ally's interest or ambition led it to want the war to go on, for reasons that might justify us in making a separate peace. In that case, our ally would clearly find it much easier to use bribes and schemes to stop our government from making peace if two-thirds of all the votes were needed, than if a simple majority were enough. In the first case, it would only need to corrupt a smaller number. In the second, it would need to corrupt a larger number.

By the same reasoning, it would be much easier for a foreign power we were at war with to confuse our councils and weaken our efforts. And we could face the same kind of problem in trade. Suppose we had a trade treaty with one nation. That nation could much more easily prevent us from making an agreement with its trade rival, however much that agreement might help us.

Dangers like these shouldn't be thought imaginary. Republics have many advantages, but one of their weak points is that they give foreign corruption too easy a way in. A hereditary monarch is often willing to sacrifice his people to his ambition. But he has such a large personal stake in the government and in the nation's glory abroad that a foreign power can't easily pay him enough to make up for what he would lose by betraying his country. So the world has seen few examples of a king selling out his country this way, though it has seen plenty of every other kind of royal misconduct.

In republics, the voters raise ordinary citizens to positions of great importance and power. These people may be offered rewards for betraying their trust. To anyone without exceptional virtue, those rewards may seem bigger than their share in the common good, and bigger than their sense of duty. That is why history gives us so many shameful examples of foreign corruption in republics.

I have already described how much this helped ruin the ancient republics. It is well known that the deputies of the United Provinces of the Netherlands have, many times, been bought by agents of neighboring kingdoms. The Earl of Chesterfield, if I remember correctly, wrote in a letter to his government that his success in an important negotiation depended on getting an army officer's position, a major's commission, for one of those deputies.

And in Sweden, the political parties were bought, first by France and then by England, so openly and shamelessly that the whole nation was disgusted. This was a main reason why the most limited king in Europe, in a single day, without riots, violence, or opposition, became one of the most absolute and unchecked.

:::vocab
Hereditary: Passed down from parent to child, as a crown is passed from a king to his heir.
Commission: An official document that appoints someone to a rank, especially in the military.
United Provinces: The Dutch Republic, a union of seven provinces in the Netherlands.
:::

:::commentary
The **Earl of Chesterfield** was a British diplomat who served as ambassador to the Dutch Republic in the 1720s and 1730s. He is best known today for his letters of advice to his son. Hamilton himself admits he is quoting from memory.

The Swedish example refers to 1772. For decades, Sweden's parliament had held most of the power, and its king had little. Two rival parties, known as the "Hats" and the "Caps," took money from foreign governments. The Hats were backed mainly by France. The Caps were backed mainly by Russia and Britain. In 1772, King Gustav III seized power in a quick, bloodless takeover, and many Swedes welcomed it.

Hamilton's fear of foreign influence shaped the Constitution. It bars federal officials from accepting gifts or titles from foreign governments without Congress's consent. In his Farewell Address in 1796, George Washington also warned Americans against "the insidious wiles of foreign influence." Hamilton helped write that address.
:::

## No national courts {orig=14-16}

One defect tops off all the others: the lack of a national judiciary. Laws are a dead letter without courts to explain and define their true meaning and effect.

The treaties of the United States, to have any force at all, must be treated as part of the law of the land. Their true meaning, as it applies to individuals, must be decided by courts, like all other laws. To make sure these decisions are the same everywhere, they should be sent in the end to one SUPREME COURT. And that court should be created by the same authority that makes the treaties. Both of these things are essential.

If each state has its own court of final appeal, there may be as many different final rulings on the same question as there are courts. People's opinions differ endlessly. We often see not only different courts but even judges on the same court disagreeing with each other. Contradictory rulings by many independent courts would cause unavoidable confusion. To avoid it, every nation has found it necessary to set up one court above the rest. That court oversees the others and has the authority to settle and declare, once and for all, a single rule of justice.

This is even more necessary where the government is built so that the laws of the parts may conflict with the laws of the whole. In that situation, suppose the local courts have the final word. Besides the contradictions we can expect from differences of opinion, there is much to fear from local views and prejudices and from the interference of local laws. Whenever such a conflict came up, there would be reason to fear that the state laws would be preferred over the national laws. For nothing is more natural for officials than to show special respect for the authority that gave them their jobs.

Under the current system, the treaties of the United States can be broken by thirteen different legislatures, and by as many different courts of final appeal acting under those legislatures. So the good faith, reputation, and peace of the whole Union are always at the mercy of the prejudices, passions, and interests of every one of its members. Can foreign nations possibly respect or trust such a government? Can the people of America possibly agree any longer to trust their honor, their happiness, and their safety to such a shaky foundation?

:::quote
Laws are a dead letter without courts to expound and define their true meaning and operation.

Meaning: A law means nothing in practice unless there are courts to interpret it and apply it to real cases.
:::

:::vocab
Judiciary: The branch of government made up of courts and judges.
Tribunal: A court.
Jurisdiction: The authority to make legal decisions, or the area where that authority applies.
Expound: To explain in detail.
:::

:::commentary
The Articles created no national court system, except for special courts to hear cases about ships captured at sea and to settle disputes between states. This mattered most for the 1783 peace treaty with Britain. The treaty promised that British creditors could collect debts owed from before the war, and that Loyalists would not be further punished. But several states passed laws that went against it. In New York, Hamilton himself argued the case of *Rutgers v. Waddington* (1784). He defended a British merchant who was being sued under a state law, arguing that the peace treaty should come first.

The Constitution answered Hamilton's demand. Article III creates "one supreme Court," and Article VI makes treaties part of "the supreme Law of the Land." The Judiciary Act of 1789 let the Supreme Court review state court decisions on federal questions. In *Ware v. Hylton* (1796), the Court ruled that the peace treaty overrode a Virginia law about British debts. Hamilton explains the national courts in detail in Nos. [78](/papers/78/)–83.
:::

## A system beyond repair {orig=17-18}

In this review of the Confederation, I have kept to its most serious defects. I have skipped the flaws in its details, which have largely canceled out even much of the power it was meant to have. By now, it must be clear to every thoughtful person who can set aside old prejudices that the system is wrong and unsound at its roots. It can't be fixed except by completely changing its main features and character.

The way Congress itself is organized makes it completely unfit to use the powers that must be placed in the Union. A single assembly may be a fit place for the thin, or rather chained-up, powers given to the national government so far. But it would go against every principle of good government to trust it with the added powers that even the more moderate and reasonable opponents of the Constitution admit the United States should have.

Suppose the Constitution is not adopted. Suppose also that the need for union manages to survive the ambitious plans of men who dream of gaining personal power from its breakup. Then we would probably try giving additional powers to Congress as it is now set up. One of two things would happen. The machine might fall to pieces because of its built-in weakness, despite our foolish efforts to prop it up. Or, as necessity pushed us, we would keep adding to its strength, step by step. In the end, we would pile all the most important powers of government into a single body. We would leave our descendants one of the most hateful forms of government that human foolishness ever invented. That way, we would actually create the very tyranny that the Constitution's opponents are anxious, or pretend to be anxious, to prevent.

:::vocab
Prerogative: A special right or power that belongs to an office, especially to a king.
Posterity: Future generations.
Aggrandizement: An increase in one's own power, wealth, or importance.
:::

:::commentary
Under the Articles, Congress was a **single house**, with no separate executive or courts. Hamilton warns that giving more power to such a body would be dangerous, because nothing inside the government would check it. This links to the idea of **separation of powers**, which Madison explains in [No. 47](/papers/47/) and [No. 51](/papers/51/). The Constitution split Congress into two houses and added an independent president and courts.

Notice Hamilton's jab at the Constitution's opponents: they "either are, or affect to be," worried about tyranny. "Affect" here means pretend. He suggests some of them aren't sincere, which is a debating move, not a proof.
:::

## Built on the consent of the people {orig=19}

The weaknesses of the current federal system have been made much worse by the fact that it was never ratified by the PEOPLE. It rests on nothing stronger than the consent of the separate state legislatures. Because of that, it has faced frequent and complicated questions about whether its powers are valid.

In some cases, it has even given rise to the outrageous idea that a state legislature has a right to repeal it. The Articles were ratified by a state law. So some have argued that the same authority could repeal the law that ratified them. It is a serious error to claim that one PARTY to a COMPACT has the right to cancel that COMPACT. Yet respected people have supported the idea.

The fact that such a question can even come up proves that we must lay the foundations of our national government deeper than the mere approval of delegated authority. The structure of the American nation ought to rest on the solid basis of THE CONSENT OF THE PEOPLE. The streams of national power ought to flow directly from that pure, original fountain of all lawful authority.

:::quote
The fabric of American empire ought to rest on the solid basis of THE CONSENT OF THE PEOPLE. The streams of national power ought to flow immediately from that pure, original fountain of all legitimate authority.

Meaning: The national government must get its authority directly from the people, not just from the state governments. In 1787, "empire" often meant a large nation or realm, not a country ruling over colonies.
:::

:::vocab
Ratify: To formally approve.
Compact: An agreement or contract between two or more parties.
Heresy: A belief that goes against accepted teaching. Here, an idea Hamilton thinks is badly wrong.
Legitimate: Lawful and rightful.
:::

:::commentary
This closing paragraph explains why the Constitution was sent to **ratifying conventions** elected by the people of each state, not to the state legislatures. Article VII said it would take effect once nine state conventions approved it. The Constitution's first words, "We the People of the United States," make the same point. Its authority comes from the people, not from the states as governments. The Articles, by contrast, were approved by the state legislatures, a process that finished only in 1781, when Maryland finally agreed.

The idea Hamilton calls a "heresy," that the states made a compact and can undo it, did not go away. In 1798, the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, written secretly by Madison and Jefferson, described the Constitution as a compact among the states. In 1860 and 1861, Southern states used the compact theory to claim a right to secede. The Civil War settled that question by force, and in *Texas v. White* (1869) the Supreme Court described the Constitution as creating "an indestructible Union," which no state can leave on its own.
:::

:::today
Many of the issues in this essay are still argued about. The Senate gives every state an equal vote, and the filibuster lets a minority of senators block most bills. Americans disagree about whether those rules protect minorities and force compromise, or let a few block the will of the many, as Hamilton warned. The Commerce Clause, created to fix the trade problems he describes, is now one of the biggest sources of federal power and one of the most disputed. And Hamilton's warning about foreign influence in a republic remains a live concern in debates about elections and lobbying.
:::
