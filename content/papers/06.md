:::summary
Hamilton turns from foreign dangers to a danger he thinks is even worse: wars among the states themselves if the Union breaks apart. He argues that people are "ambitious, vindictive, and rapacious," and that nations act on the same passions, including the private grudges of their leaders. He gives examples from Athens, England, and France to show how personal motives have started great wars. Then he takes on the popular idea that republics and trading nations are naturally peaceful. History, he says, proves the opposite: Athens, Carthage, Venice, Holland, and Britain all traded and all fought. He ends with a famous rule of politics: neighboring nations are natural enemies, unless they join in a confederate republic.
:::

:::keypoints
- If the states split apart, or formed a few separate confederacies, they would **fight each other often and violently**.
- Nations go to war for many reasons: the **love of power**, **fear of others' power**, **trade rivalries**, and the **private passions of leaders**.
- Leaders like **Pericles** of Athens and **Cardinal Wolsey** of England dragged their countries into war for personal reasons.
- Some people claim that **republics** and **commerce** make nations peaceful. Hamilton says commerce has only **changed what nations fight over**.
- **Republics are run by men**, and popular assemblies feel rage, jealousy, and greed just as kings do.
- Trading republics like **Athens, Carthage, Venice, and Holland** fought constantly, and **Britain**, a trading nation with an elected legislature, was often pushed into war by its own people.
- Americans should wake up from the "dream of a golden age." **Neighboring nations are natural enemies** unless they unite in a confederate republic.
:::

:::context Background
In Nos. 2–5, John Jay argued that a united America would be safer from foreign nations. With No. 6, Hamilton begins a new topic: what would happen *between* the states if the Union fell apart. Nos. 6–8 form a group. No. 6 explains why separate states would fight, [No. 7](/papers/7/) lists what they would fight about, and [No. 8](/papers/8/) explains what constant war would do to liberty.

Hamilton was answering a hopeful view that was common in his time. Many writers of the 1700s believed that trade linked nations together and made war less likely, and that republics, ruled by the people, would be less warlike than kings. Some opponents of the Constitution suggested that if the Union failed, the states or a few regional confederacies could simply live side by side in peace.
:::

## A danger from within {orig=1-2}

The last three papers described the dangers we would face from the armies and schemes of foreign nations if the states were not united. I will now describe dangers of a different kind, and perhaps even more alarming. These are the dangers that would most likely come from quarrels among the states themselves, and from factions and violent upheavals at home. I have already touched on some of these in passing, but they deserve a closer and fuller look.

A person would have to be lost in fantasy to doubt this: if these states were completely separated, or joined only in partial confederacies, the pieces would have frequent and violent conflicts with each other. Someone might argue that they would have no reason to fight, but that would be to forget that men are ambitious, vindictive, and rapacious. To expect lasting harmony among several independent, unconnected nations in the same neighborhood would be to ignore the steady course of human events and to defy the experience of all the ages.

:::quote
To presume a want of motives for such contests as an argument against their existence, would be to forget that men are ambitious, vindictive, and rapacious.

Meaning: Don't assume the states would have no reason to fight. People are hungry for power, eager for revenge, and greedy, and they will always find reasons.
:::

:::vocab
Confederacy: A loose union of independent states. Some opponents suggested splitting the thirteen states into two or three regional confederacies.
Utopian: Based on an imaginary perfect world; unrealistic.
Vindictive: Eager for revenge.
Rapacious: Greedy; eager to grab what belongs to others.
Sovereignty: Supreme, independent political authority.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the bluntest statements of human nature in the whole series. Like Madison in [No. 10](/papers/10/) and [No. 51](/papers/51/), Hamilton builds his argument on the idea that people are not angels. He thinks good intentions won't keep independent states at peace. Only a common government can do that.
:::

## Why nations go to war {orig=3}

The causes of hostility among nations are countless. Some of them act on whole societies in a general and almost constant way. These include the love of power, or the desire to be first and to rule others. They also include the fear of other nations' power, or the desire for equality and safety.

Other causes have a narrower reach, but they are just as powerful within it. One example is the rivalry and competition between trading nations.

And there are still others, just as many as the first two kinds, that come entirely from private passions. These are the friendships, hatreds, interests, hopes, and fears of the leading men in each community. Men like this, whether they are the favorites of a king or of the people, have too often abused the trust placed in them. Pretending to act for some public reason, they have not hesitated to sacrifice their nation's peace for their own gain or satisfaction.

## Wars started by private passions {orig=4-7}

The famous Pericles, leader of ancient Athens, gave in to the anger of a prostitute. At great cost to the lives and wealth of his own people, he attacked, defeated, and destroyed the city of Samos.

The same man was also the original cause of the famous and deadly Peloponnesian War, which, after many ups and downs, pauses, and new outbreaks, ended in the ruin of Athens. He may have had a private grudge against the people of Megara, another Greek city. He may have wanted to escape being prosecuted as a partner in a theft by the sculptor Phidias. Or he may have wanted to escape charges that he had wasted public money to buy popularity. Perhaps it was a combination of all of these causes.

Cardinal Wolsey, the ambitious chief minister of King Henry VIII of England, vainly hoped to wear the triple crown and become pope. He expected to win that splendid prize with the help of the Emperor Charles V. To gain the favor of this bold and powerful monarch, Wolsey rushed England into a war with France. This went against the plainest rules of good policy. It put at risk the safety and independence of England, and of Europe in general. If any ruler ever came close to making himself master of all Europe, it was Charles V, and Wolsey was both his tool and his dupe.

The influence of three women on the politics, conflicts, and peace treaties of much of Europe has been discussed too often to need retelling. One acted out of religious bigotry, another out of bad temper, and a third out of secret scheming.

It would be a waste of time to pile up more examples of how personal motives have produced great national events, at home or abroad. Anyone with even a basic knowledge of history will remember many cases. And anyone with a fair understanding of human nature doesn't need such examples to see how real and how widespread these motives are.

Still, it may be useful to point to one case that happened recently among ourselves. It shows the general principle well. If Shays had not been a DESPERATE DEBTOR, it is very doubtful that Massachusetts would have been plunged into a civil war.

:::vocab
Pericles: The leading statesman of ancient Athens in the 400s B.C., during its greatest period of power and culture.
Peloponnesian War: The long war (431–404 B.C.) between Athens and Sparta and their allies. It ended in Athens's defeat.
Triple crown: The tall crown worn by the popes. Hoping for the "triple crown" meant hoping to become pope.
Charles V: Holy Roman Emperor and King of Spain in the early 1500s, who ruled more of Europe than anyone of his time.
Dupe: A person who is fooled and used by someone else.
Bigotry: Narrow-minded intolerance, especially toward other religions.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's examples come from history books his readers knew well:

- **Pericles:** Hamilton's footnotes name his source as Plutarch, an ancient Greek writer of biographies. The woman was **Aspasia**, Pericles's longtime partner, an educated woman from the city of Miletus. Athens fought Samos after Samos went to war with Miletus. Plutarch reports that Pericles's enemies blamed Aspasia, but these were accusations from his opponents, and modern historians doubt that she was a prostitute. Plutarch also reports the charges about the sculptor Phidias, who supposedly stole gold meant for a great statue of the goddess Athena (Minerva). Athens's trade ban against Megara is often listed as one cause of the Peloponnesian War, though historians stress deeper causes, especially Sparta's fear of Athens's growing power.
- **The three women:** Hamilton's footnotes name them. **Madame de Maintenon** was the deeply religious second wife of King Louis XIV of France. The **Duchess of Marlborough** was a close friend of Queen Anne of England, and their famous quarrels affected English politics. **Madame de Pompadour** was the powerful companion of King Louis XV of France.
- **Shays:** Daniel Shays was a Revolutionary War veteran in western Massachusetts. In 1786–87, farmers buried in debt and taxes closed courts to stop them from taking farms and jailing debtors. The uprising was named for Shays, but historians see it as a broad movement with many leaders and deep economic causes, not one man's personal troubles.
:::

:::commentary
Notice how Hamilton describes the women in his examples. Aspasia is reduced to a "prostitute," and the three European women are summed up by bigotry, bad temper, and scheming. That reflects the attitudes of his time, when women were shut out of official power and any influence they had was often described as improper. His own examples of Pericles and Wolsey show that men in power acted from the same kinds of personal motives.
:::

## "Trade will keep us peaceful" {orig=8-9}

But in spite of all this evidence, some dreamers and schemers still argue for an idea that contradicts it: that the states would stay at peace forever, even if they were cut off from each other and became strangers. "Republics are peaceful by nature," they say. "The spirit of commerce softens people's manners. It puts out the fiery passions that have so often burst into war. Trading republics like ours will never want to waste themselves in ruinous fights with each other. They will be guided by their shared interest, and they will build friendship and goodwill."

We may ask these political dreamers some questions:

- Isn't it the true interest of *all* nations to develop the same kind, wise spirit? If so, have they actually done it?
- Hasn't it always turned out that passions of the moment and immediate interests control human behavior more strongly than broad or distant concerns of policy, usefulness, or justice?
- Have republics in practice been less fond of war than monarchies? Aren't republics run by MEN just as monarchies are?
- Don't dislikes, favorites, rivalries, and the desire to grab what isn't theirs affect nations as well as kings?
- Aren't popular assemblies often driven by rage, resentment, jealousy, greed, and other wild and violent urges?
- Isn't it well known that their decisions are often controlled by a few individuals they trust, and so are colored by those individuals' passions and goals?
- Has commerce done anything so far except change what wars are fought over?
- Isn't the love of wealth just as commanding and aggressive a passion as the love of power or glory?
- Since trade became the main business of nations, haven't there been as many wars over trade as there once were over land and power?
- Hasn't the spirit of commerce, in many cases, actually given nations new reasons to want both wealth and power?

Let experience, the most reliable guide to human opinion, answer these questions.

:::quote
Has commerce hitherto done anything more than change the objects of war?

Meaning: Trade hasn't ended war. It has only given nations new things to fight about.
:::

:::vocab
Commerce: Trade, especially between nations or states.
Commercial republic: A republic whose people make their living largely through trade and shipping.
Popular assembly: A lawmaking body elected by, or made up of, the people.
:::

:::commentary
The idea Hamilton is attacking had famous supporters. Montesquieu, the French writer quoted so often in these debates, wrote that the natural effect of commerce is to lead to peace. Thomas Paine argued in *Common Sense* (1776) that trade would win America the friendship of Europe. The reasoning is that countries that depend on each other's goods have too much to lose by fighting.

Hamilton doesn't deny that trade creates shared interests. His point is that short-term passions usually beat long-term interests, and that wealth is something nations fight over too. This debate never ended. In the 1900s and 2000s, some scholars argued that trade and democracy make wars less likely, while critics pointed out that World War I broke out between countries that traded heavily with each other.
:::

## Trading republics went to war too {orig=10-13}

Sparta, Athens, Rome, and Carthage were all republics. Two of them, Athens and Carthage, were trading republics. Yet they fought wars, both to attack and to defend, just as often as the monarchies around them at the same time. Sparta was little more than a well-run army camp, and Rome never had enough of killing and conquest.

Carthage, a trading republic, was the attacker in the very war that ended in its destruction. Its general Hannibal had carried its armies into the heart of Italy and up to the gates of Rome. Then the Roman general Scipio, in turn, defeated him in Carthage's own territory and conquered the republic.

In later times, the republic of Venice took part more than once in wars of ambition. Finally, the other Italian states came to see it as a threat. Pope Julius II managed to form a powerful league against it, which dealt a deadly blow to the power and pride of this arrogant republic.

The provinces of Holland took a leading part in the wars of Europe until they were buried under debts and taxes. They fought fierce wars with England for control of the seas, and they were among the most stubborn and unforgiving enemies of King Louis XIV of France.

:::vocab
Carthage: A wealthy trading city in North Africa, in what is now Tunisia. It fought three wars against Rome.
Venice: A rich trading city-state in northern Italy that was a republic for more than a thousand years.
Holland: The leading province of the Dutch Republic (the United Provinces of the Netherlands), a great trading and shipping power in the 1600s.
League of Cambrai: An alliance formed against Venice in 1508 by Pope Julius II, the Holy Roman Emperor, the King of France, the King of Aragon, and many Italian states.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton chose these examples because each was famous for trade, not just for war. Athens had a great navy and a busy port. Carthage was a seafaring trading power. Venice and Holland were the commercial centers of Europe in their day.

Hamilton compresses the story of Carthage. Hannibal invaded Italy in the Second Punic War, famously crossing the Alps with war elephants, and Scipio defeated him at the Battle of Zama in North Africa in 202 B.C. That defeat ended Carthage as a great power, but the city itself was destroyed by Rome in a later war, in 146 B.C. Whether Carthage was really "the aggressor" is also something historians debate.

The Dutch fought three wars against England in the 1600s, mostly over trade and sea power. They also led the alliances against Louis XIV, who tried to expand France's borders.
:::

## Britain: wars of the people and wars of trade {orig=14-17}

In Britain's government, one branch of the legislature is made up of the people's representatives. For ages, trade has been that country's main pursuit, yet few nations have gone to war more often. And many of the wars that Britain has fought were started by the people.

There have been almost as many popular wars as royal wars, if I may put it that way. On many occasions, the people's outcry and their representatives' demands have dragged their kings into war, or kept them in it, against the kings' wishes and sometimes against the nation's real interests.

Take the great struggle for supremacy between the rival royal families of Austria and Bourbon, which kept Europe in flames for so long. It is well known that the English hatred of the French helped prolong that war beyond what sound policy called for. That hatred supported the ambition, or rather the greed, of a favorite military leader. For a long time, it kept the war going against the wishes of the royal court.

The wars of Britain and France have largely grown out of trade. They came from the desire to push the other nation out, and the fear of being pushed out, of particular kinds of trade or of the general advantages of trade and shipping. Sometimes they even came from the guiltier desire to share in other nations' trade without their consent.

One war between Britain and Spain started because British merchants tried to carry on an illegal trade with the Spanish colonies in America. This wrong behavior by the British led to harsh treatment of British subjects by the Spanish. That treatment was not any more justified, because it went beyond fair punishment and was inhumane and cruel. Many of the English captured on the Spanish coast were sent to dig in the silver mines of Potosí. And as resentment grew, as it usually does, the innocent were soon punished along with the guilty, without distinction.

The merchants' complaints set off a violent fire of anger across the nation. It soon broke out in the House of Commons, and from there it spread to the king's ministers. Letters of reprisal were issued, and a war followed. Its results overturned all the alliances that had been formed only twenty years earlier, with eager hopes of great benefits.

:::vocab
House of Commons: The elected lower house of Britain's Parliament.
House of Bourbon: The royal family of France, and later of Spain.
Spanish Main: The Spanish-controlled coasts of the Caribbean, Central America, and northern South America.
Potosí: A city in the Andes Mountains, in what is now Bolivia, famous for its huge silver mines worked by forced labor.
Letters of reprisal: Official permission from a government allowing private ships to attack and seize another nation's ships in revenge for wrongs.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point here is aimed squarely at people who thought popular government meant peace. Britain had an elected House of Commons and lived by trade, and its *people* were often more eager for war than its rulers.

The "favorite leader" in Hamilton's footnote is the **Duke of Marlborough**, the great English general in the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714), part of the long rivalry between the Habsburgs of Austria and the Bourbons of France. The British war with Spain is most likely the **War of Jenkins' Ear**, which began in 1739. It got its odd name from a British sea captain, Robert Jenkins, who claimed Spanish coast guards had cut off his ear. The war soon merged into a larger European war.

Hamilton fairly admits that the British merchants were in the wrong to begin with. He is not saying one side was good. He is saying that trade gave both sides reasons for anger and revenge.
:::

## Wake up from the dream {orig=18-20}

This is what has happened in other countries whose situations are most like our own. So what reason do we have to trust the fantasies that would tempt us to expect peace and friendship among the states if they separated? Haven't we already seen enough of the errors and excesses of those empty theories? They have entertained us with promises that we would be free of the flaws, weaknesses, and evils that come with every kind of society. Isn't it time to wake up from the false dream of a golden age? Isn't it time to adopt a practical rule for our political conduct: that we, like everyone else on earth, are still far from the happy kingdom of perfect wisdom and perfect virtue?

Look at how low our national honor and credit have sunk. Look at the problems felt everywhere from weak and poorly run government. Look at the revolt in part of North Carolina, the recent threatening disturbances in Pennsylvania, and the actual uprisings and rebellions in Massachusetts. Let them give the answer!

Most people's understanding of the world is far from the beliefs of those who try to lull us into thinking there is no danger of conflict among the states if they separate. In fact, long observation of how societies develop has made it a kind of axiom in politics that being neighbors makes nations natural enemies. One intelligent writer, the Abbé de Mably, puts it this way:

> "NEIGHBORING NATIONS are naturally enemies of each other unless their common weakness forces them to league in a CONFEDERATE REPUBLIC, and their constitution prevents the differences that neighborhood occasions, extinguishing that secret jealousy which disposes all states to aggrandize themselves at the expense of their neighbors."

This passage points out both the EVIL and the REMEDY.

:::quote
Is it not time to awake from the deceitful dream of a golden age, and to adopt as a practical maxim for the direction of our political conduct that we, as well as the other inhabitants of the globe, are yet remote from the happy empire of perfect wisdom and perfect virtue?

Meaning: Americans are not special exceptions to human nature. We should plan our government for people as they really are, not as we wish they were.
:::

:::quote
NEIGHBORING NATIONS are naturally enemies of each other unless their common weakness forces them to league in a CONFEDERATE REPUBLIC...

Meaning: Countries that share a border tend to become rivals, unless they join together in a union that settles their disputes.
:::

:::vocab
Axiom: A basic truth so obvious that it doesn't need to be proven.
Confederate republic: Montesquieu's term for a union of several smaller republics into a larger one.
Aggrandize: To make larger or more powerful.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton points to three recent troubles. The "revolt of a part of" North Carolina refers to the **State of Franklin**, which settlers in what is now eastern Tennessee declared in 1784, breaking away from North Carolina. The Pennsylvania disturbances most likely refer to the conflict in the **Wyoming Valley**, where settlers from Connecticut and Pennsylvania fought over land claims. The Massachusetts rebellion is **Shays' Rebellion**. His point is that if the states can't keep order *now*, it is foolish to think separate nations would keep peace with each other.

The Abbé de Mably was a French political writer of the 1700s. His quote, like Montesquieu's in [No. 9](/papers/9/), gives Hamilton exactly what he wants: the "evil" is rivalry between neighbors, and the "remedy" is a confederate republic, which is what the Constitution would create.
:::

:::today
The debate Hamilton joined here is still going on. Many people today argue that trade between countries makes war less likely, because nations that depend on each other have too much to lose. That idea helped inspire the European Union, which began after World War II partly to tie France and Germany together economically so they would never fight again. Others side with Hamilton. They point out that countries with close trade ties have still gone to war, and that trade itself can become a source of conflict, through tariff battles, sanctions, and fights over resources. The European Union is also an example of something like Hamilton's remedy: neighbors who were once enemies joining together in a shared system.
:::
