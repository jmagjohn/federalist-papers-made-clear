:::summary
Seats in the House of Representatives, and any "direct taxes," are divided among the states by population. Madison explains and defends the most controversial part of that rule: the compromise that counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person. He states the objection that slaves are treated as property, so they shouldn't be counted for representation. Then he answers it in the voice of an imagined Southerner, who argues that the law treats enslaved people as partly persons and partly property, and that property also deserves some weight in government. Madison admits this reasoning is "a little strained" in places, but says it fully reconciles him to the rule. He ends by noting that tying representation and taxes together gives states a reason to report their populations honestly.
:::

:::keypoints
- House seats and **direct taxes** are both divided among the states by the same rule: population.
- Population is the natural measure of representation. It is a poor measure of wealth, but the most workable one.
- **The objection:** slaves are treated as property, not persons, so they should count for taxes but not for representation.
- **The Southern reply:** the laws treat slaves as **both persons and property**, so the Constitution's "mixed" treatment is correct.
- Counting slaves for taxes but not for representation would be inconsistent, and the South would never have agreed to it.
- Many free people who can't vote are still counted fully, so the South could have demanded more. The compromise treats the slave as "divested of two fifths of the MAN."
- Government protects **property** as well as persons, so wealth deserves some weight in representation.
- Using one rule for both seats and taxes gives states opposite temptations, which balance out and encourage an **honest census**.
:::

:::context Background
This essay is part of a series (Nos. 52–61) about the House of Representatives. It deals with **apportionment**: how the House's seats are divided among the states. The Constitution said that seats would be based on each state's population, counted in a census every ten years. But it added that "three fifths of all other Persons" would be counted along with free persons. "All other Persons" meant enslaved people, though the Constitution never used the word "slave."

The rule was a compromise between Northern and Southern delegates at the Constitutional Convention. During the ratification debate, many Northern critics said it rewarded slaveholding states with extra power. In this essay, Madison, himself a Virginia slaveholder, defends it.
:::

## Seats and taxes: the same rule {orig=1-2}

The next question I will consider about the House of Representatives is how its members are divided among the states. The Constitution decides this by the same rule it uses for dividing direct taxes.

No one argues that the number of people in each state should *not* be the standard for deciding how many representatives it gets. Using the same rule to divide taxes will probably be just as little disputed, even though the rule doesn't rest on the same principle in that case. For representation, the rule is tied to the personal rights of the people, and that connection is natural and universal. For taxes, the rule is tied to wealth. Population is never a precise measure of wealth, and usually it is a poor one.

Still, even with its flaws as a measure of each state's wealth and fair share of taxes, it is clearly the least objectionable rule that could work in practice. And America had approved it so recently that the Convention was naturally ready to prefer it.

:::vocab
Apportionment: Dividing seats in a legislature (or a tax burden) among states or districts, usually by population.
Direct tax: In 1787, a tax such as a tax on each person (a "head tax") or on land. The Constitution required these to be divided among the states by population.
Census: An official count of a country's population. The Constitution requires one every ten years.
:::

:::commentary
The rule Madison describes is in Article I, Section 2 of the Constitution. Each state's population would be found by "adding to the whole Number of free Persons" (including indentured servants, and leaving out "Indians not taxed") "three fifths of all other Persons." That last group meant enslaved people.

When Madison says America had "too recently" approved the rule, he means a 1783 proposal by Congress under the Articles of Confederation. It would have divided the states' payments to Congress by population, counting slaves at three-fifths. Many states approved it, but it never took effect, because changing the Articles required every state's approval. The three-fifths ratio was already familiar when the Convention met.
:::

## The objection: slaves are property {orig=3}

"All this is granted," someone may say. "But does it follow that slaves should be counted when we divide up representatives? We may agree that population should measure representation. We may agree that slaves, along with free citizens, should be counted for taxes. But slaves are considered property, not persons. So they should be included when we figure taxes, which are based on property. And they should be left out of representation, which is based on a count of persons."

This, as I understand it, is the objection at its full strength. I will be just as honest in stating the reasoning that may be offered on the other side.

:::commentary
Notice how Madison sets up the debate. He states the objection himself, and then says he will present the other side "candidly." What follows is not written in his own voice. He puts it in the mouth of an imagined Southerner, "one of our Southern brethren." This lets Madison lay out the slaveholding states' case without saying every word of it as his own. At the end of the essay, though, he says that the argument fully convinces him.

At the Convention, some Northern delegates had made exactly this objection. If slaves were property, they asked, why should they count toward representation any more than the horses and cattle of the North?
:::

## The Southern reply: both persons and property {orig=4}

One of our Southern brethren might answer like this:

"We agree that representation is connected more directly to persons, and taxation more directly to property. And we agree that this distinction applies to our slaves. But we deny that slaves are considered *only* as property, and not in any way as persons. The truth is that they are both. Our laws treat them as persons in some ways and as property in others.

"In some ways, the slave may seem to be lowered from the human rank and classed with the animals that the law calls property. He is forced to work, not for himself, but for a master. He can be sold by one master to another. And at any time, his liberty can be restrained and his body can be punished at the whim of another person.

"On the other hand, the law protects his life and limbs against violence from everyone else, even from the master who owns his labor and his liberty. And he himself can be punished for any violence he commits against others. In these ways, the law clearly treats the slave as a member of society, not as an animal. It treats him as a moral person, not as a mere piece of property.

"So the federal Constitution decides very properly about our slaves when it treats them as a mix of persons and property. This is in fact their true character. It is the character given to them by the laws they live under, and no one will deny that these laws are the proper standard. After all, the only reason anyone disputes their place in the population count is the claim that the laws have turned them into property. And everyone admits that if the laws gave back the rights that have been taken away, they could no longer be denied an equal share of representation with the other inhabitants."

:::commentary
The speaker describes slavery plainly: forced labor, sale from one owner to another, and physical punishment "by the capricious will of another." He then argues that because the law also protects slaves from violence and holds them responsible for crimes, it treats them partly as persons. In practice, that legal protection was weak. In most slave states, for example, enslaved people could not testify in court against white people.

Notice what the speaker admits at the end: slaves are treated as property only because the *laws* made them so. If those laws gave back "the rights which have been taken away," the speaker agrees, they would have to be counted fully, like anyone else.
:::

## Fair to count them for taxes but not seats? {orig=5}

"We can look at the question another way. Everyone agrees that population is the best measure of wealth and taxes, just as it is the only proper measure of representation. Would the Convention have been fair or consistent if it had left slaves off the list of inhabitants when it counted shares of representation, and then put them back on when it set shares of taxes?

"Could anyone reasonably expect the Southern States to agree to a system that treated their slaves partly as men when burdens were handed out, but refused to do so when benefits were handed out?

"And isn't it surprising that some people criticize the Southern States for the barbarous policy of treating part of their fellow human beings as property, yet argue that the national government should treat this unfortunate race even more completely as property than the very state laws they complain about?"

:::commentary
The speaker turns the North's moral criticism around. Northerners called slavery "barbarous" for treating people as property. Yet, the speaker says, by refusing to count slaves for representation, they would treat slaves *more* like property than Southern law did.

The argument is clever, but notice who would gain from counting slaves. Enslaved people would not get any voice in government. The extra seats would go to the states that held them in slavery, and the votes for those seats would be cast by free, mostly white, voters.
:::

## "But slaves can't vote" {orig=6-7}

"Someone may answer that none of the slave states count their slaves when they divide up their own state representatives. Slaves don't vote, and they don't add to their masters' votes. So on what principle should they be counted when representation is divided up by the federal government? If the Constitution had left them out entirely, it would simply have followed the very state laws we just called the proper guide.

"One observation answers this objection. A basic principle of the proposed Constitution is this: the total number of representatives each state gets is set by a federal rule, based on its total number of inhabitants. But the right to *choose* those representatives belongs to whichever inhabitants the state itself decides. The voting qualifications are probably not the same in any two states, and in some states the differences are large. In every state, the state constitution denies the vote to some inhabitants who will still be counted in the federal census that divides up the representatives.

"From this point of view, the Southern States could turn the complaint around. They could insist that the Convention's principle meant ignoring how each state treats its own inhabitants. So slaves, as inhabitants, should have been counted at their full number, just like other inhabitants who don't receive all the rights of citizens under the laws of other states.

"But those who would gain from strictly following this principle are willing to give it up. All they ask is that the other side show equal moderation. Let the case of the slaves be treated as what it truly is: a special one. Let both sides accept the compromise the Constitution offers. It treats slaves as inhabitants, but as inhabitants lowered by slavery below the equal level of free inhabitants. It treats the SLAVE as stripped of two-fifths of the MAN."

:::quote
...which regards the SLAVE as divested of two fifths of the MAN.

Meaning: The compromise counts an enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person. "Divested" means stripped or deprived. This line is the source of the common phrase that the Constitution counted enslaved people as "three-fifths of a person."
:::

:::vocab
Suffrage: The right to vote.
:::

:::commentary
The speaker's comparison is to free people who couldn't vote but were still counted in full. In 1787 that included women, children, and, in many states, men who didn't own enough property. The speaker argues that slaves were in the same position, so the South could have demanded that they count fully. In fact, some South Carolina delegates had argued for exactly that at the Convention.

The practical effect of the clause was large. Enslaved people could not vote and gained nothing from the representation counted in their name. But their numbers added House seats to the slaveholding states. Because each state's votes in the Electoral College equal its number of representatives plus its two senators, the clause also gave those states extra power in choosing the president.
:::

## Property deserves a voice too {orig=8}

"After all, isn't there another argument that defends this part of the Constitution even more easily? So far, we have assumed that representation is about persons only, and not at all about property. But is that right?

"Government exists to protect people's property just as much as their persons. So both property and persons can be thought of as represented by those who run the government. This is why, in several states, and especially New York, one branch of the government is meant to be the special guardian of property. That branch is elected by the part of society most concerned with this goal of government.

"The federal Constitution doesn't follow this policy. It places property rights in the same hands as personal rights. So some attention should be paid to property when choosing those hands."

:::commentary
The speaker points to New York's own state constitution. At the time, only men who owned a certain amount of land could vote for New York's state senate. The idea that the wealthy needed special protection in government was common in 1787. The U.S. Constitution, as the speaker notes, did not set any property requirement for voting in federal elections. It left voting rules to the states.
:::

## Why richer states deserve more weight {orig=9}

"There is another reason why each state's votes in Congress should bear some relation to its wealth compared with the other states. States don't have the kind of influence over each other that wealthy people have over their neighbors.

"The law gives a rich citizen only one vote for his representative. But the respect and importance that come with his fortune very often lead other people to vote the way he wants. Through this quiet, unseen channel, the rights of property make their way into public representation. A state has no such influence over other states. The richest state in the Confederacy will probably never influence the choice of a single representative in any other state. And the representatives of the larger and richer states will have no advantage in Congress over the others, except what comes from having more members. So to the extent that their greater wealth and weight fairly entitle them to any advantage, it should be secured for them by a larger share of representation.

"In this way, the new Constitution is very different from our present Confederation. It is also different from the United Netherlands (the Dutch Republic) and other similar confederacies. In each of those, a federal decision takes effect only if the member states later choose, voluntarily, to carry it out. So even though the states have an equal vote in the common council, their influence is unequal. It depends on how much their later, voluntary decisions matter.

"Under the proposed Constitution, federal laws will take effect without needing the individual states to act. They will depend only on a majority vote in Congress. So every vote will have equal weight and effect, whether it comes from a larger or smaller state, or a richer or poorer one. It is the same in a state legislature. There, the votes of members from counties or districts of unequal size have exactly equal value. If there is any difference, it comes from the personal character of the representative, not from the size of the district he comes from."

:::vocab
Confederacy: A league of independent states that agree to act together for certain purposes.
:::

:::commentary
This argument is about states, not individuals. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress could only *ask* the states for money and troops, so a rich state's cooperation mattered more than a poor state's, even though each had one vote. Under the Constitution, federal laws act directly on individuals, so a rich state loses that informal influence. The speaker says it should be made up with extra seats.

Notice how this argument ties back to slavery. In the speaker's view, enslaved people were part of the South's wealth, and that wealth deserved representation.
:::

## Madison's verdict, and an honest census {orig=10-11}

That is the kind of reasoning someone speaking for the Southern interests might use on this subject. It may seem a little strained in some places. But on the whole, I must confess that it fully reconciles me to the rule for representation that the Convention has set up.

In one respect, using the same measure for both representation and taxes will have a very healthy effect. The accuracy of the census Congress takes will depend a great deal on the attitude, and maybe even the cooperation, of the states. So it is very important that the states feel as little temptation as possible to inflate or shrink their numbers. If the count decided only their share of representation, they would want to exaggerate their population. If it decided only their share of taxes, they would be tempted the opposite way. By applying the rule to both, the states will have opposite interests. These interests will control and balance each other, and produce the fairness we need.

:::commentary
Madison steps back into his own voice. He calls the Southern argument "a little strained," but he does not reject it. He says it "fully reconciles" him to the rule. Madison owned enslaved people at his Virginia plantation, Montpelier, for his whole life. At the Convention, he had said it would be wrong for the Constitution to admit the idea that there could be property in men. In this essay, he defends a rule that rested on exactly that idea.

His closing point about the census is a classic Federalist argument: set interests against each other so they balance. But it worked less well than he suggested. Congress rarely imposed direct taxes, so the tax side of the bargain mattered much less than the representation side. Slaveholding states gained extra House seats and electoral votes from every census until the Civil War.
:::

:::today
The three-fifths clause no longer has any force. The 13th Amendment (1865) abolished slavery. The 14th Amendment (1868) replaced the clause with a rule that House seats would be divided by "the whole number of persons in each State," leaving out only "Indians not taxed." But the basic system Madison described remains. Every ten years, the census still decides how many House seats and electoral votes each state gets. Americans still argue about who should be counted and where, for example whether to count noncitizens, and whether prisoners should be counted where they are held or in their home communities. Federalist No. 54 is a reminder that how a country counts its people decides who holds power.
:::
