:::summary
Hamilton finishes his defense of Congress's power over the "times, places and manner" of federal elections. Some critics say the Constitution should at least have required elections to be held in the counties where voters live. Hamilton answers that such a rule would do no harm, but it would add little real protection. He points out that New York's own constitution gives the state legislature just as much room to make voting hard, and no one calls it a threat to liberty. He then names a positive benefit of the clause: Congress can set **one election day** for the whole House. If states voted at different times, the House would never be fully renewed at once, and a bad spirit in it could pass from old members to new ones. Finally, he explains why the Constitution leaves the exact date to Congress instead of fixing it.
:::

:::keypoints
- The fairer critics accept the clause but want a rule that elections be held **in each voter's county**.
- Such a rule would be **harmless** and might calm fears, but it would add **little real security**.
- **New York's constitution** gives its legislature similar freedom over where elections are held. If the federal clause is dangerous, so is the state's.
- There's no reason to think **state representatives** are more trustworthy than national ones, and the whole nation is **harder to oppress** than a single state.
- Congress's power allows **one uniform time** for House elections, so the whole House can be **replaced at once**.
- A House that changed **gradually** would let a bad spirit **spread** from old members to new ones.
- A uniform time also helps the **Senate's rotation** and lets Congress **meet on a set date** each year.
- The date wasn't fixed in the Constitution because a **legislature can choose better**, and states may want to hold state and federal elections together.
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of Hamilton's three essays on the Elections Clause (Article I, Section 4). In [No. 59](/papers/59/), he argued that Congress needs the final say so the states can't destroy the national government. In [No. 60](/papers/60/), he argued that Congress wouldn't use the power to favor the rich.

Here he answers a more moderate objection. Some critics were willing to accept the clause if it were limited, for example by requiring that elections be held in each county, close to where voters lived. In 1788, traveling even twenty miles to vote could take a full day or more on horseback or on foot.
:::

## Should elections have to be held in each county? {orig=1}

The fairer opponents of the Constitution's election clause will sometimes admit, when pressed in argument, that it is proper. But they add a condition. They say it should have come with a statement that all elections must be held in the counties where the voters live. This, they say, was a necessary safeguard against abuse of the power.

A statement like this would certainly have been harmless. To the extent that it calmed people's fears, it might even have been a good thing. But in fact it would have added little or no real protection against the feared danger. And a fair and sensible judge will never see its absence as a serious objection to the plan, much less as one that can't be overcome.

The different points made in the two papers before this one should satisfy all calm and thoughtful people of this: if public liberty is ever sacrificed to the ambition of the national rulers, this power, at least, will not be to blame.

:::vocab
Candid: In 1787, this usually meant fair-minded and open to reason, not just "honest" or "blunt."
Insuperable: Impossible to overcome.
:::

## New York's own constitution {orig=2}

Some people pay attention only to their suspicions. If they would use those suspicions to look carefully at the state constitutions, they would find almost as much reason for worry in the freedom most states allow over elections as in the freedom given to the national government. A review of the states would do a lot to remove any bad impressions that remain on this subject. But since that would lead into long and boring details, I'll use just one example: the state in which I write.

New York's constitution says only this about *where* elections are held. Members of the Assembly are to be elected in the COUNTIES. Members of the state Senate are to be elected in the large districts into which the state is or may be divided. There are four of these districts right now, and each includes from two to six counties.

It is easy to see that the New York legislature could defeat the votes of New York's citizens by limiting elections to certain places just as easily as Congress could defeat the votes of the Union's citizens the same way. Suppose, for instance, that the city of Albany were made the only place to vote for its county and district. Wouldn't the people of that city soon become the only voters for the state Senate and Assembly from that county and district?

Think of voters who live in the remote parts of Albany, Saratoga, Cambridge, and so on, or anywhere in Montgomery County. Can we imagine they would take the trouble to travel to Albany to vote for the Assembly or Senate, any more than they would travel to New York City to help choose members of the federal House of Representatives?

The alarming lack of interest people already show in using this precious right, under laws that make it as easy as possible, gives a ready answer. And even without experience, we can easily see that when the polling place is an INCONVENIENT DISTANCE from the voter, the effect on him will be the same, whether that distance is twenty miles or twenty thousand.

So it must be clear that objections to the way the federal power over elections is arranged apply, in substance, just as strongly to the same power in New York's constitution. For this reason, it is impossible to approve one and condemn the other. A similar comparison would lead to the same conclusion about most of the other states' constitutions.

:::commentary
Hamilton turns the opponents' argument against them. Many of the Constitution's strongest critics in New York were also proud defenders of the state's own constitution of 1777. Hamilton shows that the state constitution has the same supposed flaw: it doesn't stop the legislature from making people travel far to vote.

His remark about the "alarming indifference" of voters is striking. Even in 1788, turnout was often low, and Hamilton admits it. His point is that any real inconvenience would keep people home, whether it was caused by the state or by Congress.
:::

## Why judge the states differently? {orig=3}

Someone may say that flaws in the state constitutions are no excuse for flaws in the proposed plan. I answer this way. No one has ever accused the state constitutions of carelessness about protecting liberty. So when the charges made against the new plan turn out to apply to them too, we should assume the charges are the nitpicking of opponents who made up their minds in advance, not the sound conclusions of an honest search for truth.

Some people see as innocent oversights in the state constitutions the very things they call unforgivable flaws in the Convention's plan. Nothing can be said to them. At most, we can ask them to give some solid reason why the representatives of the people of a single state should be harder for the lust for power, or other bad motives, to corrupt than the representatives of the people of the United States.

If they can't do this, they ought at least to prove to us that it is easier to destroy the liberties of three million people, who have their state governments to lead their resistance, than the liberties of two hundred thousand people who have no such advantage.

And on the specific point we are discussing, they ought to convince us of one more thing. They must show that a ruling faction in a single state, trying to keep its power, is less likely to favor a particular class of voters than the representatives of thirteen states would be. Those representatives will be spread over a huge territory, and they will differ from each other in many ways because of their different local conditions, prejudices, and interests.

:::vocab
Caviling: Raising petty or trivial objections.
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton returns to two of the Federalists' favorite arguments. First, a large nation is harder to oppress than a small state, because the states stand ready to lead the resistance (see [No. 28](/papers/28/) and [No. 46](/papers/46/)). Second, a single faction is more likely to take over a small government than a large one with many different interests, as Madison argued in [No. 10](/papers/10/).
:::

## The benefit of one election day {orig=4-5}

So far, I have defended this provision on three grounds: that it is right in principle, that it would be dangerous to place the power anywhere else, and that it is safe to place it as proposed. But there is still a positive advantage to mention, which could not have been gained as well any other way. I mean that the time of elections for the federal House of Representatives can be made the same everywhere.

Experience may well show that this uniform timing is very important for the public good. It can be a protection against the same spirit lasting on and on in the House, and a cure for the diseases of faction.

If each state could choose its own election date, there might be as many different dates as there are months in the year. The dates the states now use for their own elections range from as early as March to as late as November. Because of this variety, the House could never be completely dissolved and renewed at one time. If a bad spirit of any kind took hold in it, that spirit would tend to pass into the new members as they arrived, one group after another. The body as a whole would probably stay nearly the same, constantly shaping the newcomers to fit in. There is a contagion in example that few people have the strength of mind to resist.

I am inclined to think that a term *three times as long*, with the whole body replaced at once, might be less dangerous to liberty than a term one-third as long, with members changing gradually and one group at a time.

A uniform time for elections also seems necessary to carry out the plan for a regular rotation in the Senate, and to bring Congress together conveniently on a set date each year.

:::quote
There is a contagion in example which few men have sufficient force of mind to resist.

Meaning: People tend to copy the behavior of those around them. New members joining a group one at a time usually take on the group's habits instead of changing them.
:::

:::vocab
Uniformity: Being the same everywhere.
Renovation: In 1787, this could mean renewal, such as replacing the members of a legislature.
Rotation (in the Senate): The system by which one-third of the Senate's seats come up for election every two years.
:::

:::commentary
This is Hamilton's most original point in the three papers. He worries that a legislature that changes slowly develops a culture that absorbs and shapes new members. A body elected all at once, by contrast, can be swept clean by the voters. It's an interesting contrast with the Senate, which was *designed* to change slowly so that it would be stable (see [No. 62](/papers/62/) and [No. 63](/papers/63/)).

Hamilton's hope took a long time to come true. The Constitution allowed a single election day but didn't require one. In 1845, Congress set a uniform day for choosing presidential electors: the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. Later laws set the same day for House elections and, after the 17th Amendment let the people elect senators, for Senate elections. Even so, exceptions lasted for a long time. Maine, for example, held its congressional elections in September well into the 1900s. A common explanation for the choice of a Tuesday in November is that it came after the harvest and gave rural voters time to travel without voting on Sunday.
:::

## Why not fix a date in the Constitution? {orig=6}

Someone may ask: then why didn't the Constitution fix a time? The most eager opponents of the Convention's plan in this state are, in general, just as eager admirers of the state's own constitution. So we may turn the question around and ask: why didn't New York's constitution fix a time for the same purpose?

No better answer can be given than this. It was a matter that could safely be left to the judgment of the legislature. And if a time had been set, experience might have shown it to be less convenient than some other time.

The same answer applies to the question about the federal Constitution. We may add one more point. The supposed danger of gradual change is only a theory. It would hardly have been wise, because of a theory, to set a fixed rule in the Constitution that would take away from several states the convenience of holding their own elections and the national elections at the same time.

:::commentary
Hamilton's answer shows a theme that runs through the Constitution: leave details to the legislature so they can be adjusted as conditions change. The Constitution itself originally said Congress would meet each year on the first Monday in December, unless Congress set a different day by law. The 20th Amendment (1933) moved the start of Congress's term to January 3. That change came partly because the long gap between Election Day and the start of a new Congress let defeated "lame duck" members keep voting for months.
:::

:::today
Election Day itself is still debated. Some people want it to be a national holiday, or moved to a weekend, so more people can vote. Many states now offer early voting or voting by mail, so for millions of Americans "Election Day" has become an election season. Supporters say this makes voting easier and reduces lines. Critics worry about the security of mail ballots and about results taking longer to count, and there have been court fights over whether ballots that arrive after Election Day can count. Hamilton's concern about distance also lives on in arguments over how many polling places there should be and where they should be located. All of these debates still rest on the clause Hamilton defended: states set the rules first, and Congress can change them.
:::
