:::summary
Hamilton paints a grim picture of America under the Articles of Confederation. The country can't pay its debts, can't force Britain to leave its forts, can't get Spain to open the Mississippi River, and has no respect abroad. Everyone agrees something is wrong. But Hamilton says the problem isn't a few small flaws. It is a basic design error: Congress makes rules for **states**, not for **individual people**. So its "laws" are really just requests, and the states ignore them whenever they like. A real government must be able to enforce its laws on individuals through courts. Otherwise the only way to enforce them is war against the states.
:::

:::keypoints
- America has reached "almost the last stage of national humiliation": unpaid debts, lost territory, no army, no money, and no respect.
- Even opponents of the Constitution admit the system is broken, but they **oppose the only cure that could work**.
- The "great and radical vice" of the Articles: Congress can only make rules for **states as governments**, not for **individual citizens**.
- So Congress's decisions are laws in theory but **"mere recommendations"** in practice.
- A law without a **penalty** is only advice. Penalties can be enforced on individuals by courts, but on states only by **military force**.
- Government exists "because the passions of men will not conform to the dictates of reason and justice, without constraint."
- Groups act worse than individuals, and people with power resist being controlled. So states will never reliably obey a central government that depends on them.
:::

:::context Background
The Articles of Confederation were adopted during the Revolutionary War and took effect in 1781. They created a "league of friendship" among the states. Congress could declare war, make treaties, and borrow money, but it had no power to tax and no way to enforce its decisions. To raise money, it sent "requisitions" (requests) to the states, which often paid little or nothing. Between 1781 and 1786, the states paid only a fraction of what Congress asked for. Meanwhile, the country owed millions of dollars to foreign lenders and to American soldiers and citizens who had funded the war.

This essay opens a new part of the series. Nos. 2–14 argued that the Union is valuable. Nos. 15–22 argue that the Articles can't preserve it.
:::

## The journey ahead {orig=1-2}

My fellow citizens, in the essays so far I have tried to show you, clearly and convincingly, how important the Union is to your political safety and happiness. I have described the many dangers you would face if you let ambition or greed, jealousy or lies, cut the sacred knot that ties the American people together.

As we go on, new facts and arguments will further confirm these truths. Parts of the road ahead may seem long or tiresome. If so, remember a few things. You are seeking information on the most important subject a free people can consider. The ground we must cover is wide. And people using clever but false arguments have made the trip harder than it needs to be. I will try to clear the way as briefly as I can, without giving up usefulness for speed.

Following the plan I set out, the next topic is the "insufficiency of the present Confederation to the preservation of the Union." Someone might ask why this needs any proof. Hardly anyone disputes it. People of every class agree with it, in their heads and in their hearts. Even the new Constitution's opponents admit it, at least in substance.

It's true that, however much they disagree on other points, both sides seem to agree that our national system has serious flaws, and that something must be done to save us from coming anarchy. The facts behind this view are no longer guesses. The people have felt them directly. And these facts have finally forced a reluctant admission from the very people whose mistaken policies did the most to bring us to this crisis. They now confess the defects that wise friends of the Union have pointed out and regretted for a long time.

:::vocab
Confederation: The union of the states under the Articles of Confederation, the first national government (1781–1789).
Anarchy: A state of disorder with no effective government.
Sophistry: Clever-sounding arguments that are actually misleading or false.
:::

## The last stage of national humiliation {orig=3}

We have almost reached the last stage of national humiliation. There is hardly anything that can hurt the pride or lower the character of an independent nation that we are not experiencing.

- **Do we have promises we are bound by every rule of honor to keep?** We break them constantly and shamelessly.
- **Do we owe debts to foreigners and to our own citizens, borrowed when our very existence was in danger?** We have made no proper plan to pay them.
- **Does a foreign power still hold valuable territory and important forts that it agreed, by treaty, to hand over long ago?** It still keeps them, against our interests and our rights.
- **Can we push back against this aggression?** We have no troops, no treasury, and no government.
- **Can we at least protest with dignity?** Not until we answer fair charges that we have broken the same treaty ourselves.
- **Do nature and treaty give us the right to use the Mississippi River?** Spain shuts us out of it.
- **Is public credit, the ability to borrow, essential in times of danger?** We seem to have given it up as hopeless.
- **Is trade important to national wealth?** Ours is at its lowest point.
- **Does respect from foreign powers protect against foreign attacks?** Our government is so weak that other nations won't even negotiate with us. Our ambassadors abroad are just actors pretending to represent a real nation.
- **Is a sharp, unnatural fall in land prices a sign of national distress?** In most of the country, farmland is worth far less than the supply of open land can explain. The only full explanation is the lack of public and private confidence, which is alarmingly common among all classes and lowers the value of every kind of property.
- **Does private credit help business grow?** The most useful kind, lending and borrowing, has shrunk to almost nothing. That's due more to fear that loans won't be repaid than to any shortage of money.

To cut short a list that gives neither pleasure nor instruction, we might simply ask: what sign of national disorder, poverty, and weakness could strike a country as blessed by nature as ours, that is not already on this dark list of our public misfortunes?

:::commentary
Hamilton's list refers to real problems of the 1780s:

- **British forts:** The 1783 Treaty of Paris required Britain to leave its forts in the Great Lakes region. Britain refused, partly because American states had broken the treaty too, by blocking British creditors from collecting prewar debts. That's why Hamilton says America couldn't "remonstrate with dignity."
- **The Mississippi:** Spain controlled New Orleans and closed the river to American shipping in 1784. Western farmers depended on the river to get their crops to market.
- **Debts:** The national government owed about $40 million in foreign and domestic debt and couldn't even pay the interest.
- **"Neither troops, nor treasury, nor government":** Hamilton's footnote clarifies that he means the *national* government. The states had all three.

This passage is persuasive writing, and Hamilton paints the darkest picture he can. Some historians argue that the 1780s were not quite as disastrous as the Federalists claimed, and that the economy was starting to recover by 1787. But the national government's helplessness was real.
:::

:::vocab
Public credit: A government's ability to borrow money, which depends on lenders trusting it to repay.
Ambassador: An official who represents a country in a foreign nation.
:::

## Stand firm now {orig=4}

This is the sad situation we have been brought to by the very ideas and advice that now urge us not to adopt the Constitution. Not satisfied with leading us to the edge of a cliff, they seem determined to push us into the pit below.

Here, my countrymen, moved by every motive that should guide an informed people, let us take a firm stand for our safety, our peace, our dignity, and our reputation. Let us finally break the deadly spell that has kept us so long from the path of happiness and prosperity.

## Opponents want the impossible {orig=5}

It's true, as I said before, that facts too stubborn to resist have led nearly everyone to agree, in general terms, that our national system has serious flaws. But the longtime opponents of national measures make that admission useless. They fiercely oppose the only kind of remedy that has any chance of working.

They admit the government of the United States has no energy. But they fight against giving it the powers needed to supply that energy. They still seem to want things that contradict each other and can't fit together: more national authority without less state authority, a sovereign Union with completely independent members. In short, they still seem blindly devoted to the political monster of an *imperium in imperio*, a government within a government.

This makes it necessary to lay out the main flaws of the Confederation in full. We need to show that our troubles don't come from small or partial imperfections. They come from basic errors in the structure of the building itself. These can only be fixed by changing its founding principles and main pillars.

:::vocab
Imperium in imperio: Latin for "a sovereignty within a sovereignty." Hamilton uses it to mean two supreme governments over the same people, which he thought was impossible.
Energy (in government): Strength and the ability to act effectively.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton says his opponents want two incompatible things: a national government strong enough to work, and states that remain completely sovereign. Madison would later argue in [No. 39](/papers/39/) that the Constitution actually *does* divide sovereignty, giving each level supreme power in its own area. So the Constitution itself became a kind of answer to Hamilton's "monster," by splitting authority carefully rather than stacking one sovereign on top of another.
:::

## The great flaw: laws for states, not people {orig=6-7}

The great, basic flaw in the design of the current Confederation is this: it makes LEGISLATION for STATES or GOVERNMENTS, as COLLECTIVE bodies, rather than for the INDIVIDUAL people who live in them.

This principle doesn't apply to every power given to the Union. But it controls the powers that all the others depend on. The United States has unlimited discretion to make requisitions, or requests, for soldiers and money. But it has no authority to raise either one through rules that apply to individual American citizens.

As a result, its decisions on these matters are, in theory, laws that legally bind the states. But in practice they are just recommendations, which the states follow or ignore as they choose.

It shows how unpredictable the human mind can be that, after all the warnings experience has given us, some people still object to the new Constitution for abandoning this principle. This principle has been the curse of the old system. It obviously can't work with the very idea of GOVERNMENT. In short, it's a principle that, if it is enforced at all, must replace the mild influence of courts and officials with the violent and bloody force of the sword.

:::quote
The great and radical vice in the construction of the existing Confederation is in the principle of LEGISLATION for STATES or GOVERNMENTS, in their CORPORATE or COLLECTIVE CAPACITIES, and as contradistinguished from the INDIVIDUALS of which they consist.

Meaning: The Articles' deepest problem was that Congress could only give orders to state governments, not to individual people.
:::

:::vocab
Requisition: A formal request. Under the Articles, Congress asked the states for money and soldiers but couldn't make them provide either.
Radical: Here, "at the root" or "basic," not "extreme."
Vice: A serious flaw or defect.
:::

:::commentary
This is the single most important idea in the essays about the Articles. Imagine a school club that can only *ask* its members for dues, with no way to make anyone pay. Some members pay, many don't, and soon the club can't do anything. Now multiply that by thirteen states and a war debt in the millions.

The Constitution fixed this by letting the national government act directly on individuals. Congress can tax you, federal laws apply to you, and federal courts can enforce them. That one change, more than any other, turned a league of states into a real government.
:::

## A league is not a government {orig=8-10}

There's nothing absurd or unworkable about a league or alliance between independent nations for certain specific purposes. Such a treaty spells out every detail of time, place, circumstance, and amount. It leaves nothing to future judgment, and it depends on each side's good faith to carry it out. All civilized nations make agreements like this. Like everything else between nations, they are kept or broken, through peace and war, as the interests or passions of the parties dictate.

Early in this century, Europe had a craze for this kind of agreement. Politicians of the time hoped they would bring benefits that never came. They used every tool of diplomacy to create a balance of power and peace in that part of the world. They formed triple and quadruple alliances. But these alliances were broken almost as soon as they were made. That taught humanity a useful but painful lesson: treaties held together only by good faith can't be relied on. They set general ideas of peace and justice against immediate interests and passions, and the interests and passions usually win.

If the states of this country want to have that kind of relationship with each other, and give up the idea of a general authority that can make decisions over them, the plan would indeed be harmful. It would bring us all the evils described in the earlier essays. But it would at least be consistent and workable. Giving up any idea of a confederate government, this would leave us with a simple alliance for attack and defense. We would become friends and enemies by turns, as our jealousies and rivalries, encouraged by the schemes of foreign nations, directed.

But suppose we don't want to be in that dangerous position. Suppose we still want a national government, or, what amounts to the same thing, a supervising power directed by a common council. Then we must include in our plan the features that make the difference between a league and a government. We must extend the Union's authority to the citizens themselves, who are the only proper objects of government.

:::commentary
Hamilton draws a sharp line between a **league** (like an alliance between countries) and a **government**. A league depends on good faith, and good faith breaks down whenever a member's interests change. Think of how international agreements today can fall apart when a country decides it no longer benefits. A government is different because it can enforce its rules.

The "triple and quadruple alliances" were European treaties from the early 1700s, such as the Triple Alliance of 1717 between Britain, France, and the Netherlands, and the Quadruple Alliance of 1718. They were meant to keep the peace but quickly fell apart.
:::

## A law needs a penalty {orig=11}

Government means the power to make laws. It is essential to the idea of a law that it comes with a sanction, in other words, a penalty or punishment for disobeying it. If there's no penalty for disobedience, the so-called laws are really nothing more than advice or recommendations.

This penalty, whatever it is, can only be imposed in two ways. One is through the courts and officers of justice. The other is through military force. In other words, by the COERCION of the courts, or the COERCION of arms.

The first kind can obviously apply only to individual people. The second must be used against political bodies, communities, or states. There's clearly no court procedure that can, in the end, force a state to obey the laws. Courts may issue rulings against states for failing in their duties. But those rulings can only be carried out with the sword.

In a union where the central authority deals only with the states as collective bodies, every violation of the laws must lead to a state of war. Military force must become the only way to make anyone obey. Such a situation surely doesn't deserve to be called a government, and no sensible person would trust his happiness to it.

:::quote
It is essential to the idea of a law, that it be attended with a sanction; or, in other words, a penalty or punishment for disobedience.

Meaning: A rule with no consequence for breaking it isn't really a law. It's just a suggestion.
:::

:::vocab
Sanction: A penalty for breaking a law.
Coercion: Forcing someone to do something, by threat or by force.
Magistracy: Courts, judges, and officials who enforce the law.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's logic is simple and powerful. You can fine or jail a person who breaks the law. But how do you punish a *state*? You can't put Virginia in jail. The only way is to send an army, which means civil war. So a union that acts only on states has to choose between being ignored and going to war.

Ironically, the United States did eventually go to war with its own states, in 1861. But that war was about whether states could leave the Union, not about a central government trying to collect taxes from them, which is the situation Hamilton describes.
:::

## Why states won't obey: human nature {orig=12-13}

There was a time when we were told not to expect the states to break the rules of the national government. People said that a sense of shared interest would guide each member and lead them to fully meet all of the Union's constitutional requests. Today, that kind of talk sounds wild. So will much of what we hear from the same people now, once we have learned more lessons from experience, the best teacher of wisdom.

That argument always showed ignorance of what really drives human behavior. It also ignored the original reason governments were created. Why has government been created at all? Because human passions will not follow the commands of reason and justice without being forced to.

Have we found that groups of people act more honestly or more selflessly than individuals? Every careful observer of human behavior has concluded the opposite, and for obvious reasons. Concern for reputation has less effect when the shame of a bad act is shared among many people than when it falls on one. And a spirit of faction, which tends to poison the decisions of every group, often pushes its members into wrong and extreme actions they would be ashamed of as individuals.

On top of all this, people who hold sovereign power naturally resent being controlled. This makes them look with suspicion on any outside attempt to limit or direct how they use it. So in every political union made up of smaller sovereign powers, there is a tendency for the smaller parts to pull away from the center, like objects flying off a spinning wheel.

This tendency isn't hard to explain. It comes from the love of power. A power that is controlled or limited is almost always the rival and enemy of the power that controls or limits it. This simple fact shows how little reason there is to expect the leaders of each member state to always carry out the decisions of the central authority cheerfully and with fair regard for the public good. Human nature produces the opposite result.

:::quote
Why has government been instituted at all? Because the passions of men will not conform to the dictates of reason and justice, without constraint.

Meaning: If people always did what was reasonable and fair, we wouldn't need government. We need government because they don't.
:::

:::commentary
This passage shares the realistic view of human nature that runs through the whole series. Compare Madison's "If men were angels, no government would be necessary" in [No. 51](/papers/51/). Hamilton adds two sharp observations:

- **Groups behave worse than individuals.** When blame is shared, no one feels fully responsible. Think about how people act in a crowd or online, compared to how they act alone.
- **Power resents control.** State leaders would always see the national government as a rival. That's not because they are bad people, but because people with power want to keep it.
:::

## How the system broke down {orig=14-15}

So if the Confederacy's decisions can't be carried out without the help of the individual state governments, there is little chance they will be carried out at all.

The leaders of each state, whether they have a legal right to or not, will decide for themselves whether the measures are proper. They will ask whether a request fits their immediate interests or goals, and whether it is convenient for them right now. They will do this with a selfish and suspicious attitude. They won't have the knowledge of national conditions and needs required for good judgment, and they will strongly favor local interests, which will almost surely mislead them.

This same process must be repeated in every member state. So the plans made by the council of the whole will always depend on the poorly informed and biased opinions of every part. Anyone who has watched popular assemblies knows how hard it often is, even without outside pressure, to get them to agree on important issues. So it's easy to see how impossible it must be to get many such assemblies, meeting far apart, at different times, and with different views, to cooperate on the same goals for very long.

In our case, under the Confederation, every important national measure needs the agreement of thirteen separate sovereign wills to be fully carried out. What happened was exactly what anyone could have predicted. The Union's measures have not been carried out. The states' failures have grown, step by step, until they have stopped all the wheels of the national government and brought them to a terrible halt. Congress today barely has the means to keep up the appearance of governing until the states can agree on a real replacement for this shadow of a national government.

Things didn't reach this desperate point all at once. At first, the causes I've described only made the states obey the Union's requests unevenly. Some states fell much further behind than others. That gave the states that paid, or paid more, both an example and a selfish reason to cut back. "Why should we do more than our share, when others in the same boat are doing less? Why should we carry more than our fair part of the common load?" Human selfishness couldn't resist these questions, and even thoughtful people who saw the long-term consequences found them hard to argue against. Giving in to the persuasive voice of immediate interest or convenience, each state has, one after another, withdrawn its support. Now the weak and shaky building seems ready to fall on our heads and crush us under its ruins.

:::quote
Why should we do more in proportion than those who are embarked with us in the same political voyage? Why should we consent to bear more than our proper share of the common burden?

Meaning: Once some states stopped paying, the others asked why they should keep paying. The system unraveled.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is describing what economists today call a **collective action problem** or **free-rider problem**. Everyone benefits from the national government, but each state gains by letting others pay for it. Once a few states stop paying, the honest states feel like suckers, and they stop too. Group projects at school can work the same way when one person does nothing and still gets the same grade.

The solution Hamilton proposes is to stop depending on voluntary contributions at all, by giving the national government its own power to tax individuals. He develops that argument in Nos. 30–36.
:::

:::today
The problem Hamilton describes still shows up wherever independent members try to act together without a central authority that can enforce rules. Alliances like NATO have long debated members who don't pay their agreed share of defense costs. The United Nations depends on member nations to enforce its decisions. Climate agreements rely on each country's good faith. In each case, Hamilton's question applies: can an agreement work if it can only make requests, not laws?
:::
