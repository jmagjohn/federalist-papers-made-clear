:::summary
Hamilton defends the way the Constitution fills the top offices of the national government. The president nominates, and the Senate must approve. Hamilton argues that one person will judge candidates more carefully than a group, because he alone will be responsible and will have fewer friends to reward. When a group makes appointments, the choice usually comes from party victories or deals: "Give us the man we wish for this office, and you shall have the one you wish for that." But the Senate's approval is still valuable. It quietly guards against favoritism, against unfit choices, and against picking people only because they come from the president's state or family. Hamilton denies that a president could buy the Senate's cooperation, and he points to rules that keep members of Congress from taking offices they created.
:::

:::keypoints
- The president **nominates**, and with the Senate's **advice and consent**, appoints ambassadors, Supreme Court justices, and other officers. Congress may let lesser officers be appointed in other ways.
- "The true test of a good government is its aptitude and tendency to produce a good administration," and that depends on choosing good officials.
- **One person of good judgment** is better at matching people to particular jobs than a group.
- A single appointer feels **sole responsibility** and has fewer personal attachments. Groups make choices through **party victories or bargains**, not merit.
- The power to **nominate** gives the president nearly all the advantages of the power to appoint. Everyone appointed will be his choice.
- The Senate will **rarely reject** a nominee without strong reasons, but the need for its approval will be a **silent check** on favoritism and unfit choices.
- The president can't buy the whole Senate. People are neither **completely corrupt nor completely virtuous**.
- The Constitution bars members of Congress from taking **new or better-paid offices** created during their terms.
:::

:::context Background
Who should appoint government officials was a hard question at the Constitutional Convention. For much of the summer, the draft plan gave the Senate the power to appoint Supreme Court justices and ambassadors. Others wanted the president to appoint alone. Near the end, the Convention settled on a compromise: the president nominates, and the Senate confirms.

Hamilton knew the problem from New York. There, the governor shared the appointing power with a "Council of Appointment" chosen by the legislature. Hamilton had already criticized it in [No. 70](/papers/70/), where he said that the governor and council blamed each other for scandalous appointments. He will compare the two systems in detail in [No. 77](/papers/77/).
:::

## The appointments clause {orig=1}

The Constitution says the president is "to nominate, and, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, to appoint ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, judges of the Supreme Court, and all other officers of the United States whose appointments are not otherwise provided for in the Constitution. But the Congress may by law vest the appointment of such inferior officers as they think proper, in the President alone, or in the courts of law, or in the heads of departments. The President shall have power to fill up all vacancies which may happen during the recess of the Senate, by granting commissions which shall expire at the end of their next session."

:::commentary
This clause sets up three ways of filling offices. The most important officers must be **nominated** by the president and **confirmed** by the Senate. Today that includes cabinet secretaries, federal judges, ambassadors, and many other top officials, more than a thousand positions in all. Congress may let **inferior officers** (lower-level officials) be appointed by the president alone, by the courts, or by department heads. And the president may make **recess appointments** while the Senate is away, which last until the end of the Senate's next session.

Recess appointments have caused fights between presidents and the Senate. In *NLRB v. Noel Canning* (2014), the Supreme Court ruled that the Senate is in session when it says it is, as long as it can still conduct business. The Court also said a break of fewer than ten days is usually too short to count as a recess.
:::

:::vocab
Ambassador: An official who represents a country in a foreign nation.
Consul: An official who lives in a foreign city to help his own country's citizens and businesses there.
Advice and consent: The Senate's constitutional role in approving the president's nominations and treaties.
Inferior officers: Lower-level officials whose appointment Congress may give to the president alone, the courts, or department heads.
Commission: An official document that gives someone the authority of an office.
:::

## Good government depends on good officials {orig=2-3}

In an earlier paper I observed that "the true test of a good government is its aptitude and tendency to produce a good administration." If that is right, then the method of appointing officers in these clauses deserves special praise when we examine it. It is hard to imagine a plan better designed to promote wise choices of people to fill the offices of the Union. And it needs no proof that the character of the government's administration will depend mainly on this.

Everyone will agree that, in ordinary cases, the power of appointment should be arranged in one of three ways. It should be given to a single man. Or it should be given to a select assembly of moderate size. Or it should be given to a single man acting with the agreement of such an assembly. Everyone will easily admit that the people as a whole cannot make appointments. Leaving aside every other reason, it would leave them little time to do anything else. So when I mention an assembly or a body of men below, I mean a select assembly like the one I just described.

The people as a whole are too numerous and too spread out to be organized by the systematic scheming and plotting that I will point to as the main objection to giving this power to a group.

:::commentary
The "earlier paper" is [No. 68](/papers/68/), where Hamilton used the same line while defending the way the president is chosen. It sums up his view of government. A constitution is judged not by how good it sounds, but by whether it puts capable people in office and gets the job done.
:::

:::vocab
Administration: The day-to-day running of the government, and the officials who do it.
Cabal: A small, secret group plotting together, usually to gain power.
Intrigue: Secret scheming.
:::

## Why one person chooses better than a group {orig=4-5}

Some readers have thought about how the president will be chosen, or have read what was said about it in earlier papers. I assume they will agree that the office will very likely always be filled by a man of at least respectable ability. With that in mind, I state this rule. One man of good judgment is better able to analyze and weigh the particular qualities needed for particular offices than a group of men with equal, or perhaps even greater, judgment.

The sole and undivided responsibility of one man will naturally create a stronger sense of duty and a closer concern for his reputation. So he will feel more obligated, and more motivated, to study carefully the qualities each office requires. He will also feel more bound to pick fairly the people with the best claims to those offices. He will have fewer personal friendships to satisfy than a group of men, each of whom may have just as many. So he will be much less likely to be misled by friendship and affection. A single, well-directed man, with a single mind, can't be pulled apart and twisted by the variety of views, feelings, and interests that often pull apart and twist the decisions of a group.

Nothing stirs people's passions as much as personal matters, whether they concern ourselves or others we are choosing among. So whenever an assembly of men makes appointments, we must expect to see a full display of all its members' private and party likes and dislikes, favorites and enemies, loyalties and grudges. Under these conditions, any choice will result either from one party's victory over the other or from a compromise between them. Either way, the candidate's real merit will too often be lost from view. In the first case, the qualities that best unite the party's votes will count more than the qualities that fit the person for the job. In the second case, the deal will usually rest on some trade of interests: "Give us the man we wish for this office, and you shall have the one you wish for that." This will be the usual condition of the bargain. And the good of the public service will rarely be the main goal, either of party victories or of party deals.

:::quote
Give us the man we wish for this office, and you shall have the one you wish for that.

Meaning: When a group makes appointments, members trade votes. Each side gets its favorite, whether or not that person is the best choice.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's picture of group appointments is based on what he had seen in New York's Council of Appointment and in the Continental Congress, where choosing officials often turned into a contest between states and factions. His argument depends on the same idea as [No. 70](/papers/70/): when one person is responsible, the public knows whom to praise or blame.

Critics might reply that the president has friends and party loyalties too. Hamilton's answer comes in the next sections: that's exactly why the Senate must approve.
:::

:::vocab
Discernment: Good judgment; the ability to see what is really true or valuable.
Suffrages: Votes.
:::

## Nominating is nearly the same as appointing {orig=6-7}

The most thoughtful critics of the Convention's plan seem to have felt the truth of these principles. They argue that the president alone should have been given the power to make appointments under the federal government. But it is easy to show that the power to nominate, which the plan gives him, provides nearly every advantage of that arrangement. At the same time, it avoids several disadvantages that might come with giving him complete power to appoint.

In nominating, only his judgment would be used. His only duty would be to name the man who, with the Senate's approval, would fill an office. So his responsibility would be as complete as if he made the final appointment himself. In this sense, there is no difference between nominating and appointing. The same motives that would lead him to do his duty well in one case would exist in the other. And since no one could be appointed without first being nominated by him, everyone appointed would in fact be his choice.

But couldn't the Senate overrule his nomination? I admit it could. Yet this could only make room for another nomination by the president himself. The person finally appointed must be someone he prefers, even if not his first choice.

It is also not very likely that his nominations would often be overruled. The Senate could not be tempted to reject a nominee just because it preferred someone else. It could never be sure that the person it wanted would be named in a second nomination, or in any later one. It could not even be sure that a future nominee would be any more acceptable. Also, a rejection might put a kind of stain on the person rejected. It might also look like a criticism of the president's judgment. So the Senate probably would not often refuse its approval unless it had special and strong reasons.

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction has mostly held true. The Senate confirms the great majority of nominees, though some are withdrawn before a vote when they seem likely to fail. Outright rejections are uncommon but not unknown. In 1795, the Senate rejected John Rutledge, whom Washington had named chief justice. In 1987, it rejected Robert Bork's nomination to the Supreme Court, and in 1989 it rejected John Tower as secretary of defense.

In recent decades, confirmation fights have become more intense, especially over judges. In 2016, the Senate did not act on President Obama's nomination of Merrick Garland to the Supreme Court. Senators have also changed their rules so that nominees can be confirmed by a simple majority instead of needing 60 votes to end debate. Democrats made that change for most nominees in 2013, and Republicans extended it to Supreme Court nominees in 2017. People disagree about whether these changes made the process fairer or more partisan.
:::

:::vocab
Nominate: To formally name someone as a candidate for an office.
Stigma: A mark of shame or disgrace.
:::

## Why the Senate's approval still matters {orig=8-9}

Why, then, require the Senate's cooperation at all? I answer that the need for its agreement would have a powerful effect, though usually a silent one. It would be an excellent check on any tendency toward favoritism in the president. It would greatly help prevent the appointment of unfit people for reasons such as:

- prejudice in favor of his own state,
- family connections,
- personal friendship,
- or a wish to gain popularity.

In addition, it would be an effective source of stability in the administration.

It is easy to see that a man who alone controlled all offices would be guided much more by his private wishes and interests. He would act differently if he had to submit his choices to be discussed and decided by a different and independent body, and that body a whole branch of the legislature. The possibility of rejection would be a strong reason to be careful in nominating.

Suppose he showed favoritism, or an improper hunger for popularity, in front of a body whose opinion would carry great weight with the public. That would endanger his reputation, and, since he is elected, his political career. This danger could not fail to act as a barrier against both faults. He would be both ashamed and afraid to put forward, for the most honored or best-paid positions, candidates whose only merit was one of these:

- they came from his own state;
- they were connected to him personally in some way;
- or they were unimportant and weak enough to become his obedient tools.

:::quote
It would be an excellent check upon a spirit of favoritism in the President, and would tend greatly to prevent the appointment of unfit characters from State prejudice, from family connection, from personal attachment, or from a view to popularity.

Meaning: Because the Senate must approve, the president will think twice before naming friends, relatives, or yes-men to important jobs.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point about the Senate's "silent operation" is similar to his point about the veto threat in [No. 73](/papers/73/). Most of the Senate's influence is never seen in a vote. Presidents choose nominees they expect can be confirmed, and they often consult senators in advance. A long-standing custom called "senatorial courtesy" gives senators extra influence over appointments, such as district judges and U.S. attorneys, in their own states.

Hamilton says the Senate's role would add "stability in the administration." He develops this idea in [No. 77](/papers/77/), where he makes a claim about removing officials that turned out to be controversial.
:::

:::vocab
Favoritism: Unfairly giving jobs or benefits to friends, relatives, or supporters.
Obsequious: Too eager to please or obey.
:::

## Could the president buy the Senate? {orig=10}

Some have objected to this reasoning. They say the president could use his power of nomination to win the Senate's obedience to his wishes. But assuming that everyone can be bought is almost as big a mistake in political reasoning as assuming that everyone is honest. The very idea of delegating power assumes that there is some virtue and honor among people, enough to be a reasonable basis for trust. Experience supports this idea. Such virtue has been found even in the most corrupt periods of the most corrupt governments.

The British House of Commons has long been accused of corruption, both in Britain and in this country. No doubt the charge is true to a large extent. But it is just as certain that a large part of that body is always made up of independent and public-spirited men who have real influence in the nation's decisions. That is why the views of the Commons are often seen to restrain the wishes of the king, about both people and policies. This is true even in the present reign.

So we might suppose that the president could sometimes influence a few senators. But the idea that he could generally buy the honesty of the whole body would be strained and unlikely. Consider a man who sees human nature as it is, without flattering its virtues or exaggerating its vices. He will find enough reason to trust the honesty of the Senate. He will be satisfied that the president could not corrupt or seduce a majority of its members. He will also be satisfied that the need for the Senate's cooperation in appointments will be a significant and healthy restraint on the president's conduct.

Nor is the Senate's honesty our only protection. The Constitution provides some important guards against the danger of the president's influence over Congress. It declares:

> "No senator or representative shall during the time for which he was elected, be appointed to any civil office under the United States, which shall have been created, or the emoluments whereof shall have been increased, during such time; and no person, holding any office under the United States, shall be a member of either house during his continuance in office."

:::quote
This supposition of universal venalty in human nature is little less an error in political reasoning, than the supposition of universal rectitude.

Meaning: It is as foolish to assume that everyone can be bribed as it is to assume that everyone is honest.
:::

:::commentary
This passage shows the founders' balanced view of human nature, the same view behind Madison's "If men were angels" in [No. 51](/papers/51/). Hamilton doesn't claim senators will be saints. He claims only that enough of them will be honest enough that the president can't buy the whole body.

The clause he quotes at the end is in Article I, Section 6. In Britain, the king's ministers sat in Parliament, and the crown could reward members with well-paid offices. The Constitution forbids anyone from serving in Congress and holding an executive office at the same time. That is why a member of Congress must resign to join the cabinet. It also bars members from taking an office that was created, or whose pay was raised, during their term.
:::

:::vocab
Venality: Willingness to be bribed or to sell one's influence. Hamilton spells it "venalty."
Rectitude: Moral uprightness; honesty.
Emolument: Salary or profit from holding an office.
:::

:::today
Senate confirmation fights, especially over Supreme Court justices, are now among the most watched events in American politics. Hamilton expected the Senate to approve most nominees and to reject them only for "special and strong reasons." Supporters of a strong Senate role say senators should judge a nominee's views and judicial philosophy, not only qualifications and character. Others argue that the Senate should generally defer to the president's choices, as Hamilton seemed to expect, and save rejection for unfit nominees. Either way, Hamilton's basic design still works: the president chooses, the Senate checks, and the public can hold both responsible.
:::
