:::summary
This is the longest essay in *The Federalist*. Hamilton answers what he calls the most successful objection to the Constitution in New York: that it fails to guarantee jury trials in civil (non-criminal) cases. He argues that saying nothing about something is not the same as banning it. Congress will be free to use juries in civil cases, and most civil cases will stay in state courts anyway. He values the civil jury, especially as a check on corruption, but he doubts it is essential to liberty in the way the criminal jury is. The real problem, he explains, is that the states use juries in very different ways, so no single national rule would have worked. Juries are also poorly suited to some cases, like disputes over ships captured at sea and complicated equity cases. The wisest choice was to leave the matter to Congress.
:::

:::keypoints
- The Constitution guarantees **jury trials in criminal cases** but says nothing about **civil cases**. Critics called this an abolition of the civil jury.
- **Silence is not abolition.** Requiring juries in criminal cases does not forbid them in civil cases. Congress may provide for them.
- Most civil cases ordinary people care about, like land disputes and quarrels between neighbors, will **stay in state courts** under state rules.
- Hamilton values civil juries, but says the jury that truly protects liberty is the **criminal jury**, along with **habeas corpus**. Both are in the Constitution.
- The strongest argument for civil juries is that they are a **check on corruption**, because a bribe would have to win over both judge and jury.
- The states use juries in **very different ways**. No single rule could fit them all, and copying one state's system would have caused jealousy.
- Juries are **poorly suited** to some cases, like **prize cases** involving foreign nations and complicated **equity** cases.
- Proposals from the Pennsylvania minority and from Massachusetts show how **hard it is to write a good rule**.
- The general **"genius" of a government** matters more than particular written guarantees. Connecticut has no written constitution at all, yet protects jury trial well.
:::

:::context Background
Jury trial was one of the most treasured rights in the English legal tradition. American colonists had protested bitterly when Britain sent some cases to admiralty courts, where judges decided without juries. The Declaration of Independence complained of the king "depriving us in many cases, of the benefits of Trial by Jury."

The Constitution, in Article III, requires jury trials for "all Crimes, except in Cases of Impeachment." But it says nothing about civil cases, which are lawsuits between private parties over things like property, contracts, and debts. Anti-Federalists seized on this silence. Some claimed the Constitution would wipe out the civil jury entirely. This is the last of Hamilton's six essays on the federal courts. He had touched on the issue in [No. 81](/papers/81/), and here he takes it on in full.
:::

## Silence is not abolition {orig=1-2}

The objection to the Convention's plan that has had the most success in New York, and perhaps in several other states, concerns the lack of a constitutional guarantee of jury trial in civil cases.

The dishonest way this objection is usually stated has been pointed out and exposed again and again. But the plan's opponents keep repeating it in all their conversations and writings. They describe the Constitution's mere *silence* about civil cases as an *abolition* of jury trial. And the speeches built on this excuse are cleverly designed to make people believe that this supposed abolition is complete and universal. They suggest it covers not only every kind of civil case but even criminal cases. Arguing about criminal cases, however, would be as pointless as seriously trying to prove that physical matter exists. It would be like trying to prove any statement that is obviously true as soon as it is put in clear words.

As for civil cases, critics have used arguments almost too silly to deserve an answer to support the idea that something which is simply *not provided for* is entirely *abolished*. Every person of good judgment can see at once the huge difference between silence and abolition. But the inventors of this false argument have tried to support it with certain legal rules of interpretation, which they have twisted from their true meaning. So it may be worth examining the ground they stand on.

:::vocab
Civil case: A lawsuit between private parties, such as a dispute over property, a contract, or a debt. It is different from a criminal case, where the government charges someone with a crime.
Jury: A group of ordinary citizens chosen to hear the evidence in a trial and decide the facts. In a criminal case, the jury decides guilt or innocence.
Disingenuous: Dishonest, especially by pretending to be sincere or by twisting the truth.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is right that the criminal jury was protected. Article III says: "The Trial of all Crimes, except in Cases of Impeachment, shall be by Jury; and such Trial shall be held in the State where the said Crimes shall have been committed." Critics who suggested otherwise were exaggerating.

But the civil jury question was a real one, and Hamilton admits that it was the opposition's most effective argument in New York. Delegates had briefly discussed the issue near the end of the Constitutional Convention, but they added no guarantee. To many voters, that seemed like a dangerous gap.
:::

## How critics twisted two legal rules {orig=3-6}

The legal rules they rely on go like this: "Listing particular things excludes general ones," or "Stating one thing excludes another." So, they say, since the Constitution sets up jury trial in criminal cases and is silent about civil ones, the silence must be a hidden ban on jury trial in civil cases.

The rules of legal interpretation are simply rules of common sense that courts have adopted for reading laws. So the true test of whether a rule is being used correctly is whether it fits with common sense, the source it came from. With that in mind, let me ask a question. Does common sense suggest that a rule *requiring* the legislature to use juries in criminal cases *takes away* its right to allow juries in other cases? Is it natural to think that a command to do one thing is a ban on doing another thing? That is especially doubtful when there was already a power to do the second thing, and it doesn't conflict with what was commanded. If that idea would be unnatural and unreasonable, then it can't be rational to argue that requiring juries in some cases forbids them in others.

A power to create courts includes a power to decide how trials will be held. So if the Constitution said nothing at all about juries, Congress would be free either to use them or not. For criminal cases, that freedom is limited by the clear command to use juries in all of them. But for civil cases, Congress's freedom is left untouched, because the Constitution says nothing about them. Requiring one method of trial in all criminal cases does remove any *obligation* to use the same method in civil cases. But it doesn't reduce the legislature's *power* to use that method if it thinks it wise. So the claim that Congress wouldn't be fully free to send all federal civil cases to juries has no real basis.

From all this, we can conclude that jury trial in civil cases would not be abolished. And the way critics have tried to use these legal rules goes against reason and common sense, so it can't be accepted. Even if the rules had the exact technical meaning the critics claim, which they don't, they still wouldn't apply to a constitution. For a constitution, the true guide to its meaning is the natural and obvious sense of its words, apart from any technical rules.

:::vocab
Maxim: A short statement of a general rule or truth.
Construction: In law, the act of interpreting what a text means. To *construe* a text is to interpret it.
:::

:::commentary
The legal rule Hamilton is discussing is still used today. It is often known by its Latin name, *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, meaning "the expression of one thing is the exclusion of another." Courts use it, carefully, when interpreting laws. Hamilton doesn't reject the rule. He argues that it only makes sense in certain situations, as he explains next.

His point that a constitution should be read by "the natural and obvious sense of its provisions" is also important. It is an early statement of a view that many judges still hold: the Constitution was written for ordinary people, so its words should be understood the way an ordinary reader would understand them.
:::

## When the rule does apply {orig=7-10}

Now that we've seen that the critics' rules won't support the use they make of them, let's figure out their proper use and true meaning. Examples will show this best.

The Convention's plan says that the power of Congress, the national legislature, will extend to certain listed subjects. This list clearly rules out any claim to a general power to make laws on everything. Granting specific powers would be absurd and useless if a general power was intended.

In the same way, the Constitution says that the authority of the federal courts covers certain specific kinds of cases. Listing those cases marks the exact limits beyond which the federal courts can't go. Since the cases they can hear are listed, the list would be meaningless if it didn't rule out any broader authority.

These examples are enough to explain the rules and show how they should be used. But to avoid any misunderstanding, I'll add one more case. It will show both the proper use of these rules and the way they have been abused.

Suppose that under New York law, a married woman could not transfer her property. Suppose the legislature, considering this a bad thing, passed a law saying she could transfer her property by a deed signed in the presence of a magistrate. In that case, the specific method would clearly exclude any other method of transfer. The woman had no power to transfer her property before, so the law sets the particular method she must use to do it.

But now suppose that a later part of the same law said that no woman could transfer property worth more than a certain amount without the consent of three of her nearest relatives, who would show their consent by signing the deed. Could anyone conclude from this rule that a married woman couldn't get her relatives' approval for a deed to transfer property of *lesser* value? The idea is too absurd to deserve an answer. Yet that is exactly the idea that people must prove if they claim that civil jury trial is abolished just because the Constitution requires juries in criminal cases.

:::vocab
Deed: A signed legal document that transfers ownership of property, especially land.
Magistrate: A government official, especially a local judge.
Enumerated powers: The specific powers the Constitution lists for Congress, mostly in Article I, Section 8.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's first two examples show when the rule makes sense. Congress has a *list* of powers, so it can't claim powers that aren't on the list. That is the basic idea of enumerated powers, which Madison also explained in [No. 45](/papers/45/).

His third example reflects the law of his time. Under a legal rule called **coverture**, a married woman in 1788 generally could not own property, make contracts, or bring lawsuits in her own name. Her legal identity was mostly absorbed into her husband's. Hamilton uses this as an ordinary example and doesn't question it. New York passed a law in 1848 letting married women keep their own property, and other states made similar changes over the following decades.
:::

## Most jury trials won't be affected at all {orig=11}

These points prove beyond question that the proposed Constitution doesn't abolish jury trial in any case. It is equally true that, in the disputes between individuals that most people care about, jury trial will stay exactly as the state constitutions now set it up. Adopting the plan will not change or affect it at all.

The reason is that the national courts will have no authority over those cases. They will continue to be decided only by the state courts, in the way the state constitutions and laws require. All land cases, except those involving claims under grants from different states, will belong exclusively to the state courts. So will all other disputes between citizens of the same state, unless they arise from a state legislature directly violating the Constitution.

Add to this that, under our own New York government, admiralty cases and almost all equity cases are decided without a jury. Taken all together, this shows that the jury system as it now exists in New York can't possibly be affected to any great extent by the proposed change in our government.

:::vocab
Admiralty: The branch of law dealing with ships, sailors, and events at sea, such as shipwrecks, cargo disputes, and ships captured in war. Also called **maritime** law.
Equity: A kind of justice, separate from ordinary law, that lets judges do what is fair when strict legal rules would lead to an unfair result. Equity cases were decided by a judge without a jury.
:::

:::commentary
This was one of Hamilton's strongest points, and it held true. Most legal disputes in America, then and now, are handled by state courts. The federal courts hear only the special kinds of cases listed in Article III, which Hamilton explained in [No. 80](/papers/80/). Even today, the great majority of trials in the United States take place in state courts, under state rules about juries.
:::

## Is the civil jury essential to liberty? {orig=12-16}

Friends and opponents of the Convention's plan may agree on nothing else, but they at least agree on the value of jury trial. If there is any difference between them, it is this: friends of the plan see it as a valuable safeguard of liberty, while opponents call it the very *palladium*, or sacred shield, of free government.

For my part, the more I have seen of how juries work, the more reason I have found to value them highly. There's no need to examine how useful or essential they are in a representative republic. Nor is there any need to ask whether they are more valuable as a defense against a hereditary king than as a barrier against the tyranny of elected officials in a popular government. Such discussions would be more interesting than useful, since everyone is convinced that juries are useful and friendly to liberty.

But I must admit that I can't easily see an unbreakable connection between liberty and jury trial in *civil* cases. To me, the great tools of judicial tyranny have always been these: unjust impeachments, unjust ways of prosecuting made-up crimes, and unjust punishments after unjust convictions. All of these involve *criminal* proceedings. So the question really concerns only jury trial in criminal cases, together with the right of habeas corpus. And both of these are fully provided for in the Convention's plan.

Some have said that jury trial is a safeguard against the abuse of the power to tax. This claim deserves a close look.

Clearly, juries can have no influence on the legislature's decisions about how much to tax, what to tax, or how to divide taxes among the states. So if they have any influence, it must be on how taxes are collected and how tax officers behave.

As for collection, in New York under our own constitution, jury trial is mostly not used. Taxes are usually collected through the quicker process of *distress and sale*, as in cases of unpaid rent. Everyone admits that this is essential for tax laws to work. A slow court trial to collect each person's taxes wouldn't meet the public's needs or suit the convenience of citizens. It would often run up costs heavier than the original tax.

And as for the behavior of tax officers, the guarantee of jury trial in criminal cases will provide the protection needed. Willful abuse of public power to oppress citizens, and every kind of extortion by officials, are crimes against the government. The officers who commit them can be indicted and punished according to the circumstances of the case.

:::quote
The friends and adversaries of the plan of the convention, if they agree in nothing else, concur at least in the value they set upon the trial by jury; or if there is any difference between them it consists in this: the former regard it as a valuable safeguard to liberty; the latter represent it as the very palladium of free government.

Meaning: Both sides love jury trials. The only difference is that opponents of the Constitution speak of them with even more reverence.
:::

:::vocab
Palladium: A safeguard or protection. The word comes from the Palladium, a statue of the goddess Pallas Athena that was believed to protect the ancient city of Troy.
Writ of habeas corpus: A court order requiring the government to bring a prisoner before a judge and prove that the imprisonment is legal. Latin for "you shall have the body."
Distress and sale: Seizing a person's property and selling it to pay a debt, such as unpaid rent or taxes, without a full trial.
Extortion: Using one's power or threats to force someone to hand over money or property.
Indict: To formally charge someone with a crime.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton draws a sharp line between criminal and civil juries. In a criminal case, the government itself is trying to punish someone, so a jury of ordinary citizens stands between the accused and the power of the state. This is why he treats the criminal jury, along with habeas corpus, as the real protection of liberty.

Anti-Federalists disagreed. They pointed out that the government could also be a party in civil cases, such as suits to collect taxes or debts. They also argued that juries let ordinary people take part in government and keep the law in touch with community values. Many historians note that colonial juries had sometimes refused to enforce British laws that Americans considered unjust. That is why critics saw the civil jury as a shield against tyranny too, not just a way of settling private disputes.
:::

## A check on corruption {orig=17-18}

The value of jury trial in civil cases seems to depend on reasons that have little to do with protecting liberty. The strongest argument for it is that it guards against corruption. There is always more time and opportunity to tamper with a permanent body of judges than with a jury called together for one case. So we can suppose that corrupt influence would reach judges more easily than juries.

Other points weaken this argument, however. The sheriff, who summons ordinary juries, and the court clerks, who choose special juries, are themselves permanent officials. Acting alone, they may be easier to corrupt than the judges, who act as a group. It's easy to see that these officials could choose jurors who would serve a corrupt party just as well as bribed judges would. Also, it's fair to think it would be easier to win over some jurors taken at random from the general public than to win over men the government chose for their honesty and good character.

But even after allowing for all this, jury trial must still be a valuable check on corruption. It greatly multiplies the obstacles to success. As things now stand, a person would have to corrupt both the court and the jury. Where a jury has clearly gone wrong, the court will generally grant a new trial. So in most cases it would do little good to tamper with the jury unless the court could also be won over. Here, then, is a double security. And we can easily see that this combined system tends to keep both institutions honest. By making success harder, it discourages attempts to corrupt either one. Judges will certainly face far fewer temptations to sell their decisions while they need a jury's cooperation than they would if they alone decided every case.

So despite my doubts about whether civil juries are essential to liberty, I admit that in most cases, with proper rules, they are an excellent way to decide questions of property. For this reason alone, they would deserve a guarantee in the Constitution, *if* it were possible to fix the limits of the cases they should cover. But that is always very difficult. And anyone not blinded by enthusiasm must see that the difficulty grows much greater in a federal government made up of societies whose ideas and institutions on this subject differ a great deal. For my part, every time I look at the subject again, I become more convinced of how real the obstacles were. We have been told on good authority that these obstacles are what kept the Convention from including such a provision.

:::vocab
Sheriff: A local official responsible for keeping the peace and carrying out court orders, including summoning people to serve on juries.
Special jury: A jury chosen by a special process, often from a list of better-educated or wealthier citizens, for complicated or important cases.
Probity: Honesty and strong moral character.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's "double security" argument echoes Madison's idea in [No. 51](/papers/51/) that dividing power makes abuse harder. To corrupt a trial, you would need to corrupt two separate groups, judge and jury.

When Hamilton says "we are authoritatively informed" about the Convention's reasons, he probably means public statements by delegates. For example, James Wilson of Pennsylvania, who had helped write the Constitution, explained in a well-known speech in October 1787 that the states' practices were too different for the Convention to agree on a single rule for civil juries.
:::

## How differently the states use juries {orig=19-20}

People don't generally understand how widely the use of jury trial differs from state to state. Since this should strongly affect our judgment of the omission people complain about, I need to explain it.

- **New York.** Our court system is more like Great Britain's than any other state's. We have courts of common law, probate courts (similar in some ways to England's church courts), an admiralty court, and a court of chancery. Jury trial is used only in the common-law courts, and even there with some exceptions. In all the others, a single judge presides and generally follows the methods of church law or civil law, without a jury.
- **New Jersey** has a court of chancery that works like ours, but it has no admiralty or probate courts in our sense. Its common-law courts hear cases that we send to admiralty and probate courts. So jury trial covers more cases in New Jersey than in New York.
- **Pennsylvania** goes perhaps even further. It has no court of chancery, and its common-law courts handle equity cases. It has an admiralty court, but no probate courts like ours. **Delaware** has copied Pennsylvania in these respects.
- **Maryland** is closer to New York. So is **Virginia**, except that Virginia has several chancellors instead of one. **North Carolina** is most like Pennsylvania, and **South Carolina** most like Virginia. I believe, however, that in some of the states with separate admiralty courts, cases in those courts can be tried by juries.
- **Georgia** has only common-law courts. An appeal naturally goes from the verdict of one jury to another, called a *special jury*, which is chosen in a particular way.
- **Connecticut** has no separate courts of chancery or admiralty, and its probate courts don't decide disputes. Its common-law courts handle admiralty cases and, to some extent, equity cases. In important cases, its General Assembly is the only court of equity. So in Connecticut, jury trial reaches further in practice than in any other state I have mentioned. **Rhode Island**, I believe, is in much the same situation. So are **Massachusetts** and **New Hampshire**, in the way they blend law, equity, and admiralty powers.
- In these **four New England states**, jury trial not only rests on a broader foundation than in the other states. It also has a special feature that no other state has to the same degree. A party can appeal from one jury to another as a matter of course, until there have been two verdicts out of three on the same side.

This sketch shows that jury trial in civil cases differs a great deal from state to state, both in how it works and in how far it reaches. Two obvious conclusions follow. First, the Convention could not have set any general rule that would have fit the circumstances of all the states. Second, using any one state's system as the standard would have been just as risky as leaving out a provision altogether and leaving the matter to Congress, as the Convention did. It might even have been riskier.

:::note
In a footnote, Hamilton corrects a claim that New York's court of chancery generally used juries to decide disputed facts. In truth, he says, that court rarely sends questions to a jury, and it is required to do so only when the validity of a will leaving land is in question.
:::

:::vocab
Probate: The legal process of handling a dead person's will and property.
Chancery: A court of equity, led by a judge called the chancellor.
Common law: Law built up over centuries through court decisions, rather than passed by a legislature. American law inherited it from England.
Canon law: Church law. In England, church courts used it to handle matters like marriages and wills.
Verdict: A jury's decision in a trial.
:::

:::commentary
This survey is the heart of Hamilton's case. He wants readers to see that "jury trial in civil cases" didn't mean one thing in 1788. It meant thirteen different things. A New Yorker might picture a case that would never go to a jury in New York but would always go to one in Connecticut. Any rule written for the whole country would have changed the practice of many states.

Notice also how much New England relied on juries. A losing party there could get a second jury, and sometimes a third. This shows how deeply the jury was woven into local self-government in that region.
:::

## The Pennsylvania proposal, and cases unfit for juries {orig=21-24}

The proposals made to fill this gap have shown how hard the task is more than they have solved it. The minority in Pennsylvania's ratifying convention proposed this wording: "Trial by jury shall be as heretofore." I maintain this would be meaningless and useless.

All general provisions in the Constitution must be read as referring to the United States as a whole. Jury trial, with various limits, is known in each state individually. But in the United States as such, it is completely unknown right now, because the present federal government has no judicial power at all. So there is no earlier practice that the word "heretofore" ("as before") could refer to. The provision would have no precise meaning and would do nothing because of its uncertainty.

On the one hand, the wording wouldn't achieve what its authors intended. On the other hand, if I understand their intent correctly, it would be unwise in itself. I assume they meant that cases in federal courts should be tried by jury if a similar case would be tried by jury in the state courts where the federal court sat. That would mean, for example, that admiralty cases would be tried by a jury in Connecticut but without one in New York. Having such different methods of trial for the same cases, under the same government, would be so erratic that any sound judgment would turn against it. Whether a case was tried with or without a jury would often depend on the accident of where the court and the parties happened to be.

But in my view, this isn't the greatest objection. I am deeply and carefully convinced that in many cases jury trial is not a good choice. I think this especially about cases that affect the public peace with foreign nations. That includes most cases that turn entirely on the law of nations, and among them, all prize cases.

Juries can't be expected to be competent at investigations that require a thorough knowledge of the laws and customs of nations. And they will sometimes be swayed by feelings that keep them from paying enough attention to the public policy concerns that should guide their decisions. So there would always be a danger that their verdicts would violate other nations' rights, giving those nations reasons for revenge and war. It's true that the proper job of juries is to decide facts. But in most cases, the legal results are so tangled up with the facts that separating them is impossible.

It adds great weight to this point to mention that various treaties among European powers have set special rules for deciding prize cases. Under those treaties, Great Britain decides them, in the last resort, before the king himself in his Privy Council, where the facts as well as the law are reexamined. This alone shows how unwise it would be to put a basic rule in the Constitution making the state systems the standard for the national government on this subject. It also shows the danger of loading the government down with any constitutional provisions that aren't clearly proper.

:::vocab
Heretofore: Until now; before this time.
Prize cases: Court cases deciding whether a ship and its cargo, captured during war, were lawfully taken. The winner could keep or sell the "prize."
Law of nations: The rules that govern how countries treat each other and each other's citizens. Today we call it *international law*.
Privy Council: A group of high officials who advise the British monarch. It also served as a final court of appeal for some kinds of cases.
Reprisal: An act of revenge, especially by one nation against another.
:::

:::commentary
The "minority of Pennsylvania" were the Anti-Federalist delegates who lost the vote at Pennsylvania's ratifying convention in December 1787. Afterward, they published an influential statement of their objections, which included a list of proposed amendments.

Hamilton's worry about prize cases had a real basis. In a famous case from 1778, Connecticut sailors led by Gideon Olmstead claimed a British ship they had seized, the *Active*. But a Pennsylvania jury decided largely in favor of Pennsylvania's side. A committee of Congress that heard prize appeals reversed the verdict, but Pennsylvania refused to obey. The fight was not settled until 1809. If local juries could cause that much trouble between American states, they could cause even more trouble with foreign nations, whose ships and sailors would be at the mercy of local feelings.
:::

## Why equity should stay separate {orig=25-27}

I am just as strongly convinced that keeping equity separate from ordinary law brings great advantages, and that equity cases would be wrongly given to juries.

The main purpose of a court of equity is to give relief in unusual cases that are exceptions to general rules. Combining these cases with ordinary cases would tend to unsettle the general rules and make every case that comes up a special decision. Keeping the two separate has the opposite effect. Each one guards the other and keeps it within proper limits.

Besides, the circumstances that make a case right for a court of equity are often so delicate and complicated that they don't fit the nature of jury trial. They often require long, careful, and close investigation. That would be impractical for people called away from their jobs and forced to decide before they could go back to work. Jury trial is known for being simple and quick. That requires the question to be narrowed to a single, clear point. But cases in chancery often involve a long series of small, separate details.

It's true that separating equity from law is special to the English legal system, which several states have followed. But it is equally true that jury trial has been unknown in every system where they were combined. And separation is essential to keeping the jury system in its original purity. A court of equity can easily extend its power to matters of law. But there is good reason to suspect that trying to extend the power of courts of law to matters of equity would fail to provide the benefits of courts of chancery as New York has set them up. Worse, it would gradually change the nature of the courts of law. It would undermine jury trial by bringing in questions too complicated for juries to decide.

These seemed to be conclusive reasons against combining the systems of all the states when forming the national courts, which is what the Pennsylvania minority may have been trying to do. Now let's see how well the Massachusetts proposal would fix the supposed defect.

:::note
In a footnote, Hamilton admits that the principles courts of equity use had, by his time, become "a regular system." But they still apply mainly to special circumstances that are exceptions to the general rules.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction here did not hold up over time. New York combined law and equity in the mid-1800s, and federal courts did the same in 1938. Today a single federal judge can handle both kinds of claims in one lawsuit. Jury trial survived the merger. The Supreme Court decides whether a jury is required by asking whether a claim is similar to the kind of case that would have been tried at common law, rather than in equity, back in 1791.

Hamilton was right, though, that complicated cases strain the jury system. Today, lengthy trials about technical subjects, such as patents or complex financial schemes, raise the same concern he raised: can ordinary citizens, pulled away from their jobs, fairly decide them? Supporters of juries reply that ordinary people are capable of understanding complicated evidence when it is explained well.
:::

## The Massachusetts proposal and other ideas {orig=28-34}

The Massachusetts proposal reads this way: "In civil actions between citizens of different States, every issue of fact, arising in actions at common law, may be tried by a jury if the parties, or either of them request it."

At best, this proposal covers only one kind of case. We can fairly conclude one of two things. Either the Massachusetts convention thought this was the only kind of federal case where jury trial would be proper, or it wanted a broader provision but found it impossible to write one that would work. If the first is true, leaving out a rule about such a narrow subject can never be considered a serious flaw in the system. If the second is true, it strongly confirms how extremely hard the task is.

And that's not all. Recall what I said earlier about the different courts in the states and the different powers they use. It will then be clear that no words could be more vague and uncertain than the ones used to describe which cases would get a jury. In New York, the boundaries between actions at common law and actions in equity follow the rules used in England. In many other states, the boundaries are less exact. In some of them, every case is tried in a court of common law. On that basis, every lawsuit there might be considered an action at common law, to be decided by a jury if either party wanted one.

So following this proposal would bring in the same irregularity and confusion that I pointed out in the Pennsylvania minority's proposal. In one state, a case would be decided by a jury if either party asked for it. In another state, an identical case would have to be decided without a jury, because the state courts drew the boundaries of the common law differently.

Clearly, then, the Massachusetts proposal can't work as a general rule until the states adopt some uniform plan for the boundaries between common law and equity. Designing such a plan would be a hard task in itself, and it would take a lot of time and thought to develop. It would be extremely difficult, if not impossible, to suggest any general rule that all the states would accept, or that would fit perfectly with all their institutions.

Someone may ask: why not just point to New York's constitution, which I agree is a good one, and make it the standard for the United States? My answer is that the other states probably don't think as highly of our institutions as we do. It's natural to assume they are more attached to their own, and that each would fight for its own system. If the Convention had thought of taking one state as a model for all, each state's delegation would likely have favored its own government, making agreement difficult. And it's uncertain which state would have been chosen as the model. We've seen that many of them would be poor models. I'll leave it to you to guess whether New York or some other state would most likely have been chosen. But even if the Convention could have made a wise choice, the other states would likely have felt jealous and resentful at the favoritism shown to one state's institutions. The plan's enemies would have had a fine excuse to stir up a host of local prejudices against it. That might have seriously endangered its final adoption.

To avoid the trouble of defining which cases jury trial should cover, some overly enthusiastic people suggest that the Constitution could have required it in all cases whatsoever. I believe no state in the Union provides an example of this. And the points I raised about the Pennsylvania minority's proposal should convince every sober mind that requiring jury trial in all cases would have been an unforgivable mistake in the plan.

In short, the more we think about it, the harder it seems to write a provision that doesn't say too little to serve its purpose or too much to be wise. And any such provision might have created new sources of opposition to the great and essential goal of setting up a firm national government.

:::commentary
Massachusetts ratified the Constitution in February 1788 by a close vote. It did so only after Federalists agreed to recommend a list of amendments for Congress to consider later. Several other states followed this approach, which helped lead to the Bill of Rights.

Hamilton's point about choosing one state as a model is also shrewd politics. He suggests that New York would probably *not* have been chosen, so New Yorkers should be glad no state's system was imposed on the others.
:::

## Why it's best to leave it to Congress {orig=35-38}

On the other hand, I am persuaded that the different angles from which I have examined this subject will go a long way toward removing the fears of fair-minded readers. They have shown the following:

- The security of liberty depends in a serious way only on jury trial in *criminal* cases, which the Convention's plan provides for in the fullest way.
- In by far the largest share of civil cases, including those that matter to most of the community, jury trial will stay in full force, as the state constitutions set it up. The Convention's plan doesn't touch or affect it.
- The plan doesn't abolish jury trial in any case.
- There are great, if not impossible, difficulties in the way of writing any exact and proper provision for it in a Constitution for the United States.

The best judges of the matter will be the least anxious to have civil jury trial written into the Constitution. They will be the readiest to admit that the constant changes in society may make other ways of deciding property questions better in many cases where juries are now used. For my part, I am convinced that even in New York, jury trial could usefully be extended to some cases where it isn't used now, and just as usefully cut back in others. All reasonable people agree that it shouldn't be used in every case. In these states as well as in Great Britain, changes have narrowed its old limits. That strongly suggests its former reach was found to be inconvenient, and that future experience may show the wisdom and usefulness of other exceptions. I suspect it is impossible, by the nature of the thing, to fix the exact point where jury trial should stop. To me, that is a strong argument for leaving the matter to the judgment of the legislature.

This is now clearly understood to be the case in Great Britain, and it is the same in Connecticut. Yet we can safely say that New York has made more attacks on jury trial since the Revolution, even though our constitution expressly protects it, than either Connecticut or Great Britain has made in the same time. I might add that these attacks have generally come from men who try to convince the people that they are the warmest defenders of popular liberty, but who have rarely let constitutional limits stop them from pursuing a favorite goal.

The truth is that the general CHARACTER, or "genius," of a government is the only thing we can really rely on for lasting results. Particular written provisions are not completely useless, but they have far less power and effect than people usually think. To people of sound judgment, the lack of them will never be a decisive objection to any plan that shows the main features of good government.

It certainly sounds harsh and strange to claim that a Constitution gives no security for liberty because it guarantees jury trial in criminal cases but not in civil ones. That's especially true when everyone knows that Connecticut, which has always been considered the most democratic state in the Union, has no constitutional provision for either.

:::quote
The truth is that the general GENIUS of a government is all that can be substantially relied upon for permanent effects.

Meaning: What really protects liberty is the overall character and spirit of a government, not particular promises written into it.
:::

:::vocab
Genius (of a government): Its basic character or spirit. In the 1700s, "genius" often meant the essential nature of something, not great intelligence.
Popular: In 1788, often meant "controlled by the people" or "democratic," not "well liked."
:::

Footnote 3 sends readers back to [No. 81](/papers/81/), where Hamilton answered the claim that the Supreme Court's power to review "fact" on appeal would abolish jury trial.

:::commentary
Hamilton lost this argument, at least in part. Many Americans were not satisfied with "leave it to Congress." When the first Congress drafted the Bill of Rights, it included the **7th Amendment** (ratified 1791): "In Suits at common law, where the value in controversy shall exceed twenty dollars, the right of trial by jury shall be preserved, and no fact tried by a jury, shall be otherwise re-examined in any Court of the United States, than according to the rules of the common law." It guarantees civil juries in federal cases worth more than $20, and it protects jury verdicts from being second-guessed on appeal.

Hamilton's point about Connecticut was accurate. Connecticut had no written constitution at the time. It was still governed under its colonial charter from 1662, and it did not adopt a constitution until 1818. His argument that a government's "genius" matters more than written guarantees is a theme he develops further in [No. 84](/papers/84/), where he argues against adding a bill of rights at all.

There is also a blind spot here. In 1788, juries were made up almost entirely of white men, usually property owners. The Supreme Court struck down state laws barring Black men from juries in 1880, but discrimination in jury selection continued long after. Women were not allowed to serve on juries in many states until the 1900s. The jury Hamilton and his critics praised as the voice of the community left out much of the community.
:::

:::connection
Hamilton first answered the claim that the Supreme Court could overturn jury verdicts in [No. 81](/papers/81/). His next essay, [No. 84](/papers/84/), takes on the larger complaint that the Constitution lacks a bill of rights. For his explanation of which cases federal courts hear, and of the difference between law and equity, see [No. 80](/papers/80/).
:::

:::today
The 7th Amendment still guarantees civil juries in federal court, and its $20 threshold has never been changed, though $20 is now a tiny sum. It has never been applied to the states, so each state still sets its own rules for civil juries, just as Hamilton said it would. Yet civil jury trials have become rare. Most lawsuits end in settlements, and many contracts now require disputes to go to private arbitration instead of court. Supporters of civil juries see this as a loss of a basic right that lets ordinary citizens check the powerful. Others argue, much like Hamilton, that juries are not always the best way to decide complicated disputes. The question of exactly when the 7th Amendment requires a jury still reaches the Supreme Court. In *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), for example, the Court ruled that a person facing civil penalties for securities fraud had a right to a jury trial.
:::
