:::summary
Madison answers the last charge against the House of Representatives: that Congress will refuse to add members as the population grows. The Constitution requires a new census every ten years, and the large states, which control the House, will have every reason to push for more seats. If the Senate resists, the House has a decisive tool: it alone can start the bills that raise the money the government needs. This "power over the purse," Madison says, is "the most complete and effectual weapon" the people's representatives can have. He then warns that a bigger assembly isn't always better, because the larger it gets, the more a few skilled leaders control it. Finally, he defends the rule that a simple majority, not a supermajority, should be enough to do business and pass laws.
:::

:::keypoints
- The last charge: the House **won't grow** as the population grows.
- The first House size is **temporary**, a census is required **every ten years**, and seats must be **readjusted** each time. This is as much protection as any state constitution gives.
- The **large states** dominate the House and will push for more seats. Just four of them will hold a majority.
- The **Senate** might resist, but the House speaks for most of the people, has right on its side, and can win over some senators, especially from fast-growing new states.
- The House alone can propose the bills that **fund the government**. This "power over the purse" is "the most complete and effectual weapon" of the people's representatives.
- Beyond a certain size, a bigger assembly is **easier for a few to control**: "The countenance of the government may become more democratic, but the soul that animates it will be more oligarchic."
- Requiring **more than a majority** to do business or pass laws would let the minority rule and encourage members to walk out to block action.
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of four essays (Nos. 55–58) defending the size of the House of Representatives. The Constitution set the first House at 65 members, with no more than one member for every 30,000 people. Anti-Federalists worried that the House would stay small, leaving it out of touch with the people and easy for a wealthy few to control. They suspected that Congress might simply refuse to add seats as the country grew.

Madison answers this fear, and in doing so he explains one of the most important powers in the Constitution: the House's control over money.
:::

## The last charge: will the House grow? {orig=1-2}

The last charge against the House of Representatives that I will examine assumes that its number of members won't be increased from time to time, as the growth of population may require.

It has been admitted that this objection, if well supported, would carry great weight. The following points will show that, like most other objections against the Constitution, it can only come from looking at part of the subject, or from a suspicion that discolors and distorts everything it sees.

:::vocab
Augmentation: An increase.
:::

## The Constitution requires growth {orig=3-5}

**1.** Those who make this objection seem to have forgotten something. When it comes to providing for a gradual increase in the number of representatives, the federal Constitution compares well with the state constitutions. The number of members at the start is declared to be temporary. It is limited to a short term of three years.

After that, a census of the inhabitants must be taken every ten years. These rules have two clear purposes. The first is to readjust the number of representatives each state gets to match its number of inhabitants, with the one exception that every state will have at least one representative. The second is to increase the number of representatives at the same times, with the one limit that there can't be more than one for every thirty thousand inhabitants.

If we look at the state constitutions, we will find that some have no definite rules on this subject. Others are much the same as the federal Constitution on this point. And the strongest protection in any of them comes down to a mere instruction, with nothing to enforce it.

**2.** As far as we have experience on this subject, the number of representatives under the state constitutions has at least kept pace with the growth of the population. And the representatives seem to have been as willing to agree to these increases as the people have been to call for them.

:::commentary
Madison is describing Article I, Section 2 of the Constitution. It listed how many representatives each state would get at first (65 in all) until a census was taken "within three years" after Congress first met. After that, a census would be taken every ten years.

Notice what the Constitution does and doesn't say. It sets a *maximum* (one representative per 30,000 people), but no *minimum* beyond one per state. It doesn't actually require Congress to add seats. That is why Madison has to argue that Congress will *want* to.
:::

:::vocab
Directory provision: A rule that tells officials what they should do but has no way to force them to do it.
:::

## The large states will push for growth {orig=6}

**3.** A special feature of the federal Constitution ensures that most of the people, and most of their representatives, will keep a watchful eye on the proper growth of the House. One branch of Congress represents citizens, and the other represents the states. So in the House, the larger states will have the most weight. In the Senate, the advantage will go to the smaller states.

From this we can conclude with certainty that the larger states will be strong supporters of increasing the size and weight of the branch where their influence is greatest. And it happens that just four of the largest states will have a majority of all the votes in the House. So if the representatives or people of the smaller states ever oppose a reasonable increase in members, a coalition of a very few states will be enough to overrule them. In ordinary times, rivalries and local prejudices might keep such a coalition from forming. But it would certainly form when it was not only driven by shared interest, but also justified by fairness and the principles of the Constitution.

:::commentary
Madison's argument rests on self-interest, as so often in the Federalist. He doesn't ask us to trust Congress to do the right thing. He points out that the big states will *gain* from a larger House, so they will fight for it. This is the same thinking as "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" in [No. 51](/papers/51/).

The split between a House based on population and a Senate with equal votes for each state came from the Great Compromise at the Constitutional Convention. See [No. 62](/papers/62/) for Madison's discussion of the Senate.
:::

## Won't the Senate block it? {orig=7-11}

Someone may argue that the Senate, driven by similar motives, would form an opposing coalition. Since the Senate's agreement would be needed, it could defeat the House's fair and constitutional goals. This is probably the difficulty that has most alarmed those who strongly favor a large House. Fortunately, it is one of those difficulties that exist only on the surface and vanish when examined closely. I believe the following points will be accepted as settling the matter.

The two houses will have equal power on all lawmaking subjects, except that only the House can start money bills. Even so, there's no doubt that the House will have a real advantage in any contest that depends on which side holds firm longer. It has more members. It will be backed by the more powerful states. And it will be speaking for the known and settled will of a majority of the people.

This advantage will be increased by what each side knows about its own position. One side will know it is supported by right, by reason, and by the Constitution. The other will know it is fighting against all these serious considerations.

We should also consider that the states range in size from the smallest to the largest. Several states in the middle will usually side with the smaller ones. But they are too close in size and population to the larger states to support an effort to deny the larger states' fair and lawful claims. So it is far from certain that a majority of votes, even in the Senate, would oppose proper increases in the number of representatives.

It isn't looking too far ahead to add that senators from all the new states can be won over to the House's fair goals by an obvious method. For a long time, the new states will grow in population especially fast. So they will want frequent readjustments of the number of representatives to match their population. The large states, which will control the House, need only make readjustments and increases depend on each other. Then the senators from all the fastest-growing states will have to fight for increases, because their states will want the readjustments.

:::commentary
Madison is predicting how future political deals will work. New Western states, he says, will grow fast and want their House seats updated often. The large states can offer a package deal: "We'll update your seats only if the House also gets bigger." Senators from the growing states would then have a reason to vote for a larger House.

The House did grow with almost every census during the 1800s. But Madison's confidence was not entirely borne out. After the 1920 census, Congress failed to pass any reapportionment at all, partly because members from rural areas did not want to lose power to the fast-growing cities. In 1929, Congress kept the House at 435 members and made reapportionment automatic after each census. Apart from a brief increase after Alaska and Hawaii became states, the House has stayed at 435 ever since.
:::

## The power of the purse {orig=12-13}

These points seem to give ample security on this subject, and should be enough by themselves to settle all the doubts and fears people have had about it. But suppose they all failed to overcome an unjust policy by the smaller states, or their dominant influence in the Senate. The larger states would still have a constitutional and unfailing tool that will always let them achieve their fair goals.

The House of Representatives can not only refuse the funds the government needs to operate. It alone can *propose* them. In a word, the House holds the purse. That is the powerful instrument we see in the history of the British constitution. With it, a weak, young representation of the people gradually expanded its activity and importance. In the end, it cut back, as far as it seems to have wanted, all the overgrown powers of the other branches of government. In fact, this power over the purse may be seen as the most complete and effective weapon that any constitution can give the people's direct representatives. With it, they can get every grievance corrected and put every fair and useful measure into effect.

But won't the House care as much as the Senate about keeping the government running properly? And so won't it be unwilling to risk the government's existence or reputation on whether the Senate gives in? Or, if such a test of strength between the two branches were tried, wouldn't either one be just as likely to give in first?

These questions won't trouble anyone who thinks about this: the fewer the people in power, and the longer-lasting and more visible their positions, the more strongly each of them will personally care about whatever concerns the government. Those who represent their country's dignity in the eyes of other nations will be especially aware of any sign of public danger, or of a shameful standstill in public business.

This is why the British House of Commons has always won out over the other branches of government whenever it has used the tool of a money bill. The other branches have never been expected to stand absolutely firm, and they never have, even though doing so would have thrown every part of the government into confusion. The greatest firmness the federal Senate or President can show will be no more than enough to resist a House that is backed by constitutional and patriotic principles.

:::quote
This power over the purse may, in fact, be regarded as the most complete and effectual weapon with which any constitution can arm the immediate representatives of the people, for obtaining a redress of every grievance, and for carrying into effect every just and salutary measure.

Meaning: Controlling money is the strongest tool the people's elected representatives have. If the government can't spend without their approval, they can force it to fix problems and adopt good policies. "Redress of grievances" means correcting wrongs; "salutary" means healthy or beneficial.
:::

:::vocab
The power of the purse: Control over taxing and spending. In the U.S., it belongs to Congress.
Money bill: A bill that raises money through taxes. The Constitution says these must start in the House.
Supplies: In 1787, the money a legislature grants to pay for the government's operations.
:::

:::commentary
The Constitution says that "All Bills for raising Revenue shall originate in the House of Representatives," though the Senate can amend them (Article I, Section 7). By long tradition, bills that spend money also start in the House. Madison points to English history, where Parliament's control of taxes gradually gave it power over the king. The idea that representatives of the people should control taxes was also at the heart of the American Revolution's protest of "no taxation without representation."

Madison's argument about who gives in first is interesting. He predicts that senators and the president, who are fewer and more visible, will feel more personally responsible if the government stops working, so they'll give in before the House does. Modern budget fights, including government shutdowns, test this idea. Sometimes one side gives in, sometimes the other, and observers disagree about who bears the blame.
:::

## Why a bigger House isn't always better {orig=14}

In this review of how the House is set up, I have skipped over the question of cost. In the present state of affairs, cost might reasonably have helped keep the temporary number of representatives small. If the Convention had ignored it, that would probably have been as rich a topic for attacks on the Constitution as the small number of members has been. I also won't comment on how hard it might be, under present conditions, to get a large number of the kind of people the voters will probably elect to serve in the national government.

But I must add one observation that I believe deserves very serious attention. In every legislature, the more members it has, the fewer the people who actually run it.

First, the bigger an assembly is, no matter who is in it, the more passion wins out over reason. Second, the larger the number, the greater the share of members with little knowledge and weak abilities. And it is exactly on people like this that the speaking skill and cleverness of the few have their full effect.

In the ancient republics, where all the people met in person, a single speaker or a crafty politician usually ruled as completely as if a king's scepter had been placed in his hand. On the same principle, the larger a representative assembly becomes, the more it will share the weaknesses of mass meetings of the people. Ignorance will be fooled by cunning, and passion will be ruled by clever speeches and tricky arguments.

The people could make no greater mistake than to think that by adding more and more representatives beyond a certain point, they strengthen their protection against government by a few. Experience will always warn them of the opposite. AFTER THEY HAVE ENOUGH MEMBERS FOR SAFETY, FOR LOCAL KNOWLEDGE, AND FOR A BROAD CONNECTION WITH THE WHOLE SOCIETY, every extra representative will work against their own goals. The face of the government may become more democratic, but the soul that drives it will be more oligarchic. The machine will be bigger, but the springs that control its movements will be fewer, and often more secret.

:::quote
The countenance of the government may become more democratic, but the soul that animates it will be more oligarchic. The machine will be enlarged, but the fewer, and often the more secret, will be the springs by which its motions are directed.

Meaning: A huge assembly may look more democratic, but in reality a small group of insiders will run it. "Countenance" means face or appearance. "Oligarchic" means ruled by a few.
:::

:::vocab
Oligarchy: Rule by a small group of people.
Sophistry: Clever but misleading arguments.
:::

:::commentary
This is a striking claim: more representatives can mean *less* real democracy. Madison's point is that in a very large body, ordinary members can't all speak or shape decisions, so power flows to the few who can organize and persuade. Many observers say this has come true in the modern House, where party leaders and committee chairs control much of what happens. Others argue that the House's size is not the main cause, and point to the rules and party loyalty instead.

Notice that Madison is not arguing for the smallest possible House. He says there must be "a sufficient number" for safety, local knowledge, and a connection to the whole society. His question is where the right balance lies.
:::

## Why a majority should be enough {orig=15}

Connected with the objection about the number of representatives is another one that has been raised about the number needed to do business. Some have said that more than a majority of members should have been required for a quorum. They have also said that, in some cases if not all, more than a majority of those present should have been needed to make a decision.

It can't be denied that such a precaution might have had some advantages. It might have been an extra shield for certain particular interests, and another general obstacle to hasty and one-sided measures. But these benefits are outweighed by the drawbacks on the other side:

- **The minority would rule.** Whenever justice or the general good required new laws or active measures, the basic principle of free government would be turned upside down. It would no longer be the majority that ruled. Power would be handed to the minority.
- **Minorities could abuse it.** If this power to block were limited to particular cases, a self-interested minority could use it to avoid making fair sacrifices for the general good. Or, in emergencies, it could use it to demand unreasonable favors.
- **It would encourage walkouts.** Finally, it would make it easier to carry out the harmful practice of "secessions," when members walk out to block business. This practice has appeared even in states where only a majority is required. It undermines every principle of order and regular government. It leads more directly to public upheaval and the ruin of popular governments than any other practice we have yet seen among us.

:::vocab
Quorum: The minimum number of members who must be present for a legislature to do official business.
Secession: In 1787, this could mean members walking out of a legislature to block it from acting, not only a state leaving the Union.
:::

:::commentary
The Constitution says that a majority of each house is a quorum (Article I, Section 5), and most decisions need only a majority vote. This was a deliberate break from the Articles of Confederation, under which major decisions needed the votes of nine of the thirteen states.

When Madison mentions "secessions," his readers would likely have thought of a recent event. In September 1787, members of the Pennsylvania assembly who opposed the Constitution stayed away to keep the assembly from having a quorum and calling a ratifying convention. A crowd dragged two of them back to the chamber so the vote could go forward. In recent years, legislators in several states, including Texas and Oregon, have walked out to deny a quorum, and the debate over whether this is a fair tactic continues.
:::

:::today
Madison's "power of the purse" is still at the center of American politics. Fights over the federal budget, the debt limit, and government shutdowns are all battles over this weapon. Supporters of strong congressional control say it is the people's best check on the president. Critics say using it to threaten a shutdown can hurt ordinary people who depend on the government. Madison's argument about supermajorities also echoes in today's debate over the Senate filibuster, which in practice means most bills need 60 votes to pass the Senate. Supporters say it protects the minority and forces compromise. Critics say it lets the minority rule, exactly the danger Madison described.
:::
