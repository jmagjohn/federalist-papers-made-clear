:::summary
Having argued that separated states would fight each other, Hamilton now asks what those wars would do to American freedom. At first, he says, wars between the states would be especially destructive, because America had no professional armies or forts to slow invaders down. But constant danger would soon push each state to build **standing armies** and to strengthen its **executive**. When people live in fear, they trade freedom for safety, soldiers come to be seen as masters, and governments drift toward **monarchy**. Hamilton contrasts Britain, an island that needed only a small army and so kept its liberty, with the nations of mainland Europe. A united America, protected by an ocean, could enjoy Britain's advantage. A divided America would become like Europe.
:::

:::keypoints
- Early wars between the states would be **especially destructive**: open borders, no forts, and armies of plunderers.
- "**Safety from external danger is the most powerful director of national conduct.**" People will give up liberty to feel safe.
- Constant danger would produce **standing armies** and stronger **executives**, pushing the states toward monarchy.
- Once one state built an army, its neighbors would have to do the same, spreading "**the engines of despotism**" across America.
- Ancient Greek republics didn't need standing armies because their **citizens were all soldiers**. Modern people work at farming and trade, so modern wars require **professional armies**.
- In a country that is often invaded, the military rises above civil government, and people come to see soldiers as their **superiors**, and then their **masters**.
- **Britain**, protected by the sea, kept only a small army, and that helped it stay free.
- A **united America**, protected by the Atlantic, could have the same advantage. **Disunion** would make it like mainland Europe.
:::

:::context Background
Few things frightened Americans of 1787 more than a **standing army**, a permanent force of professional soldiers kept even in peacetime. They had learned from English history and political writers that kings used such armies to crush liberty. They had their own memories too. British troops had been stationed in Boston before the Revolution, and the Declaration of Independence complained that the king had "kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the Consent of our legislatures."

Opponents of the Constitution pointed out that it allowed Congress to raise an army in peacetime. Hamilton promises to answer that objection later (he does so in Nos. 24–29). Here he turns the argument around. The real danger of standing armies, he says, comes from **disunion**, not from the Constitution.
:::

## What war between the states would look like {orig=1-3}

So let us take it as an established truth that the states, if they separated, or whatever groups of them might form out of the wreck of the Confederacy, would go through the same ups and downs of peace and war, friendship and hostility, as all neighboring nations that are not united under one government. Let us now look briefly at some of the consequences of that situation.

In the first years of their separate existence, war between the states would cause much greater suffering than war usually causes in countries that have long had professional military forces.

The trained armies always kept ready on the continent of Europe are a threat to liberty and a drain on the economy. Even so, they have had one great advantage. They have made sudden conquests impossible, and they have prevented the rapid destruction that used to mark the path of war before they existed.

The skill of building fortifications has helped in the same way. The nations of Europe are surrounded by chains of fortified places, which block invasions from either side. Whole campaigns are spent capturing two or three border forts just to get into an enemy's country. At every step, similar obstacles wear down an invader's strength and slow its advance. In the past, an invading army could reach the heart of a neighboring country almost as soon as news of its approach arrived. But now a fairly small force of trained troops, fighting defensively from strong positions, can slow down and finally defeat a much larger one.

The history of war in that part of the world is no longer a history of nations conquered and empires overthrown. It is a history of towns taken and retaken, of battles that decide nothing, of retreats that do more good than victories, of great effort and little gain.

In this country, the picture would be completely reversed. Americans' distrust of military forces would delay building them as long as possible. Without fortifications, the borders of each state would be wide open to its neighbors, which would make raids easy. The more populous states could overrun their smaller neighbors with little trouble. Conquests would be as easy to make as they would be hard to keep. So war would be scattered and full of plundering. PLUNDER and destruction always follow armies of irregular troops. The most important events of our wars would be the sufferings of ordinary people.

:::vocab
Fortification: A fort or other strong defensive structure built to protect a place from attack.
Garrison: A group of troops stationed in a fort or town to defend it.
Irregulars: Fighters who are not part of an organized, trained army.
Predatory: Living by raiding and stealing.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is describing European warfare in the 1700s. Armies were small, expensive, and professional, and generals tried hard not to lose them. Countries like France had built rings of star-shaped fortresses along their borders, so wars often became slow sieges. Hamilton's point is surprising: professional armies, which he admits are dangerous to liberty, at least made war less destructive for civilians.

Hamilton had seen the other kind of war up close. During the Revolution, he served on George Washington's staff. Fighting in places like New Jersey and New York often involved raids, plundering, and attacks on civilians by irregular forces on both sides.
:::

## People will trade freedom for safety {orig=4}

This picture is not exaggerated, though I admit it would not stay accurate for long.

Safety from outside danger is the most powerful force guiding a nation's behavior. Even a passionate love of liberty will, after a while, give way to it. War brings violent destruction of life and property. Constant danger brings constant effort and alarm. These will force even the nations most devoted to liberty to turn to institutions that tend to destroy their civil and political rights, in search of rest and security. To be more safe, they eventually become willing to risk being less free.

:::quote
Safety from external danger is the most powerful director of national conduct. Even the ardent love of liberty will, after a time, give way to its dictates.

Meaning: When people feel threatened, the desire to be safe will eventually beat even a strong love of freedom.
:::

:::quote
To be more safe, they at length become willing to run the risk of being less free.

Meaning: Frightened people will accept limits on their freedom if they think it will protect them.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the most quoted lines in the Federalist Papers. It's often brought up in modern debates about security and civil liberties, especially in wartime or after terrorist attacks. Note that Hamilton is not *recommending* this trade. He is warning that it happens almost automatically when a nation lives in constant danger, and he wants to avoid putting Americans in that position.
:::

## Standing armies and stronger rulers {orig=5-7}

The institutions I mainly have in mind are STANDING ARMIES and all the other parts of a military establishment that go with them.

Some people say that the new Constitution does not forbid standing armies, and so they conclude that standing armies may exist under it. But even by the terms of their own argument, it is at most possible, not certain, that they would exist.

On the other hand, standing armies would be the unavoidable result if the Confederacy broke up. Frequent war and constant fear call for constant readiness, and they will surely produce standing armies.

The weaker states or confederacies would turn to them first, to make themselves equal to their more powerful neighbors. They would try to make up for having fewer people and resources with a more organized and effective defense, using trained troops and fortifications. At the same time, they would have to strengthen the executive branch of their governments. As they did, their constitutions would move steadily toward monarchy. It is the nature of war to increase the power of the executive at the expense of the legislature.

These methods would soon give the states or confederacies that used them an advantage over their neighbors. Small or naturally weaker states, with energetic governments and trained armies, have often defeated large or naturally stronger states that lacked these advantages. Neither the pride nor the safety of the larger states or confederacies would let them put up for long with this humiliating advantage, which came from circumstances rather than natural strength. They would quickly use the same methods to win back their lost position.

So in a short time we would see, in every part of this country, the same machinery of tyranny that has been the curse of the Old World. At least, this would be the natural course of events. And our reasoning is more likely to be correct the more closely it follows this natural course.

These are not vague guesses based on imagined flaws in a Constitution that places all power in the hands of the people or their representatives and delegates. They are solid conclusions drawn from the natural and necessary course of human affairs.

:::quote
It is of the nature of war to increase the executive at the expense of the legislative authority.

Meaning: In wartime, power flows to the leader who commands the army and away from the lawmakers.
:::

:::note
In a footnote, Hamilton promises to answer the standing-army objection "in its proper place." He says he will show that the Constitution takes the only sensible precaution possible, and a better one than most state constitutions, many of which had no guard at all. He means the rule in Article I, Section 8 that Congress cannot fund the army for more than two years at a time. He makes this argument in [No. 24](/papers/24/) through [No. 26](/papers/26/).
:::

:::vocab
Standing army: A permanent, professional army kept even in peacetime.
Executive: The branch of government that carries out the laws, led by a president, governor, or king.
Despotism: Rule by one person with unlimited power.
Adventitious: Coming from outside circumstances rather than from natural causes.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point about an "arms race" is easy to recognize today. When one country builds up its military, its neighbors feel threatened and build up theirs, even if no one wants a war. Each step makes sense for each country, but together they leave everyone less safe and less free.

His claim that war strengthens the executive has been tested many times in American history. Presidents have gained power during wartime, from Abraham Lincoln suspending the writ of habeas corpus in the Civil War to the expansion of presidential power during the world wars and the Cold War. People still debate how much power presidents should have in wartime, and how much should remain with Congress, which the Constitution gives the power to declare war.
:::

## Why ancient Greece was different {orig=8}

Someone might object: why didn't standing armies grow out of the conflicts that so often tore apart the ancient republics of Greece? Several answers can be given, and each is equally convincing.

People today work hard and are absorbed in earning money. They are devoted to improving farming and trade. These habits don't fit with being a nation of soldiers, which is what the people of those ancient republics really were.

Also, governments today can raise far more money than before. That is because of the increase in gold and silver, the growth of industry, and the science of finance, which is a modern invention. Together with the habits of modern nations, these have completely changed the way wars are fought. They have made trained armies, separate from the body of citizens, the constant companions of frequent war.

:::commentary
In city-states like Sparta and Athens, citizens themselves were the army. A Spartan man trained for war from childhood, and Athenian citizens served as soldiers and rowers in the navy. So there was no separate class of professional soldiers who could turn against the people.

Hamilton's point is that modern people are busy farming, making things, and trading. They won't all drop their work to fight. So a modern nation that faces frequent war will hire professional soldiers, and modern governments can afford them because they can tax and borrow on a scale the ancient world never knew.
:::

## Safe countries and threatened countries {orig=9-11}

There is also a big difference between a country whose location means it is rarely invaded and a country that is often invaded and always afraid of invasion.

The rulers of the first kind of country have no good excuse, even if they wanted one, to keep armies as large as the second kind must keep. In the first case, the army is rarely if ever called out to defend the homeland, so the people are in no danger of being trained into military obedience. The laws are not used to being loosened to meet military emergencies. Civil government stays fully strong, not corrupted by or mixed up with military ways and habits. The army is so small that the natural strength of the people is more than a match for it. Citizens are not used to looking to the military for protection, or to putting up with its abuses. So they neither love nor fear the soldiers. They see them with a watchful acceptance, as a necessary evil, and they stand ready to resist any power they suspect may be used against their rights.

In such a situation, the army may usefully help the civil authorities put down a small faction, an occasional mob, or an uprising. But it will be unable to force any abuse of power on the united resistance of the great body of the people.

In a country of the second kind, the opposite of all this happens. Constant threats force the government to be always ready to fight them off, so its armies must be large enough to defend the country at a moment's notice. The constant need for soldiers' services raises their importance and lowers the standing of ordinary citizens in the same proportion. The military rises above civil government.

People who live in places where wars are often fought must constantly put up with violations of their rights, and these weaken their sense of those rights. Little by little, the people come to see the soldiers not only as their protectors, but as their superiors. It is a short and easy step from there to seeing them as their masters. And it is very hard to get a people with that attitude to put up a bold or effective resistance when the military helps someone seize power.

:::vocab
Civil (government): Run by ordinary citizens and officials, not by the military.
Magistrate: A government official who enforces the law.
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Militia: Ordinary citizens who are trained to serve as soldiers when needed, rather than full-time professional soldiers.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton describes a step-by-step slide: soldiers as **protectors**, then **superiors**, then **masters**. History offers many examples. Julius Caesar used his loyal army to take over the Roman Republic. In Hamilton's own lifetime, a little over ten years after this essay, Napoleon Bonaparte, a general made popular by France's constant wars, seized power in 1799 and later crowned himself emperor.

The United States came close to this danger early on. In 1783, some unpaid Continental Army officers at Newburgh, New York, talked of using the army to pressure Congress. George Washington personally talked them out of it. The Constitution keeps the military under civilian control by making the elected president the commander in chief and giving Congress the power to fund the army and declare war.
:::

## The lesson of Britain {orig=12-13}

The kingdom of Great Britain belongs to the first kind. It is an island, and its powerful navy largely protects it from foreign invasion. So it doesn't need a large army at home. All that has been thought necessary is a force big enough to hold off a sudden landing until the militia has time to gather and organize. No national need has called for a larger force at home, and public opinion would not have tolerated one. For a long time, there has also been little room for the other effects I described that come from war at home.

This lucky location has done a great deal to preserve the liberty that Britain still enjoys today, in spite of the widespread bribery and corruption there. If, instead, Britain had been on the continent, it would have had to keep military forces at home as large as those of the other great powers of Europe. Then, like them, it would very likely be ruled today by the absolute power of one man. It is possible, though not easy, that the people of that island could be enslaved for other reasons. But it cannot happen through the power of an army as small as the one usually kept there.

If we are wise enough to preserve the Union, we may enjoy an advantage much like that of an island for ages to come. Europe is far away from us. Its colonies near us will probably stay too weak, compared to us, to cause us any dangerous trouble. In this position, we won't need large military forces for our security.

But suppose we were divided. The separate parts would either stay separate or, most likely, be thrown together into two or three confederacies. In a short time, we would be in the same position as the powers of mainland Europe. Our liberties would become the victims of the very means we used to defend ourselves against each other's ambition and jealousy.

:::vocab
Insular: Having to do with an island. Britain's "insular situation" means its position as an island.
Venality: Willingness to be bribed; corruption.
:::

:::commentary
English law had long reflected the fear of standing armies. The English Bill of Rights of 1689 said that keeping a standing army in peacetime without Parliament's consent was against the law. Parliament approved the army's rules for only one year at a time, which gave it a regular check on the king's military power. Hamilton's "venality and corruption" refers to the way British governments of his time used government jobs, pensions, and bribes to control members of Parliament.

Hamilton's prediction about America held true for a long time. Protected by two oceans, the United States kept only a small army in peacetime for most of the 1800s. That changed in the 1900s. After World War II and during the Cold War, the United States kept large military forces permanently for the first time, raising some of the same questions Hamilton asks here.
:::

## A weighty idea {orig=14}

This idea is not shallow or empty. It is solid and important. It deserves the most serious and careful thought of every sensible and honest person, whatever his party.

Let such people pause firmly and seriously and think calmly about how important this idea is. Let them look at it from every angle and follow it to all its consequences. Then they will not hesitate to give up their small objections to a Constitution whose rejection would very likely mean the end of the Union. The airy ghosts that float before the feverish imaginations of some of its opponents would quickly give way to the far more solid shapes of dangers that are real, certain, and terrifying.

:::commentary
Hamilton's closing jab at the "distempered imaginations" of his opponents is typical of persuasive writing on both sides of the ratification debate. The Anti-Federalists' fears of a standing army under the new government were not imaginary to them. They had real historical reasons for worry. Hamilton's strategy is to say: *you fear an army under the Constitution, but you should fear far bigger armies without it.*
:::

:::today
Hamilton's warning still shapes debates today. "To be more safe, they at length become willing to run the risk of being less free" is often quoted in arguments about surveillance, security, and civil liberties. People also still argue about the size and cost of the military. In his farewell speech in 1961, President Dwight Eisenhower, a former general, warned Americans about the growing influence of the "military-industrial complex." Supporters of a strong military say the world has changed, and that oceans no longer protect America the way they did in Hamilton's time. Critics say a large permanent military brings exactly the dangers Hamilton described. Both sides are wrestling with his question: how can a nation be safe without giving up its freedom?
:::
