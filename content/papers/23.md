:::summary
Hamilton begins the third part of the series: why the Union needs a government at least as strong as the one the Constitution proposes. His core argument is simple. The government responsible for national defense must have all the powers needed to do that job. No one can predict every danger a nation might face, so the power to raise armies, build navies, and pay for them shouldn't be limited in advance. "The means ought to be proportioned to the end." The real question isn't whether these powers are too large. It's whether the government is designed well enough to be trusted with them.
:::

:::keypoints
- The Union's main purposes: **common defense**, **keeping the peace** at home and abroad, **regulating trade**, and **managing foreign relations**.
- Defense powers (armies, navies, rules for them, directing them, and paying for them) should exist **without limitation**, because future dangers can't be predicted.
- A basic rule: **the means must match the end**. Whoever is responsible for a goal must have the tools to reach it.
- The Articles of Confederation already gave Congress unlimited power to *ask* for soldiers and money. The problem was that the states didn't deliver.
- So the national government must be able to **raise troops and money directly** from citizens, not through the states.
- If a government can't be trusted with the powers its job requires, the answer is to **design it better**, not to weaken it.
- The country's large size is an argument **for** an energetic government, not against it.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 1](/papers/1/), Hamilton laid out the plan of the series. The third topic was "the necessity of a government at least equally energetic with the one proposed." Nos. 23–36 cover that topic, focusing first on defense (Nos. 23–29) and then on taxes (Nos. 30–36).

Hamilton had served through the Revolutionary War as an officer and as George Washington's aide. He had seen the Continental Army go hungry, unpaid, and barefoot because Congress couldn't make the states send supplies. At Valley Forge in the winter of 1777–78, about 2,000 soldiers died from disease and exposure. That experience shaped this essay.
:::

## Three questions {orig=1-2}

We now reach the next point: why the Union needs a Constitution at least as energetic as the one proposed.

This question naturally divides into three parts. What goals should the national government provide for? How much power does it need to reach those goals? And on whom should that power act? How the power is divided and organized will be covered later.

## What the Union is for {orig=3}

The main purposes of the Union are these:

- the common defense of its members;
- keeping the public peace, against both internal uprisings and outside attacks;
- regulating trade with other nations and between the states;
- managing our political and commercial relations with foreign countries.

## Defense powers can't be limited in advance {orig=4-5}

The powers essential to the common defense are these: to raise armies; to build and equip navies; to set rules for governing both; to direct their operations; and to pay for them.

These powers should exist without limits, BECAUSE IT IS IMPOSSIBLE TO PREDICT OR DEFINE HOW GREAT OR HOW VARIED A NATION'S EMERGENCIES MAY BE, OR HOW GREAT OR VARIED THE MEANS NEEDED TO MEET THEM. The situations that can endanger a nation's safety are endless. For that reason, no constitutional chains can wisely be placed on the power that is responsible for that safety. This power should be as broad as every possible combination of dangers. And it should be directed by the same body that is in charge of the common defense.

This is one of those truths that any clear and fair mind can see for itself. Argument can cloud it, but can't make it any clearer. It rests on rules that are as simple as they are universal. The MEANS should match the END. And the people expected to achieve a goal should have the means to achieve it.

:::quote
These powers ought to exist without limitation, BECAUSE IT IS IMPOSSIBLE TO FORESEE OR DEFINE THE EXTENT AND VARIETY OF NATIONAL EXIGENCIES, OR THE CORRESPONDENT EXTENT AND VARIETY OF THE MEANS WHICH MAY BE NECESSARY TO SATISFY THEM.

Meaning: Since no one knows what wars or emergencies the country will face, the Constitution shouldn't put a ceiling on the power to defend it. Hamilton used capital letters to stress that this was his main point.
:::

:::quote
...the MEANS ought to be proportioned to the END...

Meaning: If you give someone a job, you have to give them the tools to do it. An "end" is a goal, and the "means" are the tools for reaching it.
:::

:::vocab
Exigency: An urgent need or emergency.
Axiom: A basic truth so obvious that it doesn't need to be proven.
Common defense: Protecting the whole country from attack. The phrase appears in the Constitution's Preamble.
:::

:::commentary
This is Hamilton's central argument, and it is still used today. If Congress is responsible for national defense, it can't do the job with its hands tied. Imagine a constitution that said "the army may never have more than 10,000 soldiers." What happens when a much larger enemy invades?

Critics saw the danger in this logic. If defense powers have no limits, what stops a government from using "national security" as an excuse to do anything? Hamilton's answer comes at the end of the essay: don't limit the powers, but design the government carefully so it can be trusted with them.
:::

## Once you decide, the powers must follow {orig=6}

Whether there should be a national government in charge of the common defense is, at first, a question open for debate. But the moment the answer is yes, it follows that this government should have all the powers needed to fully carry out that responsibility.

Unless someone can show that the dangers to public safety can be kept within definite limits, we have to accept the consequence. There can be no limit on the authority responsible for defending and protecting the community, in anything essential to making that defense work. That means anything essential to FORMING, DIRECTING, or SUPPORTING the NATIONAL FORCES.

## The Articles had the right idea but no way to carry it out {orig=7-8}

The current Confederation has been shown to be full of defects. Even so, its authors seem to have fully accepted this principle, though they didn't make proper provision for carrying it out.

Congress has unlimited discretion to request soldiers and money, to govern the army and navy, and to direct their operations. Its requests legally bind the states, which have the most solemn duty to provide what is asked of them. So the clear intention was that the United States should command whatever resources it judged necessary for "the common defense and general welfare." People assumed that the states' sense of their true interests, along with good faith, would be enough to make sure they did their duty to the central government on time.

Experience, however, has proven that this hope was mistaken and imaginary. I think the points made in the last several essays are enough to convince any fair and thoughtful person of this. The basic principles of the system must be completely changed.

If we are serious about giving the Union strength and a long life, we must give up the useless plan of making laws for the states as collective bodies. We must extend the laws of the national government to the individual citizens of America. We must throw out the false system of quotas and requests, which is both unworkable and unfair.

The result of all this is that the Union should have full power to raise troops, to build and equip navies, and to raise the money needed to form and support an army and navy, in the normal ways used by other governments.

:::commentary
Hamilton points out something surprising: the Articles of Confederation *already* gave Congress unlimited power over defense on paper. Congress could ask for as many soldiers and as much money as it wanted. The problem was that the states ignored the requests. So the issue was never the *amount* of power. It was the *method*: acting through the states instead of directly on people. This is the same "great and radical vice" Hamilton described in [No. 15](/papers/15/).
:::

:::vocab
Quota: A fixed share that each state was supposed to contribute.
Requisition: A formal request from Congress to the states for money or soldiers.
:::

## Each level of government gets full power over its own tasks {orig=9}

Our country's situation may call for a compound government instead of a single one, a confederation instead of one central government. If so, the key task is to sort out, as far as possible, which GOALS belong to each level of government. Then each level should get the fullest authority to reach the goals assigned to it.

Is the Union to be the guardian of the common safety? Are navies, armies, and money necessary for this? Then the national government must have power to pass all laws and make all rules related to them. The same goes for trade, and for every other area the national government is allowed to handle.

Is justice between citizens of the same state the proper job of the local governments? Then the states must have all the authority connected with that job, and with every other job assigned to them.

Not to give each level enough power to match its goals would break the most obvious rules of good sense. It would foolishly trust the nation's great interests to hands too weak to manage them well.

:::commentary
This paragraph balances the rest of the essay. Hamilton isn't saying the national government should have unlimited power over *everything*. He's saying it should have full power over the *specific tasks* it is given, like defense and trade. The states should have full power over *their* tasks, like ordinary justice between citizens. The key is dividing the tasks clearly. Madison describes this division in [No. 39](/papers/39/) and [No. 45](/papers/45/).
:::

## Who is best placed to provide for defense? {orig=10}

Who is most likely to make proper plans for the public defense? Surely it is the body that has been given responsibility for the public safety. Several things make it the best choice:

- As the center of information, it will best understand how large and urgent the dangers are.
- As the representative of the WHOLE country, it will care most about protecting every part.
- Because it is responsible for this duty, it will feel most strongly the need to act.
- Because its authority reaches across all the states, it alone can create unified and coordinated plans for the common safety.

Isn't it obviously inconsistent to give the national government responsibility for the common defense, while leaving the EFFECTIVE powers for providing it in the hands of the states? Won't lack of cooperation be the certain result of such a system? Won't weakness, disorder, an unfair division of the burdens and disasters of war, and an unnecessary and unbearable rise in costs naturally follow? Haven't we clearly experienced these effects during the revolution we just fought?

:::commentary
Hamilton's final question would have hit hard with readers in 1787. During the Revolution, Congress was responsible for the war but depended on the states for everything. Some states sent their share of soldiers and supplies; others didn't. Washington wrote desperate letters complaining that his army lacked food, clothing, and pay. In 1783, unpaid officers at Newburgh, New York, even discussed threatening Congress, until Washington personally talked them out of it. Hamilton's readers had lived through all of this.
:::

## Don't limit the powers. Design the government well. {orig=11}

Any honest look at this subject will convince us that it is both unwise and dangerous to deny the national government full authority over all the matters it is responsible for.

The people should pay very close attention to make sure the government is designed so that it can safely be given these powers. If any plan, now or in the future, fails this test on calm examination, it should be rejected. A government whose structure makes it unfit to be trusted with all the powers a free people should give to any government would be an unsafe and improper place to keep the NATIONAL INTERESTS. But wherever those national interests can properly be trusted, the matching powers can safely go with them. This is the correct conclusion from all sound reasoning on the subject.

So the opponents of the Convention's plan should have limited themselves to showing that the internal structure of the proposed government makes it unworthy of the people's trust. They shouldn't have wandered into angry speeches and meaningless complaints about how broad its powers are. The POWERS are not too broad for the GOALS of the national government, in other words, for managing our NATIONAL INTERESTS. No convincing argument can be made that they are.

Some writers on the other side have hinted that the difficulty comes from the nature of things, and that the size of the country won't let us create a government that can safely hold such broad powers. If that were true, it would prove that we should lower our sights and split into separate confederacies that could work on a smaller scale. Otherwise, we would constantly face the absurdity of giving a government control of the nation's most essential interests while not daring to give it the powers needed to manage them properly. Let's not try to reconcile contradictions. Let's firmly choose a reasonable alternative.

:::quote
The POWERS are not too extensive for the OBJECTS of federal administration...

Meaning: The powers given to the national government are not too big for the jobs it has to do.
:::

:::commentary
This is the most important move in the essay. Hamilton tells the Anti-Federalists they are arguing about the wrong thing. Don't ask whether the powers are too big; the jobs require them. Ask whether the government is *designed* well enough to be trusted with them. That is exactly the question Madison takes up in Nos. 47–51, on separation of powers and checks and balances.

This argument changed the debate. Instead of "how much power?" the question became "how is power controlled?" The American answer has been structure: elections, two houses of Congress, a president who can be impeached, independent courts, and power shared with the states.
:::

## The country's size calls for strong government {orig=12}

I trust, however, that no one can prove a single national system is unworkable. Unless I'm badly mistaken, nothing convincing has yet been said to show it. I believe these essays have made the opposite as clear as anything can be that still depends on future time and experience.

At any rate, this much must be clear. The very difficulty people point to, the size of the country, is the strongest argument for an energetic government. No other kind could ever hold together a Union so large.

If we adopt the ideas of those who oppose the Constitution as our political beliefs, we will surely prove true the gloomy predictions that a national system can't work across the whole territory of the current Confederacy.

:::commentary
Hamilton turns his opponents' argument around. They said the country was too big for a strong central government. He says it's too big for anything *else*. A weak government could never hold together thirteen states stretched along a thousand miles of coast. If you follow the Anti-Federalists, he warns, their prediction that the Union will fail will come true, because they will have made it fail.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's argument that defense powers can't be limited in advance is still at the center of debates over national security. After the September 11 attacks, for example, Congress passed laws giving the government broad new powers to fight terrorism, and people argued over whether they went too far. Debates over military spending, war powers, surveillance, and emergency powers all come back to Hamilton's question: how do you give a government enough power to protect the country without giving it enough to threaten freedom? His answer, to focus on designing the government well rather than limiting its powers, is still debated.
:::
