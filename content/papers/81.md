:::summary
Hamilton explains how judicial power is divided among the federal courts. He defends having a Supreme Court that is separate from the legislature, unlike in Britain, where the House of Lords was the highest court. Critics feared the Court would be above Congress and impossible to control. Hamilton answers that the danger of judges grabbing power is "a phantom," since courts are weak and judges can be impeached. He explains why Congress needs the power to create lower federal courts. He shows that the Supreme Court will hear only a few kinds of cases first, and most others only on appeal. Along the way, he argues that states can't be sued by individuals without their consent. Finally, he denies that the Court's power to review "fact" on appeal will abolish trial by jury.
:::

:::keypoints
- The nation needs **one supreme court** with the final say. The real question is whether it should be separate from the legislature.
- Critics said the Court could interpret laws by "the spirit of the Constitution" and would answer to no one. Hamilton says **every state court** under a limited constitution has the same duty to follow the constitution over a law.
- Lawmakers make **poor final judges**. They may defend their own bad laws, they serve short terms, they lack legal training, and they are divided by party.
- No legislature, in Britain or America, can **overturn a court's ruling** in a particular case. It can only change the rule for future cases.
- The fear of judges taking over is **"in reality a phantom,"** because courts are weak and Congress can **impeach** judges who abuse their power.
- Congress may create **lower federal courts** because some state courts are too dependent or too local to enforce national law.
- The Supreme Court has **original jurisdiction** in only two rare kinds of cases. In everything else, it has **appellate jurisdiction**, subject to rules set by Congress.
- A state, as a sovereign, **cannot be sued by an individual without its consent**.
- The Court's power to review **"law and fact"** on appeal does not abolish jury trials, and Congress can make rules to protect jury verdicts.
:::

:::context Background
Article III, Section 1 says the judicial power belongs to "one supreme Court" and to lower courts that Congress may create. In [No. 78](/papers/78/), Hamilton defended judges' life tenure and their duty to strike down unconstitutional laws. In [No. 80](/papers/80/), he listed the kinds of cases federal courts can hear. Now he explains how those cases are divided between the Supreme Court and the lower courts.

Much of this essay answers the Anti-Federalist writer "Brutus." Brutus warned that the Supreme Court would be beyond anyone's control. He argued that it could reshape laws by reading them according to the Constitution's "spirit," and that nothing could correct its mistakes. Other critics feared that the Constitution would let individuals sue states over their war debts, and that it would end jury trials in civil cases.
:::

## One Supreme Court, separate from Congress {orig=1-3}

Let's now return to how judicial power is divided among different courts, and how those courts relate to each other.

Under the Convention's plan, "the judicial power of the United States" is "to be vested in one Supreme Court, and in such inferior courts as the Congress may, from time to time, ordain and establish."

No one is likely to dispute that there should be one court with supreme and final authority. The reasons have been given elsewhere and are too obvious to repeat. The only question raised about it seems to be whether it should be a separate body or a branch of the legislature.

Here we see the same contradiction we have noticed in several other cases. Some critics object to making the Senate the court for impeachments, because that would improperly mix the branches' powers. Yet these same people suggest, at least indirectly, that the final decision in all cases should be given to all or part of the legislature.

:::vocab
Inferior courts: Lower courts. In the Constitution, the word "inferior" means "below the Supreme Court," not "poor quality."
Jurisdiction: The area of authority of a government or court. For a court, it means the kinds of cases it has the power to hear and decide.
:::

:::note
In a footnote, Hamilton mocks a claim that the power to create "inferior courts" was meant to abolish the states' county courts, which were commonly called inferior courts. The Constitution, he points out, speaks of courts "inferior to the supreme Court." It plainly means lower federal courts, not county courts. "It is ridiculous to imagine that county courts were in contemplation," he writes.
:::

## The charge that the Court will be above Congress {orig=4-5}

The arguments behind this charge, or rather the suggestions, go like this:

> "The authority of the proposed Supreme Court of the United States, which is to be a separate and independent body, will be superior to that of the legislature. The power of construing the laws according to the spirit of the Constitution, will enable that court to mould them into whatever shape it may think proper; especially as its decisions will not be in any manner subject to the revision or correction of the legislative body. This is as unprecedented as it is dangerous. In Britain, the judicial power, in the last resort, resides in the House of Lords, which is a branch of the legislature; and this part of the British government has been imitated in the State constitutions in general. The Parliament of Great Britain, and the legislatures of the several States, can at any time rectify, by law, the exceptionable decisions of their respective courts. But the errors and usurpations of the Supreme Court of the United States will be uncontrollable and remediless."

When you examine this argument, you will find it is made up entirely of bad reasoning based on mistaken facts.

First of all, not a single word in the plan directly gives the national courts power to interpret the laws according to the spirit of the Constitution. Nor does it give them any more freedom in this respect than the courts of every state can claim.

I do admit that the Constitution should be the standard for interpreting the laws. Wherever a law clearly conflicts with the Constitution, the law should give way. But this idea doesn't come from anything special in the Convention's plan. It comes from the general theory of a limited Constitution. To the extent it is true, it applies equally to most, if not all, of the state governments. So this objection to the federal courts would apply just as much to the state courts in general. It would condemn every constitution that tries to put limits on the legislature.

:::vocab
Construe: To interpret; to work out the meaning of a law or text.
Usurpation: Taking power that one has no right to.
Limited Constitution: A constitution that puts specific limits on what the government, especially the legislature, may do.
:::

:::commentary
The long quotation sums up the arguments of Brutus, who wrote that the Supreme Court's judges would be "independent of the people, of the legislature, and of every power under heaven." Hamilton doesn't deny that the Court will strike down laws that clearly violate the Constitution. He defended that power in [No. 78](/papers/78/). Instead, he says it is nothing new, because state courts under written constitutions have the same duty.

Brutus's prediction is still debated. Supporters of the Court say it has mostly stayed within its role and has protected important rights. Critics say Brutus was right, and that the Court has used vague ideas like the Constitution's "spirit" to make major policy decisions that belong to elected officials.
:::

## Why lawmakers make poor final judges {orig=6-7}

Perhaps the force of the objection is supposed to lie in how the Supreme Court is organized. It is a separate body of judges instead of being one branch of the legislature, as in Britain and in New York. To press this point, the critics would have to give up the meaning they have worked so hard to attach to the famous rule that the branches of power must be kept separate.

Still, I will grant them something, in line with how that rule has been explained in these papers. Giving the final power of judging to a PART of the legislature does not violate the rule outright. But it comes so close to violating it that, for that reason alone, it is a worse choice than the one the Convention made.

There are several reasons why:

- **Lawmakers would defend their own bad laws.** A body that had any part in passing bad laws would rarely be willing to soften them when applying them. The same spirit that made them would be likely to interpret them. Still less could we expect men who had violated the Constitution as lawmakers to repair the damage as judges.
- **Lawmakers serve short terms.** Every reason for letting judges serve during good behavior argues against giving the final judicial power to a body of men chosen for limited terms. It would be absurd to have cases decided first by permanent judges and then, in the end, by judges whose positions are temporary and always changing.
- **Lawmakers lack legal knowledge.** It would be even more absurd to have judges chosen for the legal knowledge they gained through long, hard study overruled by men who, lacking that training, are bound to know less. Legislators will rarely be chosen for the qualities that make good judges. So there would be every reason to fear the bad results of poor information.
- **Lawmakers are divided by party.** Legislatures naturally tend to split into parties. So there is just as much reason to fear that the deadly breath of faction might poison the fountains of justice. The habit of always lining up on opposite sides would be too likely to drown out the voice of both law and equity.

These points should lead us to praise the wisdom of the states that have given final judicial power not to part of the legislature, but to separate and independent bodies. Some critics have called the Convention's plan new and unheard of. In fact, it just copies the constitutions of New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Georgia. The preference given to those models deserves high praise.

:::quote
...the pestilential breath of faction may poison the fountains of justice.

Meaning: Lawmakers are organized into parties that fight each other. If they were also the highest judges, party loyalty would infect court decisions.
:::

:::vocab
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
Equity: A kind of justice, separate from ordinary law, that lets judges do what is fair when strict legal rules would lead to an unfair result.
House of Lords: The upper house of the British Parliament, made up mostly of nobles and bishops in the 1700s. It also served as Britain's highest court until 2009.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton was criticizing his own state. Under New York's 1777 constitution, the highest court was called the "Court for the Trial of Impeachments and the Correction of Errors." It was made up of the state senators along with the top judges. New York kept this system until 1847. Britain, too, kept its highest court inside the House of Lords for more than two centuries after this essay, until a separate UK Supreme Court opened in 2009.

Notice the careful argument here. Hamilton admits that mixing judges and lawmakers isn't strictly forbidden by separation of powers. He had argued in [No. 47](/papers/47/) that some blending of the branches is normal. But he says that mixing them is still unwise for practical reasons.
:::

## No legislature can overturn a court's ruling {orig=8}

Second, it is not true that the British Parliament or the state legislatures can correct objectionable court decisions in any way that a future Congress couldn't. Neither the British theory of government nor the state constitutions allow a legislature to revise a court's judgment by passing a law. And nothing in the proposed Constitution forbids it any more than they do. In all these cases, the only obstacle is that it would be improper under the general principles of law and reason.

A legislature, without going beyond its proper role, cannot reverse a decision already made in a particular case. But it can set a new rule for future cases. That is the principle. It applies with exactly the same results to the state governments as to the national government now being considered. No difference can be found from any point of view.

:::commentary
This is an important distinction that still holds today. If Congress disagrees with how a court has interpreted a law, it can pass a new law that changes the rule going forward. Congress has done this many times. But it can't simply reach back and reverse the outcome of a case that has already been decided. That would make Congress into a court. In *Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm* (1995), the Supreme Court ruled that Congress cannot order federal courts to reopen cases that have already reached a final judgment.

If the Court strikes down a law as unconstitutional, the main way to overturn that decision is a constitutional amendment. This has happened several times, starting with the 11th Amendment, discussed below.
:::

## The danger of judges taking over is a phantom {orig=9}

Finally, the supposed danger that judges will take over the legislature's authority has been repeated on many occasions. It is in reality a phantom. Now and then, judges may misread a law or go against the legislature's will in particular cases. But this can never happen on such a large scale that it becomes a real problem or noticeably disturbs the order of the political system.

We can be certain of this because of the general nature of judicial power, the subjects it deals with, the way it is used, its weakness compared with the other branches, and its complete inability to back up any power grab with force.

This conclusion is made even stronger by an important check in the Constitution. One house of Congress can impeach judges, and the other can try them. This alone is complete security. Judges would never risk the combined anger of Congress through a series of deliberate power grabs while Congress had the means to punish their arrogance by removing them from office. This should remove all fears on the subject. At the same time, it is a strong argument for making the Senate the court for impeachment trials.

:::quote
...the supposed danger of judiciary encroachments on the legislative authority, which has been upon many occasions reiterated, is in reality a phantom.

Meaning: People keep warning that judges will take over Congress's powers, but Hamilton says this fear isn't real.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's confidence rested on impeachment. In practice, impeachment has not worked as a check on judges' rulings. When the House impeached Justice Samuel Chase in 1804, partly over his political conduct on the bench, the Senate acquitted him in 1805. Since then, the House has almost never impeached a judge over his rulings. The judges who have been removed were found guilty of misconduct such as taking bribes or lying under oath. Most people today agree that is the right practice, because it protects judicial independence. But it also means Hamilton's "complete security" against judges overstepping is weaker than he suggested.

Congress does have other tools, such as passing new laws, proposing amendments, and controlling the size of the Supreme Court and what cases lower federal courts can hear.
:::

## Why Congress can create lower federal courts {orig=10-14}

Having examined, and I hope answered, the objections to a separate and independent Supreme Court, I turn next to the power to create lower courts and how they will relate to the Supreme Court.

The power to create lower courts is clearly meant to avoid having to take every federal case to the Supreme Court. It is designed to let the national government set up or authorize a court in each state or district of the United States. Each court could decide matters of national jurisdiction within its area.

But, some ask, why couldn't the same purpose have been served by using the state courts? There are several answers. Even if we grant that the state courts are as fit and capable as possible, the power in question would still be a necessary part of the plan, if only to let Congress give the state courts authority over cases arising under the national Constitution. Giving that authority to the existing state courts would arguably be just as much "constituting tribunals" as creating new courts with the same power.

Still, shouldn't the Constitution have said more directly and clearly that the state courts would be used? In my opinion, there are strong reasons against it.

- Not even the wisest person can predict how far local loyalties might make state courts unfit to handle national cases.
- Anyone can see that courts set up like those in some states would be improper channels for the Union's judicial power. State judges who serve only as long as the legislature pleases, or from year to year, are too dependent to be trusted to enforce national laws firmly.
- If the first hearing of cases under national laws had to be given to such courts, the door to appeals would have to be left as wide open as possible. How easy or hard it is to appeal should depend on how much we trust or distrust the lower courts. I am fully convinced that appeals are proper in the kinds of cases the Convention's plan allows. But I would consider anything that let appeals run without limit, in practice, a source of public and private inconvenience.

I'm inclined to think it may turn out to be very useful to divide the United States into four, five, or six districts, and to set up a federal court in each district instead of one in every state. The judges of these courts, with help from the state judges, could travel through their districts to hold trials in different places. Justice could be done through them easily and quickly, and appeals could safely be kept within narrow limits. This plan seems to me, for now, the best one that could be adopted. To make it possible, the power to create lower courts must exist to the full extent the Constitution provides.

These reasons should convince any fair-minded person that the lack of this power would have been a great flaw in the plan. Now let's look at how judicial authority is to be divided between the Supreme Court and the lower courts of the Union.

:::vocab
Tribunal: A court.
Appeal: A request for a higher court to review and possibly change a lower court's decision.
Circuit: A route that a judge travels to hold court in different towns. Today, a federal "circuit" is a region served by one court of appeals.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's worry about state judges was based on real cases. In Rhode Island, judges were elected each year by the legislature. After they refused to enforce a paper-money law in *Trevett v. Weeden* (1786), the legislature called them in to explain themselves, and most of them lost their seats the next year. Judges like that could hardly be trusted to enforce unpopular national laws.

In the Judiciary Act of 1789, the first Congress did create lower federal courts. It set up district courts, generally one per state, and grouped them into three circuits. Supreme Court justices had to travel to hold circuit courts with the district judges, which was hard and tiring work. This "circuit riding" lasted in some form for about a century. Today there are 94 federal district courts and 13 courts of appeals.
:::

## Cases that start in the Supreme Court {orig=15}

The Supreme Court is to have original jurisdiction only "in cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers, and consuls, and those in which A STATE shall be a party."

Public ministers of every rank directly represent their nations' rulers. Every question involving them is so closely tied to the public peace that it is both wise and proper for such questions to go first to the nation's highest court. This protects the peace and shows respect for the nations they represent. Consuls are not, strictly speaking, diplomats. But since they are the official agents of their nations, the same reasoning mostly applies to them. As for cases where a state is a party, it would not suit a state's dignity to be sent to a lower court.

:::vocab
Original jurisdiction: The power to hear a case first, as a trial court, rather than on appeal.
Appellate jurisdiction: The power to review a case already decided by a lower court, and to confirm, change, or reverse that decision.
Ambassador: An official who represents a country in a foreign nation.
Consul: An official sent by a country to a foreign city to help its citizens and protect its trade there.
Public ministers: Diplomats. Ambassadors are the highest-ranking public ministers.
:::

## Can a state be sued? {orig=16-17}

This may be a bit of a detour from my main subject, but I want to mention a claim that has caused some alarm for very mistaken reasons. Some have suggested that if one state's government bonds were transferred to citizens of another state, those citizens could sue the state in federal court for the amount owed. The following points prove this claim has no basis.

It is part of the very nature of sovereignty that a sovereign cannot be sued by an individual without its consent. This is the general view and the general practice of humanity. Every state government in the Union now enjoys this exemption as one of the features of sovereignty. So unless the Convention's plan gives up this immunity, the states will keep it, and the danger suggested is purely imaginary.

When I discussed taxation, I explained the conditions needed for a state to lose part of its sovereignty, and I won't repeat them here. Going back to those principles will convince us that there is no basis for claiming that the state governments, by adopting the plan, would lose the privilege of paying their own debts in their own way. They would be free from every pressure except the obligation of good faith.

Contracts between a nation and individuals bind only the sovereign's conscience. They have no power to force payment. They give no right to sue apart from the sovereign's own will. And what would be the point of allowing suits against states for their debts? How could the money be collected? Clearly, it couldn't be done without waging war against the state that owed it. It would be completely forced and unjustified to read into the federal courts, by mere implication, a power that would lead to such results and destroy a right the states already have.

:::quote
It is inherent in the nature of sovereignty not to be amenable to the suit of an individual without its consent.

Meaning: A government with supreme authority can't be dragged into court by a private person unless it agrees to be sued. This idea is called *sovereign immunity*.
:::

:::vocab
Sovereignty: Supreme, independent political authority.
Sovereign immunity: The legal rule that a government cannot be sued without its consent.
Securities: Government bonds or other written promises to repay borrowed money.
:::

:::commentary
After the Revolutionary War, the states owed large debts, and many of their bonds had been bought up by people in other states and countries. Critics feared that these bondholders would use the federal courts to force states to pay. Hamilton promised that would not happen. John Marshall and James Madison gave similar assurances at Virginia's ratifying convention.

Hamilton's promise was soon tested, and at first it failed. In *Chisholm v. Georgia* (1793), a South Carolina man representing the estate of a merchant sued the state of Georgia over a debt from the Revolutionary War. Georgia refused to appear in court. The Supreme Court ruled 4–1 that a citizen of one state *could* sue another state in federal court, because Article III gives federal courts power over cases "between a State and Citizens of another State." Only Justice James Iredell dissented.

The decision alarmed the states, and Congress quickly proposed a constitutional amendment to overturn it. The **11th Amendment**, ratified in 1795, says federal judicial power does not extend to suits against a state by citizens of another state or of a foreign country. It was the first amendment adopted after the Bill of Rights, and it made Hamilton's view the law. Later, in *Hans v. Louisiana* (1890), the Court held that states are also immune from suits by their own citizens. The exact reach of state sovereign immunity is still debated by judges and scholars today.
:::

## Appeals "as to law and fact" {orig=18-20}

Let's return to the main line of my argument. We have seen that the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction would be limited to two kinds of cases, and both of them will rarely come up. In all other federal cases, the lower courts would have original jurisdiction. The Supreme Court would have only appellate jurisdiction, "with such exceptions and under such regulations as the Congress shall make."

Hardly anyone has questioned whether this appellate power is proper for questions of *law*. But there have been loud protests against applying it to questions of *fact*. Some well-meaning people in New York base their ideas on the language and procedures used in our own courts. They have come to see this power as a hidden replacement of jury trial with the civil-law method of trial. That method is used in our courts of admiralty, probate, and chancery.

In our legal language, the word "appellate" has taken on a technical meaning. We usually use it for appeals under the civil law. But if I'm not mistaken, it wouldn't have that meaning anywhere in New England. There, an appeal from one jury to another is familiar in both language and practice. It is even routine until there have been two verdicts on the same side. So the word "appellate" will not be understood the same way in New England as in New York. This shows that it would be wrong to read the word with a technical meaning taken from the legal practice of any one state.

In general terms, the word means nothing more than the power of one court to review the proceedings of another, as to the law, the facts, or both. How this is done may depend on old custom or on laws passed by the legislature. In a new government, it must depend on laws. It may be done with or without a jury, as seems wise. So if the Constitution ever allows a fact decided by a jury to be reexamined, it can be arranged so that a second jury does it. The case could be sent back to the lower court for a new trial of the facts, or the Supreme Court could order a jury trial on that issue directly.

But it doesn't follow that the Supreme Court will be allowed to reexamine a fact once a jury has decided it. Consider what happens in New York when a case is taken from a lower court to a higher court of law by a "writ of error." Can't we say, quite properly, that the higher court has jurisdiction over the facts as well as the law? It's true that it can't hold a new inquiry into the facts. But it accepts the facts as they appear in the record and declares the law that follows from them. That is jurisdiction over both fact and law. In fact, it's impossible to separate the two.

New York's common-law courts settle disputed facts by using a jury. Yet they clearly have jurisdiction over both fact and law. That is why, when both sides agree on the facts in their written pleadings, they don't call a jury at all, but go straight to judgment. So I argue on this ground that the phrase "appellate jurisdiction, both as to law and fact" does not necessarily mean that the Supreme Court will reexamine facts decided by juries in lower courts.

:::vocab
Questions of fact and questions of law: A question of fact asks what actually happened (Did the driver run the red light?). A question of law asks what the law means or requires. Juries traditionally decide facts, and judges decide the law.
Civil law: The legal system based on ancient Roman law, used in much of Europe, in which judges decide the facts without a jury. (This is a different meaning from "civil case," which means a non-criminal lawsuit.)
Common law: Law built up over centuries through court decisions, rather than passed by a legislature. American law inherited it from England.
Probate: The legal process of handling a dead person's will and property.
Chancery: A court of equity, led by a judge called the chancellor.
Writ of error: An order from a higher court asking a lower court to send up the record of a case so the higher court can check it for legal mistakes.
:::

:::note
In a footnote, Hamilton points out that the word *jurisdiction* comes from the Latin *jus* (law) and *dictio* (speaking). So it literally means "speaking or declaring the law."
:::

:::commentary
This part is technical, but the worry behind it was simple. Many Americans feared that a distant Supreme Court could throw out a jury's verdict and decide the facts itself, without a jury. Since juries were seen as a key protection of ordinary people against powerful officials and wealthy opponents, this was a serious charge.

The worry was strong enough that the Bill of Rights answered it directly. The **7th Amendment** (1791) says that "no fact tried by a jury, shall be otherwise re-examined in any Court of the United States, than according to the rules of the common law." In practice, federal appeals courts today mostly review questions of law. They generally accept a jury's findings of fact unless no reasonable jury could have reached them.
:::

## Congress can protect jury verdicts {orig=21-23}

We can easily imagine the reasoning that may have guided the Convention on this provision. It may have gone like this:

The Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction will cover cases that are decided in different ways. Some are decided by the methods of the COMMON LAW, and others by the methods of the CIVIL LAW. In common-law cases, the Supreme Court should generally review only the law. In civil-law cases, reexamining the facts is the usual practice. In some of those cases, such as prize cases about ships captured in war, it might be essential for keeping the peace with other nations. So in certain cases, appellate jurisdiction must reach questions of fact in the broadest sense.

It wouldn't work to make an express exception for all cases first tried by a jury. That's because in some states, every case is tried by a jury. Such an exception would block review of facts where it might be proper as well as where it might be improper. To avoid all these problems, the safest course is to state generally that the Supreme Court will have appellate jurisdiction as to both law and fact, and that Congress may make exceptions and rules for it. This lets the government shape it in whatever way best serves justice and public security.

In any case, this view of the matter proves beyond doubt that the claim that this provision abolishes jury trial is false. Congress would certainly have full power to provide that, in appeals to the Supreme Court, facts already tried by juries in the original case would not be reexamined. That would clearly be an allowed exception. But if, for the reason I just gave, that exception seemed too broad, it could be limited to cases decided by jury under the common law.

To sum up what I have said so far about the judicial branch:

- Its authority has been carefully limited to the cases clearly proper for national courts.
- In dividing that authority, only a very small share of original jurisdiction was given to the Supreme Court. The rest went to the lower courts.
- The Supreme Court will have appellate jurisdiction, as to both law and fact, in all the cases sent to it, subject to any exceptions and rules that seem wise.
- This appellate jurisdiction does not, in any case, abolish trial by jury.
- With an ordinary amount of good sense and honesty in the national government, the proposed court system will give us solid benefits without exposing us to any of the problems that have been predicted.

:::vocab
Prize cases: Court cases deciding whether a ship and its cargo, captured during war, were lawfully taken. The winner could keep or sell the "prize."
Exceptions Clause: The part of Article III saying the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction is subject to "such Exceptions" and "Regulations as the Congress shall make."
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton relies again on Congress to set the rules. The power he points to here, the Exceptions Clause, is still debated. It seems to let Congress take some kinds of cases away from the Supreme Court's appellate review. Some members of Congress have proposed using it to keep the Court from hearing cases on controversial subjects. Supporters say the Constitution plainly gives Congress this power. Critics say using it that way would undermine the Court's role as a check on the other branches. The Supreme Court has never clearly settled how far this power reaches.

Hamilton's final promise, that jury trials are safe, became the focus of [No. 83](/papers/83/), the longest essay in the series.
:::

:::today
Many of today's legal debates trace back to this essay. When people ask whether states can be sued for violating federal laws, they are debating the sovereign immunity Hamilton described, along with *Chisholm v. Georgia* and the 11th Amendment. When people propose limiting which cases the Supreme Court can hear, they rely on the Exceptions Clause Hamilton pointed to. And when Congress passes a new law to change the effect of a court decision, it follows Hamilton's rule that lawmakers can set new rules for the future but cannot reverse past judgments.
:::
