:::summary
Should a president be allowed to run for re-election? Hamilton says yes, and argues against term limits. He first explains that a new president tends to undo his predecessor's work and replace his officials, so frequent turnover makes government unstable. Then he lists five harms of forcing presidents out: less motive to do a good job, more temptation to grab money or power while there's still time, the loss of experienced leaders, the inability to keep the right person in a crisis, and constant instability. The supposed benefits of term limits are doubtful. In the end, the people should be free to keep a president they trust.
:::

:::keypoints
- "Administration" means the daily work of government: diplomacy, finances, spending, the military, and so on. The officials who do it are the **president's assistants**.
- New presidents tend to **undo their predecessors' work** and replace their officials, which causes instability.
- A long term plus **re-eligibility** lets the people keep a good president and keep good policies going.
- Term limits would have five harms:
- **1.** Less motive for **good behavior**, since there's no reward of re-election.
- **2.** More temptation to **profit** or even **seize power** before the term runs out.
- **3.** The loss of **experience**, "the parent of wisdom."
- **4.** Forcing out the right leader in an **emergency**, such as a war.
- **5.** Built-in **instability** in policy.
- The claimed benefits, more independence and more safety, are doubtful. A popular president barred from office might be **more dangerous**, not less.
- The people shouldn't be prevented from keeping leaders they trust.
:::

:::context Background
At the Constitutional Convention, delegates debated whether the president should be limited to one term. Many state governors could serve only a few terms in a row. Thomas Jefferson, writing from France, worried that without term limits a president could be re-elected for life, becoming a king in all but name. The final Constitution set no limit on re-election.

George Washington voluntarily stepped down after two terms in 1797, setting a powerful example. Every president followed it until Franklin D. Roosevelt won third and fourth terms in 1940 and 1944. The 22nd Amendment, ratified in 1951, then limited presidents to two elected terms. Hamilton's argument lost in the end, but it's still the best case against term limits.
:::

## Why a stable presidency means stable government {orig=1}

In its broadest sense, the "administration" of government includes everything government does, whether legislative, executive, or judicial. But in its most common and perhaps most precise meaning, it refers to executive details and belongs especially to the executive branch.

The actual work of foreign negotiations, preparing financial plans, spending public money according to the general budgets Congress approves, organizing the army and navy, directing military operations in war, and other similar matters: these are what we mostly mean by the administration of government. So the people directly in charge of these matters should be seen as the president's assistants or deputies. For that reason, they should get their offices by his appointment, or at least his nomination, and should be under his supervision.

This shows the close connection between how long the president stays in office and how stable the administration is. A new president often thinks that reversing and undoing his predecessor's work is the best way to prove his own ability and merit. And besides this tendency, when the change came from an election, the new president has reason to think the voters rejected his predecessor because they disliked his policies, and that the less he resembles him, the more he'll please them. These reasons, along with personal trust and friendships, would likely lead every new president to replace the officials in lower positions. Together, these causes would surely produce a disgraceful and harmful instability in the government's administration.

:::commentary
Hamilton predicted something that became a major feature of American politics. New presidents do often reverse their predecessors' policies, and they replace thousands of officials. By the 1830s, under President Andrew Jackson, this became known as the "spoils system": giving government jobs to political supporters as rewards. After a disappointed job-seeker assassinated President James Garfield in 1881, Congress passed the Pendleton Act (1883), which began hiring most federal workers based on merit instead of politics.

Hamilton's view that officials doing the "administration of government" are the president's deputies, under his supervision, is often cited today in debates over how much control a president should have over federal agencies.
:::

:::vocab
Administration: The day-to-day running of the government, especially by the executive branch.
Mutability: Constant change; instability.
Appropriations: Money that Congress sets aside by law for specific purposes.
:::

## Why re-eligibility matters {orig=2-3}

Along with a fairly long term, I connect the ability to be re-elected. The long term is needed to give the president the desire and determination to do his job well, and to give the community time to watch the direction of his policies and judge their merits from experience. Re-eligibility is needed so that, when the people approve of his conduct, they can keep him in office. That extends the benefit of his talents and good qualities, and secures for the government the advantage of continuing a wise policy.

Nothing seems more reasonable at first glance, or less sound on close inspection, than a plan that has had some respectable supporters on this point. I mean keeping the president in office for a certain time and then barring him from it, either for a limited period or forever. Whether the exclusion is temporary or permanent, it would have nearly the same effects. And those effects would mostly be harmful rather than helpful.

:::vocab
Re-eligibility: Being allowed to run for the same office again.
Term limits: Rules that limit how many terms, or years, a person can serve in an office.
:::

## Harm 1: less reason to do a good job {orig=4}

One bad effect of exclusion would be weaker motives for good behavior. Few people wouldn't feel much less eagerness in doing a job when they knew they must give up its advantages at a fixed time, compared with when they could hope to keep them by deserving to. No one will dispute this, as long as we admit that the desire for reward is one of the strongest drivers of human behavior, and that the best way to make people faithful is to make their interests match their duty.

Even the love of fame, the ruling passion of the noblest minds, would be affected. It might inspire a man to plan and start great, difficult projects for the public good that take a long time to complete, if he could hope to be allowed to finish what he started. But it would discourage him from starting them if he knew he must leave before he could finish, handing the work, and his reputation with it, to people who might be unable or unwilling to complete it. The most we could expect from most men in that situation is the negative merit of not doing harm, instead of the positive merit of doing good.

:::quote
...the best security for the fidelity of mankind is to make their interests coincide with their duty.

Meaning: The surest way to get people to do the right thing is to arrange things so that doing their duty also serves their own interests.
:::

:::quote
Even the love of fame, the ruling passion of the noblest minds...

Meaning: Hamilton believed the greatest leaders are driven by the desire to be remembered for doing great things. That desire can be harnessed for the public good.
:::

:::commentary
This is a classic Federalist argument: use human self-interest instead of fighting it. It matches Madison's "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" in [No. 51](/papers/51/). Hamilton thinks the hope of re-election makes presidents work harder and behave better.

Critics of this argument say re-election can also distort a president's choices. A first-term president may focus on winning the next election instead of doing what's right. That's one reason some people have proposed a single six-year term.
:::

## Harm 2: temptation to grab money or power {orig=5-7}

Another bad effect of exclusion would be the temptation to selfish goals, to stealing public money, and in some cases to seizing power.

Imagine a greedy man in the office, looking ahead to a time when he must give up the benefits he enjoys no matter what. He would feel an urge, hard for such a man to resist, to make the most of his chance while it lasted. He might not hesitate to use the most corrupt methods to make his harvest as large as it was short. Yet the same man, with a different future in front of him, might be satisfied with the regular benefits of his office, and might even be unwilling to risk the consequences of abusing it. His greed might keep his greed in check.

Also, the same man might be vain or ambitious as well as greedy. If he could hope to extend his honors through good conduct, he might hesitate to sacrifice his hunger for them to his hunger for money. But facing a certain end, his greed would likely defeat his caution, his vanity, and his ambition.

An ambitious man, too, seated at the top of his country's honors, would look ahead to the time when he must come down from that high place forever. He would know that no amount of merit on his part could save him from that unwelcome fall. Such a man would be much more strongly tempted to seize a good opportunity to try to extend his power, at any personal risk, than if he could hope to achieve the same goal by doing his duty.

Would it help the peace of the community, or the stability of the government, to have half a dozen men who had been respected enough to reach the presidency wandering among the people like discontented ghosts, longing for a place they could never hold again?

:::quote
His avarice might be a guard upon his avarice.

Meaning: A greedy president who hopes to be re-elected will behave honestly, because getting caught would cost him more in the long run. His greed keeps itself in check. "Avarice" means greed.
:::

:::quote
...wandering among the people like discontented ghosts, and sighing for a place which they were destined never more to possess?

Meaning: Hamilton pictures former presidents, forced out by term limits, unhappily roaming the country and longing to return to power.
:::

:::vocab
Peculation: Stealing public money, especially by an official in charge of it; embezzlement.
Usurpation: Seizing power illegally.
Emoluments: Salary and other payments from an office.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's "discontented ghosts" image is memorable, but history mostly didn't bear it out. Many former presidents have lived quiet lives, written memoirs, done charity work, or served in other roles. John Quincy Adams served in the House of Representatives after his presidency, and William Howard Taft became Chief Justice of the Supreme Court. A few tried to return to the White House. Grover Cleveland succeeded, winning non-consecutive terms in 1884 and 1892, and Donald Trump did the same in 2016 and 2024. Theodore Roosevelt ran again in 1912 and lost.

His worry that a term-limited leader might try to hold onto power has been a real problem in many other countries. Leaders facing term limits have sometimes changed their constitutions or refused to leave office.
:::

## Harm 3: losing experience {orig=8}

A third bad effect of exclusion would be depriving the community of the experience the president gains while in office. That "experience is the parent of wisdom" is a saying that both the wisest and the simplest people recognize as true. What quality is more desirable or more essential in the leaders of nations? And where more than in the nation's top leader?

Can it be wise to have the Constitution ban this desirable and essential quality? To declare that the moment someone gains it, he must be forced to leave the office where he gained it and where it is most useful? Yet that is exactly what all rules do that bar people from serving their country, by the choice of their fellow citizens, after their service has made them more capable of doing it well.

:::quote
That experience is the parent of wisdom, is an adage the truth of which is recognized by the wisest as well as the simplest of mankind.

Meaning: Everyone, from the wisest scholar to the most ordinary person, knows that experience teaches wisdom.
:::

## Harm 4: losing the right leader in a crisis {orig=9}

A fourth bad effect of exclusion would be banishing people from offices where, in certain emergencies, their presence might be extremely important to the public interest or safety. Every nation has, at some point, absolutely needed the services of particular people in particular situations, perhaps even to survive.

So how unwise must be any self-denying rule that forbids a nation from using its own citizens in the way best suited to its needs and circumstances! Even without assuming any one man is indispensable, it's clear that replacing the president at the outbreak of a war, or in a similar crisis, with someone else, even someone equally capable, would always hurt the community. It would replace experience with inexperience, and it would tend to unsettle and disrupt the administration's established course.

:::commentary
This argument was tested in 1940. With World War II raging in Europe, Franklin D. Roosevelt broke Washington's two-term tradition and ran for a third term. Many voters agreed with Hamilton's reasoning: this was no time to change leaders. Roosevelt won a third term in 1940 and a fourth in 1944, then died in office in 1945. Afterward, concern about one person holding the presidency for so long led to the 22nd Amendment.
:::

## Harm 5: built-in instability {orig=10-11}

A fifth bad effect of exclusion would be that it would work as a constitutional ban on stability in the administration. By forcing a change of the person in the nation's top office, it would force a change of policies. We can't generally expect the people to change while the policies stay the same. The opposite is the normal course of things.

We don't need to worry that there will be too much stability, as long as the people can still choose to make a change. And we shouldn't want to forbid the people from continuing their trust where they think it can safely be placed, and where, by staying steady, they can avoid the serious problems of shifting leadership and changing policy.

These are some of the disadvantages that would come from the principle of exclusion. They apply most strongly to permanent exclusion. But even a partial exclusion would always make a return to office distant and uncertain. So these points apply almost as fully to one as to the other.

## The supposed benefits don't hold up {orig=12-14}

What benefits are promised to balance these disadvantages? Supporters claim two: first, more independence for the president; second, more security for the people.

Unless the exclusion is permanent, there's no reason to expect the first benefit. And even if it is permanent, couldn't he have some goal beyond his current office, for which he might sacrifice his independence? Couldn't he have friends and connections for whom he might sacrifice it? And mightn't he be *less* willing to make personal enemies by acting firmly, knowing that a time is fast approaching when he not only MAY but MUST face their anger as an equal, or perhaps as someone with less power than they have? It's not easy to say whether this arrangement would increase or decrease his independence.

There's even more reason to doubt the second supposed benefit. If the exclusion were permanent, a man of reckless ambition, the only kind there would be any reason to fear, would be extremely reluctant to leave forever a position where his love of power and status had become a habit. And if he had been lucky or clever enough to win the people's affection, he might persuade them to see the rule as a hateful and unfair restriction on *themselves*, since it would stop them from showing their loyalty to a favorite again.

We can imagine situations where the people's anger at this rule, combined with the frustrated ambition of such a favorite, might put liberty in greater danger than anyone could reasonably fear from a president staying in office through the free votes of the people, using a right the Constitution gives them.

There is too much cleverness in the idea of preventing the people from keeping in office leaders who, in their opinion, have earned their approval and trust. Its advantages are speculative and uncertain at best, and they are outweighed by disadvantages that are far more certain and serious.

:::commentary
Hamilton's final point is that term limits restrict the *voters*, not just the president. If the people want to keep a leader, why should the Constitution stop them? That argument is still made against term limits for Congress and other offices.

The other side has strong arguments too. Supporters of term limits say they prevent any one person from building up too much power, bring in fresh ideas, and protect against a leader who uses the advantages of office to keep winning. The two-term limit for presidents is now widely accepted in the United States, and many other democracies have similar rules.
:::

:::today
The 22nd Amendment settled the question for presidents, but the debate over term limits continues. Many states limit how long governors and state legislators can serve. Members of Congress have no term limits, and proposals to add them come up regularly. Supporters say term limits fight corruption and keep politicians in touch with ordinary life. Opponents use Hamilton's arguments: they waste experience, shift power to lobbyists and staff who stay longer, and take choices away from voters. Hamilton's prediction that new presidents would sweep out their predecessors' officials and reverse their policies has also proved true.
:::
