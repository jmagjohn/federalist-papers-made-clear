:::summary
Hamilton covers two of the ingredients of a strong executive. The first is a secure salary. Congress sets the president's pay once, and cannot raise or cut it during the term, so it can neither bribe nor starve the president into obeying it. The second is "competent powers," starting with the veto. The veto lets the president defend the office against a legislature that tends to grab power, and it gives the country an extra check against hasty or unjust laws. Hamilton expects the veto to be used too rarely rather than too often. He argues that a *qualified* veto, which two-thirds of both houses can override, is better than an absolute one, because a president will be more willing to use it.
:::

:::keypoints
- "A power over a man's support is a power over his will." If Congress could change the president's pay at will, the president would be its servant.
- The Constitution fixes the president's salary for the whole term and forbids any **other payment** from the national government or the states.
- The **veto** is the president's shield against a legislature that tends to take over the powers of the other branches.
- It is also a check on **bad laws** passed through haste, faction, or carelessness.
- The veto doesn't assume the president is wiser than Congress. It assumes Congress is **not infallible**.
- Blocking a few good laws is a fair price for stopping many bad ones, since **unstable laws** are a great weakness of American governments.
- A president will more likely be **too timid** with the veto than too bold. Even the British king hasn't used his veto in a long time.
- A **qualified veto** is easier to use than an absolute one, and just its threat can stop bad measures before they begin.
- Judges should not share in the veto, as they do in New York, because they must stay **separate** from the executive.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 70](/papers/70/), Hamilton listed four ingredients of "energy in the executive": unity, duration, adequate support, and competent powers. He covered unity in No. 70 and duration (the four-year term and re-election) in Nos. 71 and 72. Now he turns to support, meaning the president's salary, and then to the president's powers, beginning with the veto.

In the 1780s, most state legislatures controlled their governors' pay and could override them easily. Only a few state constitutions gave the executive a veto. In New York, the veto belonged to a "Council of Revision" made up of the governor and the state's top judges. In Massachusetts, the governor alone held a veto that two-thirds of the legislature could override. At the Constitutional Convention, James Madison and James Wilson proposed a council of revision like New York's, joining federal judges with the president. The Convention rejected that idea several times and chose the Massachusetts model.
:::

## A salary Congress cannot touch {orig=1-2}

The third ingredient that gives the executive strength is an adequate provision for its support. Without proper attention to this point, the separation of the executive from the legislature would be in name only. If the legislature had full control over the salary and payments of the Chief Magistrate, it could make him as obedient to its will as it wished. In most cases, it could either starve him or tempt him with gifts until he gave up his own judgment and followed theirs.

Taken in the most extreme sense, these words say more than I intend. There are some men who could not be pressured or bribed into giving up their duty. But this stern virtue grows in few places. In general, we will find that a power over a man's support is a power over his will. If facts were needed to prove so plain a truth, there would be no shortage of examples, even in this country. Legislatures have frightened or tempted executives with threats and promises about their pay.

So it is hard to praise too highly the wise attention the proposed Constitution gives to this subject. It provides that:

> "The President of the United States shall, at stated times, receive for his services a compensation which shall neither be increased nor diminished during the period for which he shall have been elected; and he shall not receive within that period any other emolument from the United States, or any of them."

It is impossible to imagine a better rule than this. When a president is elected, the legislature must declare, once and for all, what he will be paid for his whole term. After that, it has no power to change the amount, up or down, until a new term begins after a new election. It can't weaken his courage by playing on his needs. It can't corrupt his honesty by appealing to his greed. Neither the national government nor any state will be free to give him, and he will not be free to accept, any payment other than the one set by that first act. So he can have no money-based reason to give up the independence the Constitution intends for him.

:::quote
...a power over a man's support is a power over his will.

Meaning: Whoever controls your paycheck can control your choices. That's why Congress can't raise or cut the president's pay during a term.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's "examples... even in this country" refers to colonial times, when assemblies often held back royal governors' salaries to force them to go along with the assembly's wishes. The founders had seen this tactic work.

The salary rule is still in force. Congress set the first president's salary at $25,000 a year in 1789. Today it is $400,000 a year, a level set in 2001. Any raise takes effect only for a later term. The ban on "any other emolument" from the national government or the states is known as the Domestic Emoluments Clause. Its meaning has been debated in recent years, along with a separate clause on payments from foreign governments.
:::

:::vocab
Chief Magistrate: An old term for the president, the highest executive official.
Emolument: Salary or profit from holding an office.
Compensation: Payment for work or services.
:::

## The president's powers begin with the veto {orig=3-4}

The last ingredient of energy on my list is competent powers. Let us now look at the powers the Constitution proposes to give the president.

The first one we notice is the president's qualified negative, or veto, over the acts and resolutions of the two houses of Congress. In other words, he has the power to return any bill with his objections. That stops it from becoming law, unless two-thirds of each house later approve it anyway.

:::vocab
Qualified negative: A veto that can be overridden. Under the Constitution, a two-thirds vote in both houses of Congress overrides the president's veto.
Veto: Latin for "I forbid." The executive's power to reject a bill passed by the legislature.
:::

## A shield for the president and a check on bad laws {orig=5-6}

I have already pointed out, more than once, that the legislature tends to intrude on the rights of the other branches and to absorb their powers. I have also noted that simply drawing the boundaries of each branch on paper is not enough. And I have shown that each branch needs constitutional weapons for its own defense. From these clear and certain principles, it follows that the executive should have a veto over the acts of the legislature, either absolute or qualified.

Without one or the other, the president would be completely unable to defend himself against the legislature's raids. He might be stripped of his powers step by step through a series of resolutions. Or he might be wiped out by a single vote. Either way, the legislative and executive powers could quickly end up in the same hands. Even if the legislature had never shown any desire to invade the rights of the executive, sound reasoning alone would teach us this. One branch should not be left at the mercy of another. Each should have a constitutional and effective power to defend itself.

But the veto has another use. It is not only a shield for the executive. It also gives extra protection against passing improper laws. It is a healthy check on the legislature. It is designed to guard the community against faction, against haste, and against any impulse harmful to the public good that may happen to sway a majority of Congress.

:::commentary
Hamilton is building on Madison's arguments in [No. 48](/papers/48/) and [No. 51](/papers/51/). In No. 48, Madison warned that the legislature is "everywhere extending the sphere of its activity" and that written boundaries are mere "parchment barriers." In No. 51, he said each branch needs "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives" to resist the others. The veto is the president's main tool for this.

Notice that Hamilton gives the veto two jobs: protecting the presidency, and blocking bad laws in general. Early presidents, starting with George Washington, mostly used the veto when they believed a bill was unconstitutional. Andrew Jackson's 1832 veto of a bill to renew the Bank of the United States helped establish that presidents could veto bills simply because they thought them unwise.
:::

:::vocab
Parchment barriers: Madison's phrase for rules written on paper that have no real force to stop a branch from overstepping its powers.
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
:::

## Is one man wiser than many? {orig=7-9}

Some have attacked the veto with this argument. We cannot assume that one man will have more virtue and wisdom than a large group. Unless we assume that, it would be wrong to give the executive any kind of control over the legislature.

When we examine this argument, it turns out to look better than it really is. The case for the veto doesn't depend on the idea that the executive is wiser or more virtuous. It depends on the idea that the legislature is not infallible. The love of power may sometimes lead it to invade the rights of other parts of the government. A spirit of faction may sometimes twist its debates. The feelings of the moment may sometimes rush it into measures that it would condemn itself after more careful thought.

The main reason for giving this power to the executive is to let him defend himself. The second reason is to improve the community's chances against bad laws, whether they come from haste, carelessness, or bad intent. The more often a measure is examined, and the more varied the positions of the people examining it, the smaller the danger of two kinds of errors. One kind comes from a lack of careful debate. The other comes from a shared passion or interest that spreads like a disease. It is far less likely that bad motives of any kind will infect every part of the government at the same moment, about the same issue, than that they will take turns ruling and misleading each part.

It may be said that the power to prevent bad laws also includes the power to prevent good ones. It could be used for either purpose. But this objection will carry little weight with people who understand the harm done by laws that are constantly changing. That instability is the greatest flaw in the character of our governments. These people will see any institution that restrains too much lawmaking, and keeps things as they are at a given time, as much more likely to do good than harm, because it encourages more stable laws. Any harm done by defeating a few good laws will be more than made up for by preventing a number of bad ones.

:::quote
The injury which may possibly be done by defeating a few good laws, will be amply compensated by the advantage of preventing a number of bad ones.

Meaning: Hamilton admits the veto will sometimes block good laws, but he thinks stopping bad ones is worth that cost.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's answer is a classic argument for checks and balances. It's not that the president is better than Congress. It's that a bill has to win the approval of more people in more positions before it becomes law, so mistakes are more likely to be caught.

His comment about "inconstancy and mutability in the laws" reflects the 1780s, when state legislatures often passed, changed, and repealed laws quickly, including laws on debts and paper money. Madison made the same complaint in [No. 62](/papers/62/). Whether a system that makes new laws hard to pass is a good thing is still debated. Supporters see stability and protection against rash decisions. Critics see gridlock and a bias in favor of whatever already exists.
:::

:::vocab
Infallibility: Being incapable of making mistakes.
Specious: Seeming true or reasonable at first, but actually wrong.
:::

## The veto will be used too little, not too much {orig=10-12}

That is not all. In a free government, the legislature has greater weight and influence than the executive, and the executive takes a risk in any test of strength with it. This gives us good reason to believe the veto will usually be used with great caution. People will more often accuse a president of being too timid with it than too reckless.

Consider the king of Great Britain. He has all his royal privileges and draws influence from a thousand sources. Yet today he would hesitate to veto a measure passed by both houses of Parliament. He would use every bit of his influence to kill a bill he disliked before it reached the throne. That way he would avoid a hard choice: either letting it take effect, or angering the nation by opposing the will of the legislature. And he probably would not finally dare to use his royal power except in a case where it was clearly proper or extremely necessary. All well-informed people in that kingdom will agree with this. It has been a long time since the crown used its veto.

If a ruler as powerful and well protected as a British king would hesitate to use this power, how much more cautious should we expect a president of the United States to be? He would hold executive power for only four years, in a government that is completely republican.

Clearly, the greater danger is that he won't use the veto when he should, not that he will use it too often or too much. In fact, some have used this very point to argue against the veto. They call it a power that looks hateful but is useless in practice. But just because it might be used rarely, it doesn't follow that it would never be used.

The veto is mainly meant for a direct attack on the constitutional rights of the executive. It is also meant for cases where the public good is clearly and obviously being sacrificed. In such cases, a man of reasonable firmness would use his constitutional means of defense. He would listen to the call of duty and responsibility. In the first case, his courage would be driven by his own interest in the power of his office. In the second, it would be driven by the likely support of the voters. In a doubtful case, the people would naturally lean toward the legislature. But they would hardly let that bias fool them in a very plain case. Here I am thinking of a president with only an ordinary amount of firmness. There are some men who will have the courage to do their duty in any situation, whatever the risk.

:::commentary
Hamilton was right about Britain. The last time a British monarch vetoed an act of Parliament was in 1708, when Queen Anne refused to approve a bill about the Scottish militia. No monarch has done it since.

His prediction about the presidency was less accurate. Presidents have not been nearly as shy about the veto as Hamilton expected. Franklin Roosevelt alone issued more than 600 vetoes. But Hamilton's deeper point held up: Congress overrides vetoes only rarely, so the veto has become one of the president's strongest tools.
:::

:::vocab
Prerogative: A special right or power that belongs to an office, especially to a king.
Constituents: The people an elected official represents.
:::

## Why a qualified veto works better than an absolute one {orig=13}

But the Convention chose a middle path. It makes the power easier for the president to use, while also making its success depend on the views of a large part of Congress. Instead of an absolute veto, the Constitution gives the president the qualified veto I described.

A president will be much more willing to use this power than the other. A man who might be afraid to kill a law by his single VETO might not hesitate to send it back for another look. The bill would finally fail only if more than one-third of each house agreed that his objections were good enough. He would be encouraged by the thought that if he won, a very respectable share of Congress would be on his side. Their influence would join his in defending his conduct before the public.

A direct and flat refusal looks harsher and is more likely to cause anger than simply offering reasoned objections for others to accept or reject. Because a qualified veto is less likely to offend, it is more likely to be used. For that very reason, it may turn out to be more effective in practice.

We may hope that bad motives will not often control as many as two-thirds of both houses at the same time, especially with the president's weight pushing the other way. In any case, that is far less likely than bad motives controlling a bare majority.

A power like this will often work silently and unseen, but with great force. When men pursuing wrong goals know that they may be blocked by someone they cannot control, the mere fear of opposition will often hold them back. Without that fear, they would rush eagerly into those plans.

:::commentary
Hamilton's last point describes what political scientists today call the "veto threat." Presidents often announce that they will veto a bill unless it is changed. Members of Congress then rewrite the bill to avoid a veto they can't override. So much of the veto's power is never seen in the official count of vetoes.

The Constitution also allows what is called a "pocket veto." If Congress adjourns and so prevents the president from returning a bill within ten days (not counting Sundays), the bill does not become law, and Congress gets no chance to override. Congress once tried to give presidents a "line-item veto" to cancel parts of spending bills, but in *Clinton v. City of New York* (1998), the Supreme Court ruled that this violated the Constitution's process for making laws.
:::

:::quote
When men, engaged in unjustifiable pursuits, are aware that obstructions may come from a quarter which they cannot control, they will often be restrained by the bare apprehension of opposition...

Meaning: Just knowing that the president might veto a bill can stop Congress from passing a bad one in the first place.
:::

## Why judges should not share the veto {orig=14-15}

As I have noted elsewhere, in this state the qualified veto belongs to a council made up of the governor, the chancellor, and the judges of the state Supreme Court, or any two of them. It has been used freely on many occasions, often with success. Its usefulness has become so clear that some who fiercely opposed it when the state constitution was being written have become its open admirers, based on experience.

I have also remarked that the Convention, in shaping this part of the plan, departed from the model of New York's constitution and followed that of Massachusetts instead. We can imagine two strong reasons for this choice. First, judges are the ones who interpret the laws. They might be improperly biased if they had already given an opinion on a law while reviewing it as part of the council. Second, by working closely with the executive so often, they might be drawn too far into his political goals. Then a dangerous alliance could slowly form between the executive and the judiciary. Judges can't be kept too separate from every duty except explaining the laws. It is especially dangerous to put them in a position where the executive could corrupt or influence them.

:::note A note on the original text
Hamilton's footnote names one of the former opponents who came to admire New York's council: Abraham Yates, a leading New York Anti-Federalist who opposed the proposed U.S. Constitution.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's earlier remark about New York and Massachusetts is in [No. 69](/papers/69/). His concern about judges is an early version of a principle that still matters: judges should decide cases after a law is passed, not help write or approve it beforehand. That is why federal courts today refuse to give "advisory opinions" on bills or on questions that are not part of a real case. Hamilton expands on judicial independence in [No. 78](/papers/78/).
:::

:::vocab
Chancellor: In New York at the time, the judge who headed the Court of Chancery, a court that decided cases based on fairness rather than strict legal rules.
Judiciary: The branch of government made up of courts and judges.
:::

:::today
The veto is now a central part of how laws get made. Presidents of both parties use it, and the threat of it shapes bills long before they reach the president's desk. Overrides are rare, because two-thirds of both houses seldom agree against a president. Some people say the veto gives presidents too much power over lawmaking, especially when Congress is closely divided. Others say it does exactly what Hamilton intended: it forces broad agreement before major laws are passed and protects the presidency from a powerful Congress. The salary rule also still works quietly in the background. No Congress can punish or reward a sitting president by changing his pay.
:::
