:::summary
This short essay covers three of the president's powers. First, the president is commander in chief of the armed forces. Hamilton thinks this needs little defense, because directing a war, more than any other task, calls for the leadership of one person. Second, the president may ask the heads of the executive departments for their written opinions, which Hamilton calls unnecessary to state, since the power comes with the office. Third, the president may grant pardons. Hamilton argues that one person, who alone bears the responsibility, will use mercy more carefully than a group. He even defends letting the president pardon treason, because a quick, well-timed pardon can sometimes end a rebellion.
:::

:::keypoints
- The president is **commander in chief** of the army, the navy, and the state militias when they are called into national service.
- Even states that made their governors share power with a council usually gave **military command to the governor alone**.
- Directing war means directing the nation's combined strength, and that calls for **one person in charge**.
- The president's right to ask department heads for **written opinions** is, in Hamilton's view, a "mere redundancy."
- Criminal laws are necessarily harsh, so a **pardon power** is needed to keep justice from becoming cruel.
- One person who feels **sole responsibility** will weigh mercy more carefully than a group, whose members can hide behind each other.
- Some critics wanted Congress to approve **pardons for treason**. Hamilton admits there are good reasons for that, but argues that in a rebellion, a **well-timed offer of pardon** may restore peace, and waiting for Congress could waste the chance.
:::

This is one of Hamilton's essays on the presidency, which run from [No. 67](/papers/67/) to [No. 77](/papers/77/). In [No. 70](/papers/70/) he argued that the executive should be one person, not a council, and in [No. 73](/papers/73/) he defended the veto. Here he moves quickly through three powers listed in Article II, Section 2 of the Constitution.

Shays' Rebellion was fresh in every reader's mind. In 1786 and early 1787, armed farmers in western Massachusetts, many of them deep in debt, shut down courts to stop the seizure of their farms. The state raised a militia army to put down the uprising. Hamilton refers to it at the end of this essay.

## The president commands the armed forces {orig=1}

The president of the United States is to be "commander-in-chief of the army and navy of the United States, and of the militia of the several States when called into the actual service of the United States." This rule makes obvious sense. It also matches the state constitutions closely. So little needs to be said to explain or defend it. Even those state constitutions that otherwise made the governor share power with a council have mostly given military authority to him alone.

Of all the duties of government, the direction of war most especially requires the qualities that come from power held by a single person. Directing a war means directing the combined strength of the nation. And the power to direct and use that combined strength is a normal and essential part of what we mean by executive authority.

:::quote
Of all the cares or concerns of government, the direction of war most peculiarly demands those qualities which distinguish the exercise of power by a single hand.

Meaning: In war, a country needs one leader who can make decisions quickly and firmly, not a committee.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton treats this power as the easiest to defend, and he says little about its limits. In [No. 69](/papers/69/), he explained that the president's military power would be much smaller than the British king's. The king could declare war and raise armies. Under the Constitution, those powers belong to Congress. The president commands the forces that Congress creates and pays for.

In practice, the line between Congress's power to declare war and the president's power as commander in chief has been one of the longest-running debates in American government. Congress last formally declared war during World War II. Since then, presidents of both parties have sent troops into major conflicts, such as Korea and Vietnam, without a declaration of war. In 1973, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over President Nixon's veto to require presidents to consult and report to Congress. Presidents have often questioned whether that law is constitutional. Supporters of broad presidential power point to Hamilton's argument for speed and unity in war. Critics point out that the founders gave Congress the power to decide whether the nation goes to war at all.
:::

:::vocab
Commander in chief: The top commander of the armed forces. Under the Constitution, this is the president.
Militia: Ordinary citizens who are trained to serve as soldiers when needed, rather than full-time professional soldiers.
:::

## Written opinions from department heads {orig=2}

"The President may require the opinion, in writing, of the principal officer in each of the executive departments, upon any subject relating to the duties of their respective offices." I consider this simply unnecessary in the plan, because the right it provides would come with the office anyway.

:::commentary
This clause is the only place where the Constitution hints at what we now call the president's **cabinet**. The word "cabinet" never appears in the Constitution. George Washington began meeting regularly with his department heads, such as Thomas Jefferson (secretary of state) and Hamilton himself (secretary of the treasury), and later presidents continued the practice.

The clause also fits Hamilton's argument in No. 70. Department heads give advice, but the president is not bound by it. Decisions and responsibility stay with one person.
:::

:::vocab
Redundancy: Something unnecessary because it repeats what is already there.
:::

## Why one person should hold the power to pardon {orig=3}

The president is also authorized to grant "reprieves and pardons for offenses against the United States, except in cases of impeachment." Both kindness and good policy tell us that this merciful power should be limited as little as possible. Every country's criminal laws are necessarily severe. Without an easy way to make exceptions for people whose guilt deserves sympathy, justice would look too bloody and cruel.

A sense of responsibility is always strongest when it is not shared. So we may conclude that a single man would pay the most attention to reasons for softening the harshness of the law. He would also be the least likely to give in to arguments meant to protect someone who truly deserves punishment. Knowing that a fellow human being's fate depended on his decision alone would naturally make him careful and cautious. At the same time, his fear of being accused of weakness, or of secretly helping a guilty person, would make him just as careful in a different way.

Groups of men, on the other hand, generally gain confidence from their numbers. They might often encourage each other to be harsh and unforgiving. They might also worry less about being suspected or blamed for showing mercy unwisely, or for faking mercy for some hidden purpose. For these reasons, one man seems better suited than a group to hand out the government's mercy.

:::commentary
Under the Constitution, the pardon power is very broad. In *Ex parte Garland* (1866), the Supreme Court said the power reaches every federal offense and can be used before, during, or after a trial. But it has limits written into the text. It covers only federal crimes ("offenses against the United States"), not state crimes. And it cannot stop an impeachment or undo a conviction by the Senate.

Some pardons have been very controversial. In 1974, President Gerald Ford pardoned Richard Nixon for any federal crimes he may have committed while in office. Ford said it would help the nation move past the Watergate scandal, while critics said it put a former president above the law. Other presidents of both parties have also faced criticism for pardons given to political allies, friends, or family members. Defenders of the power say it is an important way to correct injustice and show mercy. Critics argue it can be abused. Courts have never decided whether a president could pardon himself.
:::

:::vocab
Pardon: An official act forgiving a crime and canceling its punishment.
Reprieve: A delay in carrying out a punishment.
Prerogative: A special right or power that belongs to an office, especially to a king.
Clemency: Mercy shown by someone in power, such as a pardon or a shortened sentence.
:::

## Should the president be able to pardon treason? {orig=4}

If I am not mistaken, the only challenge to giving the president the pardon power has concerned the crime of treason. Some have argued that pardons for treason should require the approval of one or both houses of Congress. I won't deny that there are strong reasons for requiring Congress, or part of it, to agree in this case. Treason is a crime aimed at the very existence of society. So once the courts have established the offender's guilt, it seems fitting to let the legislature judge whether mercy should be shown. This makes even more sense because we can't completely rule out the possibility that the president himself was secretly involved.

But there are also strong objections to such a plan. In delicate situations, a single man of good judgment is surely better than any large group at weighing the reasons for and against canceling a punishment.

Here is a point that deserves special attention. Treason will often be tied to uprisings that involve a large part of the community. This recently happened in Massachusetts. In every such case, we might expect the people's representatives to be infected with the same spirit that caused the crime. When the parties were fairly evenly matched, the condemned person's friends and supporters might quietly take advantage of the good nature and weakness of others. They might often let the guilty go unpunished when a frightening example was needed. But suppose the uprising came from causes that had angered the majority party. Then that party might prove stubborn and unforgiving, even when wise policy called for patience and mercy.

But the main argument for giving the president the power to pardon in this case is this. In times of uprising or rebellion, there are often critical moments when a well-timed offer of pardon to the rebels may restore peace to the country. If that moment passes and is not used, it may never come back. The slow process of calling Congress, or one of its houses, into session to get its approval would often let that golden opportunity slip away. The loss of a week, a day, or even an hour may sometimes be fatal.

Someone might suggest that Congress could give the president a special power to pardon in emergencies like these. There are two answers. First, it is doubtful whether, under a limited Constitution, such a power could be handed over by law. Second, it would usually be unwise to take any step in advance that might hold out the hope of escaping punishment. An unusual step like that would probably be seen as a sign of fear or weakness, and it would tend to encourage the guilty.

:::quote
...in seasons of insurrection or rebellion, there are often critical moments, when a well-timed offer of pardon to the insurgents or rebels may restore the tranquillity of the commonwealth...

Meaning: Sometimes the fastest way to end a rebellion is to promise the rebels that they won't be punished if they lay down their arms. The president can make that offer at once.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction was soon tested. During the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794, farmers in western Pennsylvania rose up against a federal tax on whiskey, a tax Hamilton himself had designed as secretary of the treasury. Washington sent a large militia force, and the rebellion collapsed. Two men were convicted of treason, and Washington pardoned them both. A few years later, President John Adams pardoned John Fries, who had been sentenced to death for leading a revolt in Pennsylvania against a federal property tax. After the Civil War, President Andrew Johnson granted a broad amnesty to former Confederates. These are all examples of the use Hamilton had in mind: mercy as a way to end a conflict.

Hamilton also admits a real risk: a president might pardon people who had acted with him or for him. At Virginia's ratifying convention in 1788, George Mason raised this fear. James Madison answered that a president who used pardons to shelter his own accomplices could be impeached.
:::

:::vocab
Treason: Under the Constitution, waging war against the United States or giving aid and comfort to its enemies.
Sedition: Speech or action that stirs up rebellion against the government.
Insurrection: A violent uprising against the government; a rebellion.
Impunity: Freedom from punishment.
Amnesty: A pardon given to a whole group of people at once.
:::

:::today
Both powers in this essay remain at the center of modern debates. As commander in chief, presidents have ordered military action in many places without a declaration of war, and people still argue about how much Congress must be involved. The pardon power also makes news, especially at the end of a president's term, when many presidents have issued large numbers of pardons and commutations. Some people have proposed limiting the pardon power, for example by constitutional amendment. Others argue that Hamilton was right to leave it broad, and that the voters and the threat of impeachment are the proper checks on its abuse.
:::
