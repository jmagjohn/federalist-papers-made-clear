:::summary
Critics of the Constitution charged that the House of Representatives would be too small to know enough about the people it represents. Madison answers that a representative needs to know only about the subjects he actually makes laws on. For Congress, the main subjects that need local knowledge are **trade, taxes, and the militia**, and a few well-chosen members from each state can supply what is needed, especially since many will have served in their state legislatures. The harder task is learning about the *other* states, and that is a matter of study, not numbers. Madison closes with Great Britain: even though a large share of the House of Commons is chosen by tiny groups of voters, Britain has kept much of its freedom. So one representative for every thirty thousand people will be enough.
:::

:::keypoints
- The **second charge** against the House: it will be **too small** to know the interests of the people it represents.
- A representative needs to know only about matters **within his lawmaking power**, not every small local detail.
- The national subjects that most require local knowledge are **commerce, taxation, and the militia**.
- **Trade:** a few representatives can easily explain each state's laws and situation.
- **Taxes:** each state's own tax laws, written by members from every part of the state, will be a ready guide. Many members of Congress will also have served in their **state legislatures**.
- **Militia:** almost no local knowledge is needed, because military principles apply everywhere.
- Each state's interests are fairly uniform inside it. The real challenge is learning about **the other states**.
- **Great Britain** shows that a much smaller real representation can still protect liberty, so **one representative per 30,000 people** is safe.
:::

:::context Background
Nos. 55–58 answer the Anti-Federalists' complaints about the size of the House of Representatives. The Constitution said the House could have no more than one member for every 30,000 people, and it set the first House at just 65 members until a census could be taken. Many critics thought this was far too few to represent a country of more than three million people. In [No. 55](/papers/55/), Madison answered the first charge, that so small a body couldn't be trusted with power. Here he answers the second: that it wouldn't know enough about the people.
:::

## The second charge {orig=1-2}

The second charge against the House of Representatives is that it will be too small to have proper knowledge of the interests of its constituents.

This objection clearly comes from comparing the number of representatives with the great size of the United States, its large population, and the variety of its interests. But it ignores the features that will make Congress different from other legislatures. So the best answer is a brief explanation of those special features.

:::vocab
Constituents: The people a representative is elected to represent.
:::

## Know what you make laws about {orig=3-4}

It is a sound and important principle that a representative should be familiar with the interests and circumstances of his constituents. But this principle can extend only to the interests and circumstances that fall under the representative's authority and care. He can be ignorant of many small, particular matters that lie outside the reach of lawmaking, and still have every quality he needs to do his job well. So to decide how much information an official needs, we must look at the subjects his authority covers.

What will be the subjects of federal lawmaking? The most important ones, and the ones that seem to require the most local knowledge, are commerce, taxation, and the militia.

:::commentary
This is the key move in Madison's argument. A state legislator might need to know about every local road, court, and school. But Congress has only certain listed powers. So members of Congress need to know about only those subjects. A smaller body can handle a narrower job.

This reasoning depends on the national government staying limited. Today, Congress deals with far more subjects than in 1788, including health care, education, and the environment. Some people argue that this makes Madison's case for a small House weaker now.
:::

:::vocab
Militia: A military force made up of ordinary citizens, rather than professional soldiers, who train part-time and can be called up in emergencies.
:::

## Trade {orig=5}

Regulating commerce properly requires a lot of information, as has been pointed out elsewhere. But as far as that information concerns the laws and local situation of each state, a very few representatives would be more than enough to bring it to the national government.

## Taxes {orig=6}

Taxes will consist largely of duties on imports, which will be tied up with the regulation of commerce. So my last point applies here too. As far as taxes may be collected inside the country, a broader knowledge of each state's circumstances may be needed. But won't a very few intelligent men, elected from different parts of the state, have enough of that knowledge too? Divide the largest state into ten or twelve districts. You will find that each district has no special local interests that its representative won't know about.

Besides this source of information, the state's own laws, written by representatives from every part of it, will almost be a sufficient guide by themselves. Every state has made, and will keep making, rules on this subject. In many cases, these will leave Congress little to do except review the different state laws and combine them into one general law. A skilled person working alone in his study, with all the local laws in front of him, could write a national law on some kinds of taxes without any spoken advice at all. And we can expect that whenever internal taxes are needed, especially where they must be uniform across the states, simpler things will be chosen to tax.

The state laws will be a great help in this part of federal lawmaking. To see how much, just imagine that this state, or any other, were divided into several parts, and each part could make its own local laws. Their records would be full of local information and work already done. Isn't it clear that this would greatly shorten the work of the state legislature, and let it get by with far fewer members?

Congress will benefit greatly from one more fact. Each state's representatives will bring with them a good knowledge of its laws and of their own districts. But beyond that, they will probably in all cases have been members of the state legislature, and may even be members at the same time. All the local information and interests of the state come together in the state legislature. From there, a very few people can easily carry them into the legislature of the United States.

:::commentary
"Duties" are taxes on imported goods, also called tariffs. Madison expected these to be the main source of federal money, and for most of the 1800s they were. "Internal" taxes are taxes collected inside the country, such as taxes on land or on goods made at home.

Madison's point about state legislators turned out to be true. Many early members of Congress had served in their state legislatures. Madison himself had served in the Virginia legislature before he was elected to the first House of Representatives in 1789.
:::

:::vocab
Duties: Taxes on goods brought into or sent out of a country. Also called tariffs or customs.
:::

## The militia {orig=7-8}

What I have said about taxes applies even more strongly to the militia. The rules of military training may differ from state to state, but they are the same throughout each state. They depend on conditions that can differ only a little from one part of a state to another.

In fact, when it comes to regulating the militia, local knowledge is hardly necessary at all. Almost the only local question is the general nature of the land: whether it is mountainous or flat, and whether it is better suited to troops on foot or on horseback. The art of war teaches general principles of organization, movement, and discipline that apply everywhere.

:::note
This section covers two paragraphs that appear in different early editions of the Federalist. They are two versions of the same thought about the militia, so we have combined them.
:::

## Knowing your own state, and learning the others {orig=9}

The careful reader will see that my argument here does not contradict what I said earlier about the wide knowledge representatives should have, and the time they might need to gain it. Local knowledge is necessary and hard to gain, not because laws and conditions differ *within* a single state, but because they differ *between* the states.

Taken one at a time, each state has the same laws throughout, and its interests vary only a little. So a few men will have all the knowledge needed to represent it properly. If each state's interests and affairs were perfectly simple and uniform, knowing them in one part would mean knowing them in every part. Then the whole state could be represented well by a single member from anywhere in it.

But when we compare the states with one another, we find great differences in their laws, and in many other matters connected with federal lawmaking. Federal representatives should know something about all of these. So while a few representatives from each state can bring enough knowledge of their own state, every representative will have a great deal to learn about all the other states.

As I remarked before, the passage of time will make the states more alike compared with one another. But inside each state, time will have the opposite effect. Right now, some states are little more than communities of farmers. Few have made much progress in the kinds of industry that make a nation's affairs varied and complex. But these will develop in every state as its population grows, and then each state will need fuller representation. The Convention wisely made sure that as the population grows, the House of Representatives will grow along with it.

:::commentary
Madison is answering a possible charge that he is contradicting himself. In [No. 53](/papers/53/), he argued that representatives need a lot of knowledge and time to learn their jobs, which was one reason for two-year terms. Here he says a few members are enough. His answer: knowing your *own* state is easy, but learning about *twelve other* states takes time and study. Adding more members from each state wouldn't solve that.

His prediction about the future also came true. As the country grew and industrialized, each state's economy became more complex, with factories, railroads, and cities alongside farms. The census added seats to the House every ten years, as he expected. See [No. 58](/papers/58/) for more on how the House would grow.
:::

:::vocab
Husbandmen: Farmers.
:::

## The lesson of Great Britain {orig=10}

The experience of Great Britain, which teaches so many political lessons, both as a warning and as an example, and which we have often looked to in these essays, confirms what I have just argued.

The population of the two kingdoms of England and Scotland can't be less than eight million. Their representatives in the House of Commons number 558. Of these, one-ninth are elected by just 364 people, and half by just 5,723 people.

The half elected this way don't even live among the people at large. We can't suppose that they do anything to protect the people against the government. Nor do they add to the legislature's knowledge of the people's needs and interests. On the contrary, everyone knows that they are more often the agents and tools of the king than the guardians and defenders of the people's rights. So it would be fair to count them as worse than nothing. But we will simply subtract them from the true representatives of the nation. We won't subtract the many others who don't live among their constituents, are only loosely connected with them, and know very little about their affairs.

Even with all these allowances, only 279 people will be responsible for the safety, interests, and happiness of eight million. In other words, one representative will have to defend the rights and explain the situation of TWENTY-EIGHT THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND SEVENTY constituents. And he will sit in an assembly exposed to the full force of the king's influence. That assembly makes laws on every subject, in a nation whose affairs are very varied and complex.

Yet it is quite certain that a valuable share of freedom has been preserved in Britain under all these conditions. And only a very small part of the flaws in British law can be blamed on the legislature's ignorance of the people's circumstances. Let's give this example the weight it deserves and compare it with the House of Representatives as I have described it. Then we can be fully confident that one representative for every THIRTY THOUSAND INHABITANTS will make the House both a safe and a capable guardian of the interests placed in its care.

:::note
Madison's figures on British elections come from *Political Disquisitions*, a book by the British writer James Burgh that criticized the unfair way Parliament was elected.
:::

:::commentary
In the 1700s, many seats in the House of Commons came from "rotten boroughs," places with very few voters, where a wealthy landowner or the government could control the result. The government used jobs, pensions, and other favors to win the support of many members. That is what Madison means by "executive influence." Americans in the Revolutionary era saw this as a sign of British corruption.

Madison's argument is clever. Even if you count only the "real" representatives in the Commons, each one represents nearly 29,000 people, about the same as the House's limit of one per 30,000. And Britain is still fairly free. But notice the leap: the comparison shows that a ratio like this *can* work, not that it is the best choice. A critic could answer that Britain's flawed Parliament was exactly the kind of government Americans had rebelled against.
:::

:::today
The House has grown far beyond what the Anti-Federalists feared. It has had 435 members since the 1910s (apart from a brief increase after Alaska and Hawaii became states), and Congress kept that number by law in 1929. But the country's population has grown much faster. Today each member represents more than 750,000 people, about 25 times Madison's ratio of one per 30,000. Some people argue that the House should be made much larger so that members can know their constituents better, which is close to the Anti-Federalists' original complaint. Others say a much larger House would be hard to manage and would give more power to party leaders. Madison's question is still being asked: how many representatives does it take to truly represent the people?
:::
