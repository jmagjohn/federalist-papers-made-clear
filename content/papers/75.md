:::summary
Hamilton defends the rule that the president makes treaties "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate," with two-thirds of the senators present agreeing. He argues that the treaty power is neither purely legislative nor purely executive. Treaties are deals with foreign nations, which call for the president's skill at negotiating. But they also have the force of law, which calls for a share of the legislature. Giving the power to the president alone would be unsafe, because an elected leader serving a short term might be bribed or tempted by foreign powers. Giving it to the Senate alone would waste the president's advantages and lose the extra security of having two bodies agree. The House is too large and changes too often to handle treaties. Finally, Hamilton defends requiring two-thirds of senators *present* rather than two-thirds of *all* senators.
:::

:::keypoints
- The president makes treaties with the **advice and consent of the Senate**, and **two-thirds** of senators present must agree.
- Hamilton calls this one of the **best-designed** parts of the plan.
- The treaty power is a **separate kind of power**. Treaties are **contracts** between nations that also have the **force of law**.
- The **president** is best suited to negotiate, but the importance of treaties and their effect as laws call for the **legislature's** involvement.
- A **president alone** could be corrupted by foreign powers, since he serves only four years and must return to private life.
- The **Senate alone** would lose the president's skill, standing, and extra protection.
- The **House** is too large and changes too often to have the knowledge, steadiness, secrecy, and speed that treaties require.
- Requiring two-thirds of **all** senators would let **absences** block treaties and could amount to requiring everyone to agree.
:::

:::context Background
Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress alone made treaties, and nine of the thirteen states had to agree. The new Constitution split the power between the president and the Senate. Critics attacked this from several sides. Some said it mixed executive and legislative powers, against the principle of separation of powers. Others said too few people could approve a treaty. Some wanted the House of Representatives to have a say. Many Southerners and Westerners feared the Senate might give away American rights to navigate the Mississippi River in a treaty with Spain. A proposal along those lines had caused a bitter fight in Congress in 1786.

John Jay, who had been the nation's chief diplomat, defended the treaty power in [No. 64](/papers/64/). Here Hamilton adds some further points.
:::

## The treaty power and its critics {orig=1}

The president is to have power, "by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, to make treaties, provided two thirds of the senators present concur."

This provision has been attacked, on different grounds, with great force. Still, I don't hesitate to say that I firmly believe it is one of the best thought out and least objectionable parts of the plan.

The objections fall into two groups. The first is the tired old complaint about mixing powers. Some say the president alone should have the power to make treaties. Others say it should have been given only to the Senate.

The second objection is that too few people can make a treaty. Some who hold this view think the House of Representatives should have been included. Others seem to think it would have been enough to require two-thirds of *all* the members of the Senate, instead of two-thirds of those present.

I hope the remarks in an earlier paper on this part of the plan have already shown it, to a thoughtful reader, in a very favorable light. So here I will only add a few more remarks, mainly about the objections I have just listed.

:::vocab
Treaty: A formal written agreement between nations.
Advice and consent: The Senate's role in approving treaties (by a two-thirds vote) and appointments (by a majority).
Trite: Overused and stale.
:::

## Neither a law nor an executive act {orig=2}

About the mixing of powers, I will rely on the explanations I have given elsewhere of what that rule truly means. I will take it as proven by them that joining the president with the Senate on treaties doesn't break that rule. I would go further. The particular nature of the treaty power makes this union especially fitting.

Several writers on government place the treaty power among executive powers. But this is plainly an arbitrary choice. If we look carefully at how the power works, we find that it is more like legislative power than executive power, although it doesn't seem to fit exactly into either one.

The heart of legislative power is to make laws, or in other words, to set rules for governing society. The work of the executive seems to consist of carrying out the laws and using the nation's common strength, either for that purpose or for the common defense. The treaty power is plainly neither of these. It has nothing to do with carrying out existing laws or making new ones, and even less with using the nation's strength.

Its subjects are CONTRACTS with foreign nations. These have the force of law, but they get that force from the duty to keep one's word. They are not rules set by a government for its people. They are agreements between one sovereign nation and another.

So the treaty power seems to form a separate department of its own, belonging properly to neither the legislature nor the executive. The qualities I have described elsewhere as essential for managing negotiations with foreign countries point to the executive as the best agent in such matters. But the great importance of the task, and the fact that treaties work as laws, argue strongly for including all or part of the legislature in making them.

:::vocab
Separation of powers: The principle of dividing government into separate legislative, executive, and judicial branches.
Sovereign: Having supreme, independent authority. A sovereign nation answers to no higher government.
Good faith: Honesty and keeping one's promises.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point that treaties are neither purely executive nor purely legislative helps explain the Constitution's design. The president negotiates, because negotiation needs one voice, speed, and sometimes secrecy (Jay made this case in [No. 64](/papers/64/)). The Senate approves, because a treaty becomes part of "the supreme Law of the Land" under Article VI.

Hamilton's answer to the "mixing of powers" complaint relies on Madison's argument in [No. 47](/papers/47/) and the essays after it. There Madison showed that separation of powers doesn't mean the branches can never share any power. It means no branch should hold the *whole* power of another.
:::

## Why not the president alone? {orig=3}

In governments where the executive is a hereditary monarch, it may be proper or safe to give him the entire power of making treaties. But it would be completely unsafe and improper to give that power to an elected leader who serves for four years.

It has been pointed out before, and the point is surely correct, that a hereditary monarch has too much personal stake in his government to be in much danger of being corrupted by foreign powers, even though he often oppresses his people.

But think of a man raised from private life to the position of chief executive. He may have only a modest or small fortune. And he expects that before very long he will probably have to return to the private life he came from. Such a man might sometimes face temptations to sacrifice his duty to his own interest that only extraordinary virtue could resist. A greedy man might be tempted to betray his country's interests to get rich. An ambitious man might make his own rise to power, with the help of a foreign country, the price of betraying the people he serves.

The history of human behavior doesn't support such a high opinion of human virtue that a nation would be wise to place such delicate and important interests as its relations with the rest of the world entirely in the hands of one official, chosen and situated as the President of the United States would be.

:::quote
The history of human conduct does not warrant that exalted opinion of human virtue which would make it wise in a nation to commit interests of so delicate and momentous a kind, as those which concern its intercourse with the rest of the world, to the sole disposal of a magistrate created and circumstanced as would be a President of the United States.

Meaning: People aren't good enough to be trusted with this much power alone. A nation would be foolish to let one elected leader decide its relations with the world without any check.
:::

:::vocab
Hereditary: Passed down from parent to child.
Chief Magistrate: An old term for the president, the highest executive official.
Avaricious: Greedy for money.
Aggrandize: To increase one's own power, wealth, or importance.
:::

:::commentary
This passage shows the Federalists' realistic view of human nature. Hamilton is not saying presidents will be bad people. He is saying the Constitution shouldn't depend on their being good. He made the same point about foreign bribery in [No. 22](/papers/22/), where he argued that a hereditary king has too much to lose to sell out his own country, but an elected leader serving a short term might be tempted.

It may seem surprising that Hamilton, who argued strongly for an energetic president in [No. 70](/papers/70/), insists on limiting the president here. But it fits his overall view: give the executive the power to act, but surround it with checks where the stakes are highest.
:::

## Why not the Senate alone? {orig=4}

Giving the treaty power to the Senate alone would have meant giving up the benefits of having the president, as a constitutional officer, conduct foreign negotiations. It's true that the Senate would then be free to use him for this purpose. But it would also be free not to, and wounded pride or secret scheming might lead it to choose not to.

Besides, a mere servant of the Senate couldn't expect to have the trust and respect of foreign powers to the same degree as the nation's constitutional representative. So he couldn't act with the same weight or effect. For this reason, the Union would lose a great advantage in managing its foreign affairs.

The people would also lose the extra security that comes from the president's cooperation. It would be unwise to trust him alone with so important a power. But there's no doubt that his taking part would greatly add to the safety of society. It must be perfectly clear that the president and Senate holding the power together would give more security than either holding it alone.

And anyone who has carefully considered how a president will be chosen will be satisfied that the office is likely always to be filled by men whose agreement to a treaty will be especially valuable, both for their wisdom and for their honesty.

:::vocab
Pique: Irritation or hurt pride.
Cabal: A small group secretly plotting to gain power.
Ministerial: In 1787, describing an official who simply carries out the instructions of others, without his own authority.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's argument rests on a simple idea: two locks are safer than one. A bad treaty would need the agreement of both the president and two-thirds of the Senate. He also points out that foreign governments would take an elected president more seriously than a mere agent of the Senate.

His confidence that presidents would always be men of wisdom and integrity draws on his defense of the Electoral College in [No. 68](/papers/68/).
:::

## Why the House is left out {orig=5}

The remarks in an earlier paper, which I mentioned above, apply with full force against giving the House of Representatives a share in making treaties.

The House changes often. Counting its future growth, it will also have a very large number of members. Both facts keep us from expecting it to have the qualities needed to do this job well. These are:

- accurate and broad knowledge of foreign affairs;
- a steady and consistent commitment to the same goals;
- a careful and constant concern for the nation's reputation; and
- decision, secrecy, and speed.

These qualities don't fit the nature of a body so changeable and so large.

The very complication of the business, by requiring so many different bodies to agree, would be a solid objection in itself. The House would have to be called together more often. And it would often need to be kept in session for a long time to approve each stage of a treaty. This would cause so much inconvenience and expense that it alone should rule out the idea.

:::vocab
Despatch: Speed in getting something done. Today it is usually spelled "dispatch."
Genius (of a government): The character or spirit of a government or institution.
:::

:::commentary
The House is shut out of approving treaties, but it was never shut out of foreign affairs entirely. Many treaties need money or new laws to carry them out, and those must pass the House. This became clear in 1796, when the House debated whether to fund the Jay Treaty with Britain. The House asked President Washington for his papers on the treaty. He refused, saying the Constitution gave the House no part in making treaties. The House eventually voted the money.

Hamilton's list of qualities a treaty-maker needs, especially "secrecy and despatch," echoes his argument for a single executive in [No. 70](/papers/70/) and Jay's in [No. 64](/papers/64/).
:::

## Two-thirds of those present, not of all senators {orig=6}

The only objection left to examine is the one that would require two-thirds of *all* the members of the Senate, instead of two-thirds of the members present.

Under the second main topic of these papers, I showed that any rule requiring more than a majority of a body to act tends directly to hamper the government's work. It also tends, indirectly, to put the will of the majority under the will of the minority. This alone seems enough to show that the Convention went as far as it could to get the benefit of large numbers in making treaties, while still letting the government act and respecting the will of most of the community.

If two-thirds of the whole Senate had been required, then because some members are always absent, it would in many cases amount in practice to requiring everyone to agree. And the history of every political system that has used this principle is a history of weakness, confusion, and disorder. I could prove this with the examples of the tribunes of ancient Rome, the Polish Diet, and the States-General of the Netherlands, if an example here at home didn't make foreign examples unnecessary.

:::vocab
Supermajority: A required share of votes greater than a simple majority, such as two-thirds.
Tribunes: Officials of the Roman Republic elected to protect the common people. Any one of them could veto the acts of other officials.
Polish Diet: Poland's national legislature. Under a rule called the "liberum veto," a single member could block its decisions.
States-General: The national assembly of the Dutch Republic, where major decisions often required every province to agree.
:::

:::commentary
The "example at home" is the Articles of Confederation. Under the Articles, major decisions needed nine of the thirteen states, and any amendment needed all thirteen. Hamilton had argued in [No. 22](/papers/22/) that these rules let a small minority paralyze the government. Madison made a similar case against supermajorities in [No. 58](/papers/58/).

The Polish Diet was a famous warning. The liberum veto let one member block action, which left Poland weak, and its neighbors divided up its territory in 1772.
:::

## Attendance, and a comparison with the old Congress {orig=7}

Requiring a fixed share of the whole Senate probably wouldn't do more to gain the benefits of large numbers than simply requiring a share of the members who attend.

A fixed requirement means a set number of votes is always needed to pass a measure. That reduces members' reasons to show up on time. But when the Senate's power to act depends on a share that one member's absence or presence can change, the effect is the opposite: members have reason to attend. And because this encourages attendance, it tends to keep the Senate full. So its decisions would probably be made by as many members in this case as in the other, with far fewer delays.

We should not forget how the present Confederation works. Two members may represent a state, and usually do. So the Congress, which now holds all the powers of the Union, rarely has more members than the proposed Senate will have. Also, members of Congress vote by states, and when only one member from a state is present, that state's vote is lost. So we can fairly assume that the number of votes that actually count in the Senate, where members will vote as individuals, will rarely be smaller than in the present Congress.

When we also consider that the president must agree, we can't doubt that the American people will have more protection against misuse of the treaty power under the new Constitution than they have now under the Confederation.

And if we look one step further, to the likely growth of the Senate as new states are created, we will see good reason to be confident that the number of senators trusted with this power will be enough. In fact, we will probably conclude that a body much larger than the Senate is likely to become would be poorly suited to do this job properly.

:::commentary
Hamilton's comparison with the Confederation Congress is clever. Critics complained that as few as two-thirds of a quorum of senators could approve a treaty. Hamilton replies that the old Congress, which made treaties on its own, was often no larger. Under the Articles, each state had to have at least two delegates present to cast its vote.

Hamilton was right that the Senate would grow as new states joined. Today it has 100 members, and the rule is still two-thirds of those present.
:::

:::today
The treaty power works very differently today than Hamilton expected. The Senate has rejected some major treaties, most famously the Treaty of Versailles after World War I, which would have brought the United States into the League of Nations. Because getting two-thirds of the Senate is so hard, presidents now make most international agreements as "executive agreements" that never go to the Senate for a vote. Some are backed by laws passed by both houses of Congress. Others rest on the president's own authority. Supporters say this lets the country act quickly in a complex world. Critics say it sidesteps the check Hamilton defended here, and that agreements made by one president can easily be undone by the next. Major agreements on climate change and Iran's nuclear program, for example, were made without Senate approval, and later presidents withdrew from them.
:::
