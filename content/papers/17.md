:::summary
Some critics feared that a national government able to make laws for individual citizens would grow too powerful and swallow up the states. Hamilton argues the opposite danger is greater. National leaders would have little reason to want the states' everyday local duties, which offer no glory. And the states will always have a stronger hold on people's loyalty. They are closer to home, and they run the courts that protect people's lives and property day to day. History backs him up. In medieval Europe, powerful barons usually beat the kings above them, and in confederacies the members usually overpower the center.
:::

:::keypoints
- Critics worry that acting on individuals would let the national government **absorb the states' powers**.
- But ambitious national leaders would care about **commerce, finance, foreign affairs, and war**, not local matters like farming or private lawsuits.
- Even if they wanted more power, the **people** would stop them. The states can more easily invade national power than the other way around.
- People's loyalty is strongest toward what is **closest**: family, then neighborhood, then state, then nation.
- The states control the **ordinary courts**, the most visible protector of life and property. That gives them great influence over the people.
- The national government's work is more distant, so mostly **educated observers** will notice its benefits.
- In **feudal Europe**, powerful nobles usually defeated their kings. Kings won mainly when the nobles abused the common people.
- **Scotland's** clans were too strong for its kings until the union with England.
:::

:::context Background
Anti-Federalists often warned that a strong national government would "consolidate" the country, meaning it would gradually take over the states until they disappeared. The writer known as "Brutus," for example, argued in New York newspapers that the national government's power over taxes and laws would eventually leave the states with nothing.

In [No. 15](/papers/15/) and [No. 16](/papers/16/), Hamilton argued that the national government must act directly on citizens. Here he answers the fear that this would make it too strong. This essay ends the first group of papers on the Articles' weaknesses and sets up the historical examples in Nos. 18–20.
:::

## Why national leaders won't want local powers {orig=1}

In my last essay I answered one objection. Another, of a different kind, may also be made against making laws for the individual citizens of America. Some may say it would make the Union's government too powerful. They may say it would let the national government absorb the remaining powers that ought to be left with the states for local purposes.

I'll grant the love of power as much room as any reasonable person could ask. Even so, I admit I can't see what would tempt the people running the national government to strip the states of powers like these. Regulating the ordinary local affairs of a state seems to offer very little to attract ambition.

Commerce, finance, foreign negotiation, and war seem to include everything that appeals to ambitious minds. And all the powers needed for those things ought to be placed with the national government in the first place.

Other matters are different. Think of settling private lawsuits between citizens of the same state, overseeing agriculture, and other concerns of that kind. In short, think of all the things that should be handled by local laws. These can never be things a national government would want to take on.

So it's unlikely that national leaders would want to seize those powers. Trying to use them would be as troublesome as it would be pointless. For that reason, having them would add nothing to the dignity, importance, or splendor of the national government.

:::vocab
Residuary authorities: The powers left over for the states after certain powers are given to the national government.
Domestic police: Here, a state's internal affairs and local regulations in general, not just law enforcement.
Commerce: Trade, especially between nations or states.
Usurp: To take power that doesn't rightfully belong to you.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's first argument depends on human nature. Ambitious people want glory, and glory comes from big national matters like war, trade, and diplomacy, not from local road repairs or farm rules. So, he says, national officials won't bother taking over local affairs.

This is one of the predictions in *The Federalist* that critics say turned out badly. Over time, the national government took on many of the very things Hamilton listed as local. The federal Department of Agriculture was created in 1862. Since the 1930s, Washington has become heavily involved in farming, schools, policing, and welfare, often by attaching conditions to federal money given to the states. Supporters say this growth was needed to handle national problems. Critics say it shows Anti-Federalist fears were justified.
:::

## The people would side with the states {orig=2-3}

But let's admit, for the sake of argument, that a reckless desire for domination could be enough to create such a wish. Even then, we can safely say that the people of the states, who elect the national representatives, would stop leaders from feeding such an extreme appetite.

It will always be far easier for the state governments to invade national authority than for the national government to invade state authority. The proof rests on the greater influence that the state governments will usually have over the people, as long as they run their affairs honestly and wisely.

This same fact teaches us something else. There is a built-in weakness in all federal constitutions. So we cannot take too much care, when we design them, to give them all the strength that is consistent with the principles of liberty.

The states' greater influence would come partly from the spread-out structure of the national government. But mainly it would come from the nature of the things that state governments would deal with.

:::vocab
Encroachment: Gradually taking over someone else's power or territory.
Constituent: A voter whom an elected official represents.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is turning the Anti-Federalist argument around. They feared the national government would swallow the states. He says the real risk is the reverse: the states will overpower the national government. That was certainly true under the Articles, where the states ignored Congress whenever they liked.

Madison makes a similar argument at greater length in [No. 45](/papers/45/) and [No. 46](/papers/46/). He predicts that the people's "first and most natural attachment" will be to their state governments.
:::

## Loyalty begins close to home {orig=4-6}

It's a known fact of human nature that our feelings usually grow weaker as their object gets more distant or more spread out. A man is more attached to his family than to his neighborhood, and more to his neighborhood than to the community at large. By the same principle, the people of each state would tend to feel more attached to their local government than to the government of the Union. The only exception would be if the national government ran things much better than the states did.

This strong tendency of the human heart would get powerful help from the kinds of things the states regulate.

The states will oversee a great variety of smaller interests. These will act like many little streams of influence, running through every part of society. I could list them, but the details would be too long and dull to be worth the lesson they might teach.

:::commentary
Hamilton's "circles of loyalty" idea is easy to test in your own life. Most people feel closer to their family than to their town, and closer to their town than to the whole country. In 1787, this was even more true. Many Americans had never traveled outside their state, and some still spoke of their state as their "country."

But notice Hamilton's important exception: "unless the force of that principle should be destroyed by a much better administration of the latter." If the national government runs things better, people's loyalty may shift. Hamilton, who later served as the first Secretary of the Treasury, hoped to win that loyalty through good national government.
:::

## The great advantage: the courts {orig=7-8}

The states have one huge advantage that alone makes the matter clear and settled. I mean the ordinary running of criminal and civil justice.

This, more than anything else, is the most powerful, most widespread, and most attractive source of the people's obedience and attachment. It is the immediate and visible guardian of life and property. Its benefits and its punishments are always active in front of the public. It regulates all the personal interests and familiar concerns that people feel most directly. So it does more than anything else to fill people's minds with affection, respect, and reverence for the government.

This great glue of society will flow almost entirely through the state governments. Apart from every other source of influence, it will give them such strong control over their citizens that they will always fully balance the power of the Union. And quite often they will be dangerous rivals to it.

The national government's work, on the other hand, will happen further from the view of most citizens. Its benefits will mostly be noticed and appreciated by thoughtful, educated people. Because its work relates to broader interests, it will be less likely to touch people's feelings directly. So it will be less likely to inspire a habit of loyalty and an active feeling of attachment.

:::quote
This great cement of society, which will diffuse itself almost wholly through the channels of the particular governments, independent of all other causes of influence, would insure them so decided an empire over their respective citizens as to render them at all times a complete counterpoise, and, not unfrequently, dangerous rivals to the power of the Union.

Meaning: Because the states run the everyday courts that protect people, they will keep the people's loyalty, and will always be strong enough to balance, and sometimes challenge, the national government.
:::

:::vocab
Civil justice: Courts that settle private disputes between people, such as contracts, debts, and property.
Criminal justice: Courts and officials that deal with crimes and punish lawbreakers.
Counterpoise: A force that balances another.
Speculative men: Here, people who think about politics and public affairs, not people who gamble on investments.
:::

:::commentary
This is Hamilton's strongest point, and it still largely holds today. Most crimes are prosecuted under state law in state courts. Most lawsuits over contracts, divorces, wills, car accidents, and property are handled by state courts. When ordinary people deal with "the law," they usually deal with their state and local governments: the local police, the county court, the state judge.

Still, the balance has shifted since 1787. After the Civil War, the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) allowed federal courts to protect people's rights against their own states. In the 1950s and 1960s, federal courts and federal laws played a major role in ending legal segregation in Southern states. Many Americans came to see the national government, not their states, as the protector of their rights.
:::

## Lessons from feudal Europe {orig=9-11}

Our experience with every federal constitution we know of has proved this reasoning many times over. So has our experience with every other system that is even a little like one.

The old feudal systems were not exactly confederacies, but they shared some of the same features. There was a common head, chief, or king, whose authority covered the whole nation. Under him were a number of major vassals, or lords, who were given large shares of land. Each of these lords had many lesser vassals or followers. Those followers lived on and farmed that land, and in return they owed loyalty and obedience to the lord who gave it to them. Each major lord was a kind of king within his own lands.

The results were constant opposition to the king's authority and frequent wars among the great barons themselves. The power of the nation's head was usually too weak either to keep the peace or to protect the people from the oppression of their own lords. Historians call this period of European history the time of feudal anarchy.

When the king happened to be a forceful, warlike man of great ability, he could gain personal weight and influence. For a while, that did the job of a more regular authority. But in general, the power of the barons won out over the power of the king. In many cases, his rule was thrown off completely, and the great lands of the barons became independent principalities or states.

In the cases where the king finally won out over his vassals, his success came mainly from the vassals' tyranny over the people beneath them. The barons, or nobles, were enemies of the king and oppressors of the common people at the same time. Both feared and hated them. In the end, shared danger and shared interest united the king and the people, and that union destroyed the power of the aristocracy.

If the nobles had treated their followers with mercy and justice, and kept their loyalty and devotion, the contests between them and the king would almost always have ended in the nobles' favor. The king's authority would have been reduced or overthrown.

:::vocab
Feudal system: The way society was organized in medieval Europe. Kings granted land to nobles in exchange for loyalty and military service, and peasants worked the land for the nobles.
Vassal: In the feudal system, a person who received land from a lord in exchange for loyalty and service.
Baron: A powerful noble who held land directly from the king.
Fief: Land granted by a lord to a vassal in the feudal system.
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged upper class, often based on birth.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's comparison works like this. The **king** is like the national government. The **barons** are like the states. The **common people** are the citizens. In feudal times, the barons usually beat the king, because their followers were loyal to them. The king only won when the barons treated their people so badly that the people turned to the king for protection.

Applied to America, Hamilton's message is that the states will usually beat the national government, as long as they treat their citizens well. The national government would gain power only if the states abused the people. Some readers see a hint here of what later happened when the national government stepped in to protect citizens whom their states had mistreated.

Hamilton's picture of "feudal anarchy" simplifies a long and varied history. Feudalism looked different in England, France, and the German lands, and it changed over many centuries. But the general pattern he describes, of kings struggling to control powerful nobles, is widely accepted.
:::

## The example of Scotland {orig=12}

This isn't just a guess or a theory. Of the many examples that prove it, Scotland offers a strong one.

Early in its history, the spirit of clanship took hold in that kingdom. It bound the nobles and the people who depended on them by ties as strong as family ties. That made the aristocracy constantly too strong for the power of the king. This lasted until Scotland was joined with England. That union tamed the nobles' fierce and unruly spirit. It brought them under the rules of order that a more sensible and more effective system of government had already established in England.

:::vocab
Clan: In Scotland, a large group of families who shared a name, claimed a common ancestor, and followed the same chief.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton had a personal link to this example. His father, James Hamilton, was a Scot, the son of a landowning family in Ayrshire.

Scotland and England shared the same king starting in 1603. They were formally joined into one kingdom, Great Britain, by the Acts of Union in 1707. Hamilton credits that union with taming the clans. Many historians would add that the clans' power was finally broken after the failed Jacobite rising of 1745–46, when the British government defeated the rebels at the Battle of Culloden and then passed laws to weaken the clan chiefs.
:::

## The states as barons {orig=13-14}

The separate governments in a confederacy can fairly be compared with the feudal baronies. But the states have one advantage over the barons. For the reasons I've explained, the states will usually have the trust and goodwill of the people. With that important support, they will be able to resist any invasion of their powers by the national government. In fact, we'll be lucky if they aren't able to block its lawful and necessary authority too.

The two cases are alike in two ways. Both involve a rivalry for power. And both involve the CONCENTRATION of large shares of the community's strength in particular PLACES. In one case that strength is controlled by individual people, the barons. In the other it is controlled by political bodies, the states.

A short review of what has happened to confederate governments will further explain this important principle. Ignoring it has been the main source of our political mistakes. It has pointed our suspicion in the wrong direction. That review will be the subject of the next few essays.

:::commentary
Hamilton's final line sums up his argument: Americans have been worried about the wrong danger. They feared a strong central government, but history shows that the members of a confederacy usually overpower the center. He calls this "the great source of our political mistakes."

Was he right? Under the Articles, he clearly was. Under the Constitution, the story is more mixed. For much of the 1800s, the states remained very powerful, and Southern states even tried to leave the Union in 1861. In the 1900s, the national government grew far stronger than Hamilton predicted. People still debate whether that growth was necessary or whether it went too far.
:::

:::today
The balance of power between the states and the national government is one of the oldest and biggest debates in American politics. People argue about who should control schools, health care, policing, elections, and the environment. Some say the national government should set the same rules for everyone. Others say states should decide for themselves, so they can fit their own people's needs and try out different ideas. Hamilton's essay reminds us that this debate isn't new, and that even the Constitution's strongest supporters expected the states to remain powerful.
:::
