:::summary
Opponents of the Constitution asked what reason separated states would ever have to fight each other. Hamilton answers with a specific list drawn from America's own recent experience. The states had already quarreled over **western lands**, over **Wyoming Valley** and **Vermont**, over **trade rules** that let one state tax its neighbors, over how to **share the national debt**, and over **laws that cheated creditors** in other states. Under the Articles, the Union held these quarrels in check. Without it, he argues, the disputes would have no judge and might be settled by the sword, while European powers played the states against each other.
:::

:::keypoints
- Separate states would have the same reasons for war as every other nation, plus some **specific American ones**.
- **Western lands** were the biggest danger. States had already argued over who owned them, and breaking up the Union would reopen the fight.
- The **Wyoming Valley** dispute between Connecticut and Pennsylvania, and New York's quarrel over **Vermont**, show how bitter land disputes can become.
- **Trade rules** would cause anger. New York's import taxes, for example, were partly paid by people in **Connecticut and New Jersey**.
- Dividing up the **national debt** would set states against each other, both in deciding the shares and in paying them.
- **Laws that break private contracts**, like Rhode Island's paper-money laws, could lead to a war "not of parchment, but of the sword."
- Disunited states would get tangled in **European alliances and wars**. "Divide and rule" would be the motto of every nation that feared us.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 6](/papers/6/), Hamilton argued in general terms that neighboring nations tend to become enemies. Here he gets specific. Every example in this essay comes from the 1770s and 1780s, and his readers would have known them well.

The most serious issue was the West. Several states, such as Virginia and New York, claimed land stretching far beyond the Appalachian Mountains, based on their old colonial charters. States without such claims, like Maryland and New Jersey, argued that land won by the whole country in the Revolution should belong to the whole country. Maryland refused to approve the Articles of Confederation until the states with claims agreed to give them up, which delayed the Articles until 1781. By 1787, most of the claims had been handed over to Congress, which in July of that year passed the Northwest Ordinance to govern the lands north of the Ohio River.
:::

## What would the states fight about? {orig=1}

People sometimes ask, as if they have won the argument, what reasons the states could possibly have to make war on each other if they were separated. It would be a complete answer to say: exactly the same reasons that have, at different times, drenched every nation in the world in blood.

But unfortunately for us, the question has a more specific answer. There are causes of conflict right in front of us. Even with the limits that a federal constitution places on them, we have seen enough of where they lead. That experience lets us judge what we could expect if those limits were removed.

## The fight over western lands {orig=2}

Disputes over territory have always been one of the richest sources of conflict among nations. Perhaps most of the wars that have ruined the earth began this way. This cause would be fully at work among us.

There is a huge area of unsettled land within the borders of the United States. Several states still have conflicting and unsettled claims to it. And if the Union broke up, the same kind of claims would arise among all of them.

It is well known that the states have already had serious and heated arguments over the lands that had not been granted to anyone at the time of the Revolution, usually called crown lands. The states whose colonial borders included these lands claimed them as their own property. The other states argued that the king's rights to these lands passed to the Union. They made this argument especially about the part of the western territory that had been under the rule of the king of Great Britain, either because he actually held it or because the Indian owners had submitted to him, until he gave it up in the peace treaty. This land, they said, had in any case been gained by the Confederacy through a treaty with a foreign power.

Congress has wisely tried to calm this dispute by persuading the states to cede their claims to the United States for the benefit of all. This has gone far enough that, if the Union continues, there is a strong hope that the dispute will end peacefully.

But breaking up the Confederacy would bring the dispute back to life and create new ones on the same subject. Right now, a large part of the empty western territory belongs to the whole Union, at least because the states ceded it, even if not by any earlier right. If the Union ended, the states that ceded land as a federal compromise would probably want it back, since the reason for the gift would be gone. The other states would surely demand a share, based on their right to be represented in the decision. They would argue that a gift, once made, can't be taken back. And they would argue that it is still fair for them to share in land that was won or secured by the joint efforts of the Confederacy.

Suppose that all the states agreed, against all odds, that each had a right to a share of this common property. There would still be a hard problem: what rule should be used to divide it? Different states would propose different rules. Since each rule would favor some states over others, the dispute might not be easy to settle peacefully.

:::vocab
Crown lands: Land that belonged to the British king and had not been granted to any colony or person before the Revolution.
Cede: To formally give up land or rights to someone else.
Cession: The act of giving up land or rights. The states' "cessions" handed their western land claims to Congress.
Jurisdiction: The authority to govern and make laws in a certain area.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton refers to the "Indian proprietors," meaning owners, of the western lands. That phrase admits something important: these lands were the homes of Native nations, such as the Shawnee, Miami, Delaware, and others. But in the 1783 peace treaty, Britain handed its claims to the United States without consulting the Native nations who lived there. For the states and Congress, the question was only which *Americans* owned the land. The Native nations' own claims were mostly treated as an obstacle to be removed by treaty or by force, which led to decades of war in the Ohio country.
:::

## Lessons from Wyoming and Vermont {orig=3-4}

So in the vast western territory, we can see plenty of room for hostile claims, with no referee or common judge to step in between the two sides. If we reason from the past to the future, we have good reason to fear that the sword would sometimes be called on to decide their disputes.

The dispute between Connecticut and Pennsylvania over the land at Wyoming warns us not to be too hopeful that such conflicts can be settled easily. The Articles of Confederation required the two states to submit the matter to a federal court. They did, and the court ruled for Pennsylvania. But Connecticut showed strong signs of being unhappy with the decision. It did not seem fully to accept it until, through negotiation and careful handling, it was given something like an equal trade for what it believed it had lost.

I don't mean to criticize Connecticut's conduct in the slightest. It surely believed sincerely that the decision had wronged it. And states, like individuals, accept decisions that go against them only with great reluctance.

Those who saw the inside story of this state's dispute with the district of Vermont can tell you how much opposition we faced. It came both from states that had no claim of their own and from states that did. They can also tell you how much danger the peace of the Confederacy might have faced if New York had tried to enforce its rights by force.

Two motives drove most of that opposition. One was jealousy of our future power. The other was the interest of certain influential men in neighboring states who had received land grants from the government that actually controlled Vermont. Even the states that made claims competing with ours seemed more eager to break up New York than to win their own claims. These were New Hampshire, Massachusetts, and Connecticut. New Jersey and Rhode Island always showed a warm enthusiasm for Vermont's independence. So did Maryland, until it was alarmed by signs of a connection between Canada and Vermont. These were small states, and they looked unkindly on the prospect of our growing greatness.

By reviewing these events, we can see some of the causes that would probably drag the states into conflict with each other, if they had the bad fortune to become disunited.

:::vocab
Wyoming Valley: An area along the Susquehanna River in northeastern Pennsylvania, claimed by both Connecticut and Pennsylvania. It is not related to the later state of Wyoming.
Vermont: A territory claimed by New York and New Hampshire. Its settlers declared it independent in 1777, and it joined the Union as the fourteenth state in 1791.
Censure: Strong criticism or blame.
:::

:::commentary
These were not just legal arguments. In the Wyoming Valley, settlers from Connecticut and Pennsylvania fought a series of armed clashes, sometimes called the Pennamite Wars, with burned homes and deaths on both sides. In 1782, a special court set up under the Articles ruled for Pennsylvania. Historians usually take Hamilton's "something like an equivalent" to mean the **Western Reserve**, a strip of land in what is now northeastern Ohio. Connecticut kept it when it gave up its other western claims in 1786.

Vermont shows the danger Hamilton fears even more clearly. Vermont's settlers had land titles from New Hampshire and refused to accept New York's rule. During the Revolution, Vermont's leaders even held secret talks with British officials in Canada, which explains Maryland's alarm. Notice that Hamilton, a New Yorker writing for New Yorkers, presents New York as the injured party here. People in Vermont and the neighboring states saw it differently.
:::

## Trade rivalries {orig=5-6}

Competition over trade would be another rich source of conflict. States in less favorable locations would want to escape their disadvantages and share in the advantages of their luckier neighbors.

Each state, or separate confederacy, would follow its own trade policy. This would create special rules, favors, and exclusions, which would cause resentment. Since the first settlement of the country, we have been used to trading with each other on equal terms. That habit would make these causes of resentment sting even more than they would otherwise. WE WOULD BE QUICK TO CALL THINGS INJURIES THAT WERE REALLY THE FAIR ACTIONS OF INDEPENDENT NATIONS LOOKING OUT FOR THEIR OWN INTERESTS.

The spirit of enterprise that marks the trading part of America has never missed a chance to show itself. It is very unlikely that this unrestrained spirit would pay much respect to trade rules that particular states might pass to secure special benefits for their own citizens. Some people would break the rules. Others would try to stop and punish them. This would naturally lead to violent incidents, those incidents to revenge, and revenge to war.

Some states would be able to use trade rules to make others pay tribute to them, and the paying states would not put up with it for long. The situation of New York, Connecticut, and New Jersey is an example.

New York needs revenue, so it must charge duties on its imports. A large part of these duties must be paid by the people of the other two states, since they buy what we import. New York would be neither willing nor able to give up this advantage. Our citizens would not agree to let people from neighboring states off the hook for a tax that they themselves pay. And even if that were not a problem, it would be impossible to tell which customers in our markets came from where.

Would Connecticut and New Jersey put up for long with being taxed by New York for New York's sole benefit? Would we be allowed to enjoy our great port city in peace for long, when it gives us an advantage that our neighbors find so hateful and, in their view, so oppressive? Could we hold on to it against the weight of Connecticut on one side and the added pressure of New Jersey on the other? Only reckless people would answer yes to these questions.

:::quote
WE SHOULD BE READY TO DENOMINATE INJURIES THOSE THINGS WHICH WERE IN REALITY THE JUSTIFIABLE ACTS OF INDEPENDENT SOVEREIGNTIES CONSULTING A DISTINCT INTEREST.

Meaning: Because Americans were used to trading freely with each other, they would treat each separate state's normal trade rules as insults and attacks.
:::

:::vocab
Duties: Taxes on imported goods.
Tributary: Forced to pay tribute, a regular payment from a weaker state to a stronger one.
Metropolis: A large and important city. Hamilton means New York City and its harbor.
Temerity: Reckless boldness.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's example was very real. Under the Articles, each state set its own import taxes. Goods for Connecticut and New Jersey often arrived through the port of New York, which taxed them, so New York's treasury grew at its neighbors' expense. Madison later described New Jersey, caught between the ports of New York and Philadelphia, as being like a barrel tapped at both ends.

The Constitution solved this problem directly. It gave Congress the power to regulate trade among the states, and it barred states from taxing imports without Congress's consent.
:::

## Fighting over the national debt {orig=7-8}

The Union's public debt would be another cause of conflict among separate states or confederacies. Both dividing it up at the start and paying it off over time would create bad feelings and hostility.

How could they agree on a rule for dividing the debt that would satisfy everyone? Almost any rule that could be proposed has real flaws. And as usual, each side would exaggerate those flaws to suit its own interest.

The states even disagree about the basic principle of paying the public debt. Some care less about the nation's credit, or their citizens have little or no direct stake in the question. These states feel indifferent, or even opposed, to paying the debt owed to Americans at all. They would tend to exaggerate the difficulties of dividing it up. In other states, a large number of citizens are creditors of the public, holding more than their state's fair share of the national debt. These states would push hard for a fair and effective plan.

The delays of the first group would anger the second. Meanwhile, real differences of opinion and pretended delays would put off any agreement on a rule. Citizens of the states with a stake in the debt would protest loudly. Foreign nations would press for payment of what they are rightly owed. So the peace of the states would face two dangers at once: invasion from outside and conflict from within.

Suppose they got past the difficulty of agreeing on a rule, and the debt was divided. There is still good reason to think that, in practice, the rule would turn out to fall harder on some states than on others. The states hurt by it would naturally try to lighten their burden. The other states would just as naturally refuse to change it, since a change would probably increase their own share. The complaining states would eagerly use that refusal as an excuse to stop paying. And their failure to keep their promises would lead to bitter arguments and quarrels.

Even if the rule turned out to be fair in practice, some states would still fall behind in their payments for many other reasons:

- a real lack of resources;
- poor management of their finances;
- occasional disorder in running their governments;
- and on top of everything else, people's usual unwillingness to give up money for purposes that have outlived the emergency that created them, especially when it takes money away from present needs.

Missed payments, whatever the cause, would lead to complaints, blame, and quarrels. Perhaps nothing is more likely to disturb the peace among nations than being bound to contribute together to some common goal that doesn't give them equal benefits at the same time. For it is as true as it is familiar that there is nothing people disagree about so readily as paying money.

:::quote
For it is an observation, as true as it is trite, that there is nothing men differ so readily about as the payment of money.

Meaning: Nothing starts arguments faster than deciding who has to pay.
:::

:::vocab
Public debt: Money a government has borrowed and must repay. The United States owed money both to foreign lenders, like France and Dutch bankers, and to its own citizens.
Domestic debt: The part of the public debt owed to a nation's own citizens.
Creditor: A person or business that has lent money and is owed repayment.
Delinquency: Failure to pay what one owes on time.
Trite: So often repeated that it has become dull.
:::

:::commentary
Much of the national debt was held as certificates that the government had given to soldiers, farmers, and merchants during the war. Many of these certificates ended up in the hands of investors in Northern states. That is why Hamilton says some states had citizens holding far more than their share of the debt, while others cared little about paying it.

Hamilton would deal with this problem himself as the first Secretary of the Treasury. In 1790, he proposed that the national government take over, or "assume," the states' war debts too. States that had already paid off much of their debt, like Virginia, objected. The plan passed only after a famous compromise in which supporters agreed to place the nation's permanent capital on the Potomac River, in the South.
:::

## Laws that break contracts {orig=9}

Laws that violate private contracts are another likely source of hostility. They amount to attacks on the rights of the other states whose citizens are harmed by them.

If there are no new checks on them, we have no reason to expect that the states will pass more generous or fair laws in the future than we have seen in too many cases disgracing their law books. We have seen the desire for revenge stirred up in Connecticut by the outrageous acts of the legislature of Rhode Island. We can reasonably conclude that in similar cases, under different circumstances, a war, not of PARCHMENT but of the sword, would punish such shocking violations of moral duty and social justice.

:::quote
...a war, not of PARCHMENT, but of the sword, would chastise such atrocious breaches of moral obligation and social justice.

Meaning: Today, states fight such wrongs with angry laws and paperwork. Without the Union, they might fight with real weapons.
:::

:::vocab
Parchment: Writing material made from animal skin, used for laws and official documents. A war "of parchment" means a war of words and laws.
:::

:::commentary
In 1786, Rhode Island printed large amounts of paper money and passed laws forcing creditors to accept it at full value, even as it quickly lost its worth. People who had lent money, including merchants in other states, were effectively forced to accept far less than they were owed. To debtors, many of them struggling farmers, the laws were relief. To creditors, they were legal theft.

The Constitution responded directly. Article I, Section 10 says no state may "coin Money," "emit Bills of Credit" (print paper money), or pass any "Law impairing the Obligation of Contracts."
:::

## Divide and rule {orig=10}

Earlier papers have explained in enough detail the likelihood that different states or confederacies would form alliances with different foreign nations, alliances that would clash with each other, and the effect this would have on the peace of the whole.

From what they have shown, we must draw this conclusion. If America is not connected at all, or is tied only by the weak bond of a simple alliance for attack and defense, these clashing alliances would slowly entangle it in all the harmful mazes of European politics and wars. And because of the destructive quarrels among its separate parts, America would probably fall prey to the tricks and schemes of powers that are equally the enemies of all of them. *Divide et impera*, "divide and command," must be the motto of every nation that either hates or fears us.

:::quote
Divide et impera must be the motto of every nation that either hates or fears us.

Meaning: Any foreign power that wants to weaken America will try to split the states apart and turn them against each other.
:::

:::note
Hamilton's second footnote announces a change in the publishing schedule. To get the whole series before the public as soon as possible, the papers would now appear four times a week, in several New York newspapers. Speed mattered because New York would soon have to decide whether to ratify the Constitution.
:::

:::vocab
Divide et impera: Latin for "divide and rule" or "divide and command." The strategy of keeping enemies weak by splitting them up.
League: An alliance between independent states or nations.
:::

:::commentary
Jay made the argument about foreign alliances in Nos. [4](/papers/4/) and [5](/papers/5/). The fear was not far-fetched. In the 1780s, Britain held forts on American soil, and Spain controlled the Mississippi. Some western settlers talked openly about breaking away and making deals with Spain. A divided America could easily have become a set of rival client states, each tied to a different European power.

George Washington made a similar warning in his Farewell Address of 1796. He urged Americans to avoid permanent alliances with foreign nations and warned that regional divisions could be used to break the Union apart.
:::

This essay is the middle of a three-part argument. [No. 6](/papers/6/) explains why separate states would fight, and [No. 8](/papers/8/) explains what constant war would do to liberty. The western lands appear again in Madison's [No. 14](/papers/14/), and the debt problem returns in Hamilton's essays on taxation, such as [No. 30](/papers/30/).
:::

:::today
Many of the disputes Hamilton describes still arise among the states, but now they go to court or to Congress instead of to war. States still argue over boundaries, water rights, and taxes. The Supreme Court hears cases between states, such as fights over rivers shared by several states. States still compete for business with tax breaks, and they argue over who gets to tax online sales or out-of-state workers. Hamilton's point holds: these disputes are settled peacefully because the states share a common government with a "common judge," which is exactly what he said would disappear without the Union.
:::
