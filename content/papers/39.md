:::summary
Madison asks two questions about the new Constitution. First, is it truly **republican**? He defines a republic as a government that gets all its power, directly or indirectly, from the great body of the people, and whose officials serve for limited terms or during good behavior. The Constitution clearly passes this test. Second, critics say the Convention created a **national** government that swallows the states, instead of a **federal** one that respects them. Madison answers that the Constitution is actually a **mix of both**. He walks through five features of the new government and shows that some are federal, some are national, and some are a blend.
:::

:::keypoints
- No form of government except a republic would fit the American people or the principles of the Revolution.
- Many countries called "republics" (Holland, Venice, Poland, England) really weren't, so the word needs a clear definition.
- A **republic** gets all its powers from **the great body of the people**, not a small favored class, and is run by officials with **limited terms** or **good-behavior** tenure.
- The Constitution meets this test. It also bans titles of nobility and guarantees every state a republican government.
- **Ratification** is federal: each state decides for itself.
- **Where power comes from** is mixed: the House represents people (national), the Senate represents states (federal), and the president is chosen by a blend.
- **How power operates** is national: laws act directly on individual citizens.
- **How much power** is federal: the national government has only listed powers, and the states keep the rest.
- **Amendments** are mixed: they need more than a simple majority and are counted by states, but don't require every state to agree.
:::

:::context Background
This essay begins the main part of the series, where Publius examines the Constitution piece by piece. Opponents had made two powerful charges. First, that the plan created an aristocracy or monarchy in disguise, not a true republic. Second, that it created a "consolidated" national government that would eventually wipe out the states. Under the Articles of Confederation, the states were sovereign, and the central Congress could act only through them. To many Americans, that was what "federal" meant. Madison has to show that the new plan is still republican and still respects the states, even though it is far stronger than the Articles.
:::

## Is the plan republican? {orig=1-2}

The last essay finished the introduction to a fair examination of the Convention's plan. Now we begin that examination.

The first question is whether the general form and character of the government are truly republican. Clearly, no other form would fit the character of the American people, the basic principles of the Revolution, or the honorable decision of every lover of freedom to base all our political experiments on humanity's ability to govern itself. So if the Convention's plan turns out not to be republican, its supporters must give it up as impossible to defend.

:::commentary
Madison sets a high bar for himself. He doesn't say "mostly republican" will do. He says that if the plan isn't truly republican, its supporters should abandon it. This shows how central the idea of a republic was to Americans in 1787. They had just rejected a king, and they believed the whole world was watching to see if self-government could work.
:::

## What makes a government a republic? {orig=3-4}

What, then, are the defining features of a republic? If we tried to answer this by looking at how political writers have used the word, instead of looking at principles, we'd never find a good answer.

Holland, where none of the supreme authority comes from the people, has almost always been called a republic. So has Venice, where a small group of hereditary nobles holds absolute power over the great body of the people. Poland, which mixes the worst kinds of aristocracy and monarchy, has been honored with the same name. England, which has only one republican branch combined with a hereditary aristocracy and monarchy, has also often been wrongly called a republic. These examples are almost as different from each other as they are from a real republic. They show how carelessly the term has been used.

If instead we look at the principles on which different governments are founded, we can define a republic this way. A republic is a government that gets all its powers, directly or indirectly, from the great body of the people, and is run by people who hold their offices for as long as the people choose, for a limited period, or during good behavior.

It is **essential** that such a government come from the great body of society, not from a small portion of it or a favored class. Otherwise, a handful of tyrannical nobles who handed out power among themselves could claim to be republicans and call their government by the honorable name of republic.

It is **sufficient** that the people running the government be appointed by the people, directly or indirectly, and hold office in one of the ways just listed. If we demanded more, every government in the United States, and every other well-organized popular government that has ever existed or could exist, would fail the test.

Under every state constitution, some government officials are chosen only indirectly by the people. In most states, even the governor is chosen this way. In one state, one house of the legislature is chosen this way. Under all the state constitutions, the highest offices are held for a set period, often a period of years, in both the legislative and executive branches. And under most state constitutions, as well as in the opinion of the most respected thinkers, judges keep their offices during good behavior.

:::vocab
Republic: As Madison defines it, a government that gets all its powers, directly or indirectly, from the great body of the people, and whose officials serve for limited terms or during good behavior.
Hereditary: Passed down within a family. Hereditary nobles inherit their power instead of being chosen.
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged class, usually nobles.
Indirect election: An election where the people choose someone who then chooses the official. For example, voters choose electors, who choose the president.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's definition has two parts. The **essential** part: power must come from *the great body of the people*, not a privileged few. The **sufficient** part: officials can be chosen indirectly and can serve long terms, and the government is still a republic.

The second part matters because critics attacked the Senate (originally chosen by state legislatures), the president (chosen by electors), and judges (appointed for life) as undemocratic. Madison points out that the state governments already did all of these things.

But notice a gap between Madison's ideal and reality. In 1787, "the great body of the people" who could vote was mostly white men who owned property. Women, enslaved people, and many poor men couldn't vote at all. Over the next two centuries, amendments like the 15th, 19th, and 26th expanded the vote, bringing the country closer to Madison's own definition.
:::

## How the Constitution measures up {orig=5-6}

When we compare the Constitution with this standard, we can see at once that it meets it in the strictest sense.

The House of Representatives, like at least one house of every state legislature, is elected directly by the great body of the people. The Senate, like the current Congress and the Maryland Senate, is chosen indirectly by the people. The president is chosen indirectly by the people, as governors are in most states. Even judges and all other national officials will be chosen by the people, though at a distance, just as in the states.

The length of terms also fits the republican standard and the example of the state constitutions. Members of the House are elected regularly, as in all the states, for two years, as in South Carolina. Senators are elected for six years. That's only one year more than in Maryland's Senate and two more than in the Senates of New York and Virginia. The president serves four years. In New York and Delaware, governors are elected for three years, and in South Carolina for two. In the other states, they are elected every year.

Several states, however, don't allow their governor to be impeached at all, and in Delaware and Virginia he can't be impeached until he leaves office. The president of the United States can be impeached at any time while in office. Judges will serve during good behavior, as they certainly should. The terms of other executive officials will be set by law, as reason and the state constitutions suggest.

If anyone needed more proof that this system is republican, the strongest proof is that it completely bans titles of nobility, in both the national and state governments. It also explicitly guarantees every state a republican form of government.

:::vocab
Impeachment: The process of formally charging a government official with serious misconduct. If convicted, the official is removed from office.
Titles of nobility: Inherited ranks like duke, earl, or lord. The Constitution forbids the United States and the states from granting them.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's strategy here is to compare every feature of the new government to the state constitutions, which Americans already trusted. If six-year Senate terms are dangerous, why aren't Maryland's five-year terms? This is a persuasive move, though critics could reply that power at the national level is far greater than in any one state, so longer terms there are riskier.

The ban on titles of nobility appears in Article I, Sections 9 and 10. The guarantee of a republican government to every state is in Article IV, Section 4.
:::

## The objection: national, not federal {orig=7-8}

"But it wasn't enough," say the Constitution's opponents, "for the Convention to keep the republican form. They should have been just as careful to keep the FEDERAL form, which treats the Union as a CONFEDERACY of sovereign states. Instead, they have created a NATIONAL government, which treats the Union as a CONSOLIDATION of the states." And they ask: by what authority was this bold and radical change made? Because so much has been made of this objection, we need to examine it carefully.

Without questioning whether the distinction behind the objection is accurate, we need to look at three things to judge how strong it is. First, what is the real character of the proposed government? Second, how far was the Convention authorized to propose such a government? And third, how far could the Convention's duty to the country make up for any lack of formal authority?

:::vocab
Federal (in 1787): Describing a union of sovereign states, where the central government deals with the states rather than directly with citizens. This is close to what we would now call a "confederation."
National (in 1787): Describing a single, unified government that acts directly on individual citizens and has supreme authority over everything.
Consolidation: Merging the states into one single government, erasing them as independent political units.
Sovereign: Having supreme, independent authority; not subject to any higher power.
:::

:::commentary
The meaning of "federal" has changed since 1787, which makes this essay confusing at first. Today, "federal government" just means the national government in Washington. But in 1787, "federal" described a league of independent states, like the government under the Articles of Confederation. Critics were saying the new Constitution was not federal enough.

Ironically, the Constitution's supporters took the name "Federalists," even though they wanted a stronger central government. Their opponents, who wanted to protect state power, got stuck with the name "Anti-Federalists." Some historians think the Federalists chose their name partly to make their plan sound less radical.

Madison answers the first question in this essay. He covers the second and third questions in [No. 40](/papers/40/).
:::

## Five ways to test the government {orig=9}

First, to find out the real character of the government, we can look at five things: the foundation on which it will be established; the sources of its everyday powers; how those powers operate; how far those powers extend; and the authority by which future changes can be made.

## Test 1: How the Constitution is adopted (federal) {orig=10-11}

Looking at the first, it appears that the Constitution will be founded on the consent and ratification of the people of America, given through delegates elected for this special purpose. But this consent will be given by the people, not as individuals making up one single nation, but as members of the separate and independent states they belong to. It will be the consent and ratification of the states, coming from the supreme authority in each state, which is the authority of the people themselves. So the act that establishes the Constitution will be a FEDERAL act, not a NATIONAL one.

One fact makes it obvious that this will be a federal and not a national act, in the sense the critics mean. It will not be decided by a MAJORITY of the people of the Union, or by a MAJORITY of the states. It must come from the UNANIMOUS consent of the states that agree to it. The only difference from the states' usual consent is that it will be expressed by the people themselves, not by the state legislatures.

If the people were treated as one nation in this process, the will of the majority of all the people in the United States would bind the minority, just as the majority in each state binds the minority there. That majority would be measured either by counting individual votes or by treating a majority of the states as evidence of a majority of the people. Neither rule has been adopted. Each state, in ratifying the Constitution, is treated as a sovereign body, independent of all the others, and bound only by its own free choice. In this respect, then, the new Constitution would be FEDERAL, not NATIONAL.

:::commentary
Madison is pointing to how ratification works. The Constitution would take effect once nine states ratified it, but it would only bind the states that chose to ratify. A state that said no (as Rhode Island and North Carolina initially did) would simply not be part of the new government. No national majority could force a state in. That's why Madison calls ratification "federal."

This passage later became important in a very different way. Before the Civil War, defenders of states' rights and secession, like John C. Calhoun, argued that because each state joined the Union by its own sovereign choice, each state could also leave. Supporters of the Union, like Daniel Webster and Abraham Lincoln, replied that the Constitution created a permanent nation of the people. Madison himself, in old age, rejected the idea that states had a right to secede.
:::

## Test 2: Where the government's power comes from (mixed) {orig=12}

The second test looks at the sources of the government's everyday powers.

The House of Representatives will get its power from the people of America. The people will be represented in the same proportion and on the same principle as in a state legislature. So far, the government is NATIONAL, not FEDERAL.

The Senate, on the other hand, will get its power from the states, as equal political communities. They will be represented equally in the Senate, as they are now in Congress. So far, the government is FEDERAL, not NATIONAL.

The executive's power will come from a very mixed source. The president will be directly elected by the states acting as political units. The votes each state gets are calculated in a mixed way: partly treating the states as equal, separate communities, and partly treating them as unequal parts of the same society. If no one wins, the final choice will be made by the House, the branch made up of the nation's representatives. But for this particular vote, the House will vote by state delegations, as if it were made up of separate, equal political communities.

So from this angle, the government appears mixed. It has at least as many FEDERAL features as NATIONAL ones.

:::commentary
This is the Great Compromise of the Convention at work. Large states wanted representation based on population, and small states wanted each state to have an equal vote. The result: the House is based on population (national), and the Senate gives every state two votes (federal).

The Electoral College is a blend too. Each state's number of electors equals its number of representatives plus its two senators. The House part reflects population, and the Senate part treats every state equally. And if no candidate gets a majority of electoral votes, the House picks the president, but each state delegation gets just one vote. That happened in 1800 and 1824.
:::

## Test 3: How the government's power works (national) {orig=13}

The difference between a federal and a national government, when it comes to how the government operates, is thought to be this. In a federal government, its powers act on the states that make up the confederacy, as political units. In a national government, its powers act on the individual citizens who make up the nation.

By this test, the Constitution is NATIONAL, not FEDERAL, though maybe not as completely as people think. In several cases, especially lawsuits involving states, the states must be dealt with as political units. So the national character of the government is slightly marked here by a few federal features. But that small flaw is probably unavoidable in any plan. In its ordinary and most important actions, the government will act directly on the people as individuals. So overall, in this respect, it can be called a NATIONAL government.

:::commentary
This was the biggest change from the Articles of Confederation. Under the Articles, Congress could only *ask* the states for money and soldiers, and the states often ignored it. Hamilton called this the core defect of the old system in [No. 15](/papers/15/). Under the Constitution, the national government can tax individuals, pass laws that apply directly to them, and enforce those laws through its own courts. It doesn't have to go through the states.
:::

## Test 4: How far the government's power reaches (federal) {orig=14}

But while the government is national in how its powers *operate*, it looks different again when we consider how *far* its powers extend.

The idea of a national government includes not only authority over individual citizens, but unlimited supremacy over all people and things, as far as they can lawfully be governed. Among people combined into one nation, this supremacy belongs entirely to the national legislature. Among communities united for particular purposes, it belongs partly to the general government and partly to the local governments.

In the first case, all local authorities are below the supreme government, which can control, direct, or abolish them whenever it wants. In the second, local authorities hold separate and independent shares of supreme power. Within their own areas, they are no more subject to the general government than the general government is subject to them within its area.

In this respect, the proposed government can't be called NATIONAL. Its authority extends only to certain listed subjects. It leaves the states a remaining, untouchable sovereignty over everything else.

It's true that when disputes arise over the boundary between the two governments' authority, the court that makes the final decision will be part of the national government. But that doesn't change the principle. The decision must be made fairly, according to the rules of the Constitution, and all the usual and most effective precautions have been taken to make sure it is fair. Some such court is clearly necessary to prevent the states from settling disputes by war and breaking up the Union. And few will argue that it should be part of the state governments instead of the national government, or that it could safely be placed anywhere but in the national government.

:::vocab
Enumerated powers: Powers specifically listed in the Constitution. Most of Congress's powers are listed in Article I, Section 8.
Residuary sovereignty: The authority left over for the states after the national government's listed powers are taken out. The 10th Amendment later put this idea into words.
Jurisdiction: The area of authority of a government or court.
Tribunal: A court.
:::

:::commentary
Here Madison describes what we now call **federalism**: two levels of government, each supreme in its own area. The national government handles listed subjects like war, foreign affairs, trade between states, and money. The states keep authority over everything else, like most criminal law, schools, marriage, and property. The 10th Amendment, ratified in 1791, says this directly: powers not given to the national government "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."

Madison is very candid about one weak point. When the two levels clash, the referee, the Supreme Court, is part of the national government. Anti-Federalists warned that the national referee would side with the national government. Madison says the Court will judge fairly by the rules, and that the alternative is "an appeal to the sword," meaning war between the states. Seventy-three years later, the Civil War proved how real that danger was.
:::

## Test 5: How the Constitution is amended (mixed) {orig=15}

If we test the Constitution by its last feature, the authority to make amendments, we find it is neither entirely NATIONAL nor entirely FEDERAL.

If it were entirely national, the supreme and final authority would belong to a MAJORITY of the people of the Union. That majority, like the majority in any nation, could change or abolish the government at any time. If it were entirely federal, every single state would have to agree to any change that would bind them all.

The Convention's plan isn't based on either principle. By requiring more than a majority, and especially by counting by STATES rather than by CITIZENS, it moves away from the NATIONAL character toward the FEDERAL. But by making the agreement of fewer than all the states enough, it moves away from the FEDERAL character toward the NATIONAL.

:::commentary
Article V requires amendments to be approved by three-fourths of the states. That's more than a simple majority, and it counts states, not people, which is federal. But it doesn't require *every* state to agree, which is national. Under the Articles of Confederation, every single state had to agree to any change. Rhode Island alone had blocked important reforms, which is one reason the Convention gave up on fixing the Articles.

Because of the three-fourths rule, the Constitution has been amended only 27 times, and the first ten came all at once as the Bill of Rights.
:::

## Conclusion: a mix of both {orig=16}

So the proposed Constitution, strictly speaking, is neither a national nor a federal Constitution, but a combination of both. In its foundation, it is federal, not national. In the sources of the government's everyday powers, it is partly federal and partly national. In how those powers operate, it is national, not federal. In how far those powers extend, it is federal, not national. And finally, in the way amendments are made, it is neither entirely federal nor entirely national.

:::keypoints The five tests at a glance
- **Foundation** (how it's ratified): Federal
- **Source of power** (who chooses officials): Mixed. House is national, Senate is federal, president is a blend.
- **Operation** (whom laws act on): National
- **Extent** (how much power): Federal
- **Amendments** (how it changes): Mixed
:::

:::today
Americans still argue about the balance Madison describes. Should education, health care, marijuana laws, or election rules be decided nationally or by each state? Is the country a single nation or a union of states? These questions come up in Supreme Court cases about the limits of Congress's power and in political fights over "states' rights." Madison's answer was that the Constitution is deliberately both. That's why there is no simple answer, and why the debate never ends.
:::
