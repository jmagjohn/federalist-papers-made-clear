:::summary
After three essays on the history of other confederacies, Hamilton returns to the Articles of Confederation and names three more serious flaws. First, Congress has **no way to enforce its laws**. The Articles don't even give it the right to use force, and they say the states keep every power not *expressly* given away. Second, there is **no guarantee of the state governments**. If a rebel or tyrant seized power in a state, as nearly happened in **Shays' Rebellion** in Massachusetts, the Union could do nothing. Third, the system of **quotas** for state payments is unfair, because no simple measure like land value or population can show how wealthy a state really is. The cure is to let the national government raise its own money, mainly through taxes on goods, which limit themselves.
:::

:::keypoints
- Congress has **no sanction**: no fines, no penalties, and no clear right to use force against states that disobey.
- The Articles say each state keeps every power not **"expressly delegated"** to Congress. So Congress can't even claim an unstated power to enforce its laws.
- There is **no guarantee** that the Union will protect a state government from violent overthrow.
- **Shays' Rebellion** in Massachusetts shows the danger is real. What if the rebels had been led by a **Caesar** or a **Cromwell**?
- A guarantee wouldn't stop the people from changing their state constitutions **peacefully**. It would only work against **violence**.
- **Quotas** based on land or population are unfair, because wealth depends on countless things that can't be measured.
- Taxes on **goods people buy** are fairer and limit themselves: if they get too high, people buy less and the government collects less.
- For **direct taxes** on land and buildings, population is the simplest fair rule.
:::

:::context Background
Nos. 18–20 reviewed the histories of other confederacies: the ancient Greek leagues, the Holy Roman Empire, Switzerland, and the Netherlands. In No. 21, Hamilton returns to America.

The essay's most vivid example is **Shays' Rebellion**. In 1786 and early 1787, thousands of farmers in western Massachusetts, many of them Revolutionary War veterans, rose up against heavy state taxes and the seizure of their farms for unpaid debts. Led in part by a former Continental Army captain, Daniel Shays, they shut down courts to stop foreclosures. In January 1787 they marched on the federal arsenal at Springfield. A state militia, paid for partly by wealthy Boston merchants, broke up the rebellion. Congress was unable to help. For many leaders, the rebellion was a frightening sign that the country was falling apart, and it helped build support for the Constitutional Convention.
:::

## Knowing the disease {orig=1}

In the last three essays, I gave a brief review of the main facts and events that show the character and fate of other confederate governments. Now I will go on listing the most important defects of our own system, which have so far disappointed our hopes. To judge the right remedy safely and wisely, we absolutely must understand how widespread and how dangerous the disease is.

## No way to enforce the laws {orig=2}

The next most obvious defect of the current Confederation is that its laws have no SANCTION at all. As now organized, the United States has no power to demand obedience or to punish disobedience to its decisions. It can't impose fines. It can't suspend or take away privileges. It has no other constitutional method either.

The Articles don't clearly give Congress the authority to use force against states that fail in their duties. Someone might argue that the national government has this right anyway, as part of the basic nature of the agreement among the states. But that would have to be inferred and read into the Articles. And it would go against part of the second article, which declares "that each State shall retain every power, jurisdiction, and right, not EXPRESSLY delegated to the United States in Congress assembled."

It's clearly absurd to suppose that a right of this kind doesn't exist. But we are stuck with a choice. We can accept that absurd idea. Or we can violate or explain away a provision that the opponents of the new Constitution have repeatedly praised. In fact, they have criticized the new plan, at length and with arguments that sound convincing, because it lacks such a provision.

If we don't want to weaken this much-praised provision, we must conclude something remarkable. The United States is a government without even a shadow of constitutional power to enforce its own laws. From the examples I have cited, it is clear that the American Confederacy is different in this respect from every other system of its kind. It is something new and unheard of in the political world.

:::vocab
Sanction: A penalty for breaking a law.
Pecuniary: Having to do with money.
Delinquency: Failure to pay what one owes on time.
Construction: Here, interpreting or explaining the meaning of a law or document. To "construe" is to interpret.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is making a sharp debating point. Anti-Federalists praised the Articles' rule that the states keep every power not **"expressly"** delegated, and they complained that the new Constitution had no such rule. Hamilton replies that this very rule is what makes the Articles helpless. If Congress has only the powers *expressly* listed, and the power to enforce its laws isn't listed, then Congress can't enforce anything.

This debate had a long life. When Congress wrote the Bill of Rights in 1789, it added the **Tenth Amendment**, which reserves to the states or the people the powers "not delegated to the United States." Some members wanted to add the word "expressly," as in the Articles. Madison and others opposed it, and it was left out. In *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819), Chief Justice John Marshall pointed to that missing word as a sign that Congress could have powers that were implied, not just listed.
:::

## No guarantee of the state governments {orig=3-4}

Another major flaw in the federal plan is the lack of a mutual guarantee of the state governments. Nothing like this appears in the Articles. To assume an unwritten guarantee because it would be useful would break the clause I just mentioned even more plainly than assuming an unwritten power to use force. The lack of a guarantee might, in the long run, endanger the Union. But it doesn't attack the Union's existence as directly as the lack of any penalty for breaking its laws.

Without a guarantee, the states must give up any help from the Union against the dangers at home that may sometimes threaten their constitutions. A would-be tyrant might rise up in any state and trample on the people's liberties. Meanwhile, the national government could legally do nothing but watch his power grabs with anger and regret. A victorious faction might build a tyranny on the ruins of order and law. And the Union could not legally send any help to the people who supported the lawful government.

The stormy situation that Massachusetts has only just come through shows that dangers like this are not just theories. Who can say how her recent uprising might have ended if the rebels had been led by a Caesar or a Cromwell? And who can predict what effect a despotism set up in Massachusetts would have on the liberties of New Hampshire or Rhode Island, of Connecticut or New York?

:::vocab
Guaranty (guarantee): A promise to protect or back something up. Here, a promise by the Union to protect each state's government.
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Despotism: Rule by one person with unlimited power.
Malcontents: People who are unhappy with the government and stir up trouble.
:::

:::commentary
**Julius Caesar** was a Roman general who led his army against the Roman Republic's government in 49 BC and made himself dictator. **Oliver Cromwell** led Parliament's army in the English Civil War in the 1640s, and later ruled England as "Lord Protector." Both started as military leaders and ended up holding supreme power. Hamilton's point is that the Shays rebels had no such leader. A more skillful and ambitious one might have set up a dictatorship.

Historians still debate how dangerous Shays' Rebellion really was. To many leaders, like Hamilton and George Washington, it looked like the start of anarchy. Others saw the rebels differently. The farmers were desperate. Massachusetts demanded taxes in hard money that many of them didn't have, and courts were taking their farms. Thomas Jefferson, who was in France at the time, famously wrote that "a little rebellion now and then is a good thing."
:::

## Why a guarantee is not interference {orig=5}

Some people have too much pride in the importance of their own state. They object that a national guarantee would be a meddlesome interference in the members' internal affairs. A worry like this would take away one of the main benefits we expect from union. And it can only come from a misunderstanding of what the provision would do.

A guarantee would not stop a majority of the people from reforming their state constitution in a legal and peaceful way. That right would remain as strong as ever. The guarantee would only work against changes made by violence. We can't have too many safeguards against disasters like that. The peace of society and the stability of government depend completely on how well these safeguards work.

When all the power of a government is in the hands of the people, there's less excuse for violent remedies in times of local or temporary trouble. In a popular or representative government, the natural cure for bad government is a change of officials. A national guarantee would be aimed just as much at the power grabs of rulers as at the riots and outrages of faction and rebellion among the people.

:::quote
The natural cure for an ill-administration, in a popular or representative constitution, is a change of men.

Meaning: In a government where the people vote, the right way to fix bad leadership is to elect new leaders, not to start a rebellion.
:::

:::vocab
Faction: A group of citizens, either a majority or a minority, united by a shared passion or interest that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
Sedition: Speech or action that stirs up rebellion against the government.
:::

:::commentary
The Constitution added exactly the guarantee Hamilton asks for. Article IV, Section 4 says: "The United States shall guarantee to every State in this Union a Republican Form of Government." It also promises to protect each state against invasion and, when the state asks, against "domestic Violence."

Notice that Hamilton says the guarantee protects against *rulers* who seize power, not just against rebels. That's an important balance. The guarantee isn't meant to protect whoever happens to be in charge. It is meant to protect republican government itself.

In practice, the Guarantee Clause has rarely been used by courts. In *Luther v. Borden* (1849), which arose from a dispute over who was the lawful government of Rhode Island, the Supreme Court said it was up to Congress and the President, not judges, to decide whether a state's government was truly republican.
:::

## Quotas are unfair {orig=6-8}

Another basic error in the Confederation is the rule of setting each state's payments to the national treasury by QUOTAS. I have already pointed out that quotas can't meet the nation's needs, and our experience with them has shown it clearly enough. Here I only want to discuss them from the point of view of fairness among the states.

Anyone who has studied what creates national wealth must agree that there is no common standard, no barometer, that can measure how much of it a place has. People have suggested two rules for state payments: the value of land, and the number of people. Neither one has any claim to be a fair measure of wealth.

Compare the wealth of the Netherlands with that of Russia or Germany, or even France. At the same time, compare the total land value and population of that small country with the land value and population of the huge regions of the other three. You'll see right away that land and population don't match the nations' relative wealth at all.

A similar comparison between some of the American states would give the same result. Compare Virginia with North Carolina, Pennsylvania with Connecticut, or Maryland with New Jersey. You'll be convinced that their abilities to pay taxes have little or nothing to do with how much land they have or how many people. The same thing could be shown by comparing counties in the same state. Anyone who knows New York State will agree that the active wealth of Kings County is much greater, compared to Montgomery County, than either land values or population would suggest!

The wealth of nations depends on an endless variety of causes. These include location, soil, climate, and what the land produces. They include the type of government, the character of the citizens, and how well informed they are. They include the state of trade, of the skilled crafts, and of industry. They also include many other things too complicated, too small, or too accidental to list. These create differences in wealth among countries that are almost impossible to imagine.

The result is clear. There can be no common measure of national wealth. So there can be no general, fixed rule for deciding how much tax a state is able to pay. Any attempt to set the payments of a confederacy's members by such a rule is bound to produce glaring inequality and extreme oppression.

In America, this inequality alone would be enough to destroy the Union in the end, if there were any way to force the states to pay their requisitions. The suffering states wouldn't agree for long to stay in a union that spread the public burden so unequally. Such a system would make citizens of some states poor and oppressed. Meanwhile, citizens of other states would hardly notice the small share of the burden they had to carry. And this problem can't be separated from the system of quotas and requisitions.

:::vocab
Quota: A fixed share that each state was supposed to contribute.
Requisition: A formal request. Under the Articles, Congress asked the states for money and soldiers but couldn't make them provide either.
Barometer: An instrument that measures air pressure. Here, any tool for measuring something.
:::

:::commentary
Under Article VIII of the Articles, each state's quota was supposed to be based on the value of its surveyed land, buildings, and improvements. That was hard to measure, and states had every reason to undervalue their own land. In 1783, Congress proposed switching to a rule based on population. That proposal counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a free person. It was never adopted, but the same "three-fifths" ratio later appeared in the Constitution.

Hamilton's examples are well chosen. **Kings County** is Brooklyn, which was then a farming area close to New York City and its trade. **Montgomery County** was then a huge, thinly settled frontier county in the Mohawk Valley, upstate. Hamilton's point is that land near a busy port is worth far more, and its owners can pay far more, than the same amount of land on the frontier.
:::

## A better way: taxes on goods {orig=9-10}

The only way to avoid this problem is to let the national government raise its own money in its own way.

Imposts, excises, and in general all taxes on things people buy can be compared to a liquid. In time, a liquid finds its own level, and these taxes will find their level with people's ability to pay them. The amount each citizen pays will be partly his own choice, and he can adjust it to his resources. The rich may spend freely, and the poor can be thrifty. And careful choice of which goods to tax can always prevent oppression of individuals.

If taxes on certain goods create inequalities in some states, those will probably be balanced by similar inequalities in other states from taxes on other goods. Over time, things will balance out everywhere, as far as that is possible in such a complicated subject. Or, if some inequalities remain, they won't be as large, as constant, or as offensive as the ones that would come from quotas on any scale anyone could design.

Taxes on goods have another great advantage: their own nature protects against excess. They set their own limit, which can't be passed without defeating their purpose, which is to raise more money. In this case, the saying is as true as it is witty: "in political arithmetic, two and two do not always make four." If taxes are too high, people buy less. People also avoid paying, for example by smuggling. So the treasury collects less than it would if taxes were kept within proper and moderate limits. This creates a complete barrier against serious oppression of the citizens by taxes of this kind. It is a natural limit on the power to impose them.

:::vocab
Impost: A tax on imported goods; a duty.
Excise: A tax on goods made or sold within a country, such as liquor. Collecting it often required inspectors to enter homes and businesses.
Consumption: Buying and using goods.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's argument that high taxes can bring in *less* money is an early version of an idea economists still debate. If a tax on tea gets too high, people buy less tea or buy smuggled tea, and the government collects less. In the 1970s and 1980s, the economist Arthur Laffer made a similar idea famous, and it became known as the **Laffer curve**. Economists broadly agree that there is *some* tax rate so high that it brings in less money. They disagree, often sharply, about where that point is.

Hamilton's claim that taxes on goods are fair because the poor "can be frugal" is also debatable. Taxes on necessities like salt or bread fall hardest on the poor, who must buy them no matter what. Hamilton admits this when he says the government must make a "judicious selection" of what to tax.

For most of the 1800s, the national government did get most of its money from tariffs, which are taxes on imported goods, just as Hamilton predicted.
:::

## Direct taxes: count the people {orig=11}

Taxes of this kind are usually called indirect taxes. For a long time, they must make up most of the money raised in this country.

Direct taxes, which mainly fall on land and buildings, can be divided among the states by a fixed rule. Either the value of land or the number of people could serve as the standard. The state of a country's farming and the size of its population have been thought to be closely linked. For this purpose, the number of people is the better rule, because it is simpler and more certain.

In any country, it is an enormous task to measure the value of land. In a country that is only partly settled and is still being developed, it is almost impossible. And an accurate land valuation is always very expensive. In a kind of taxation where nothing in the nature of things limits the government's choices, a fixed rule that still lets the tax do its job may cause fewer problems than leaving those choices completely open.

:::vocab
Direct tax: A tax charged directly on people or property, such as a tax on land or a head tax, rather than on goods that are bought and sold.
Indirect tax: A tax on goods or activities, such as a tax on imports, that is paid when people buy things.
Apportionment: Dividing seats in a legislature (or a tax burden) among states or districts, usually by population.
:::

:::commentary
The Constitution followed Hamilton's advice here. It says that "direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States" according to population, counted by a census every ten years.

That population count included the **three-fifths clause**: each enslaved person counted as three-fifths of a person, both for taxes and for seats in the House of Representatives. Hamilton doesn't mention slavery here, but the "numbers of the people" used for direct taxes were tied up with it from the start. Madison discusses this compromise in [No. 54](/papers/54/).

The rule on direct taxes later mattered a great deal. In *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.* (1895), the Supreme Court ruled that a federal tax on income from property, such as rents and investments, was a direct tax. That meant it had to be divided among the states by population, which made an income tax nearly impossible to collect. The **Sixteenth Amendment** (1913) changed that by allowing an income tax without apportionment.
:::

:::today
Shays' Rebellion raised a question that still matters: when is resistance to the government justified, and how should a government respond? Hamilton's answer is that in a republic, where people can vote out bad leaders, violence is rarely justified. But the rebels felt the system wasn't listening to them. Today, Americans still debate how governments should respond to protests, and what people should do when they feel their votes don't matter. Hamilton's argument about taxes is also still alive. Politicians and economists keep arguing about whether cutting taxes can raise more money, and about whether sales taxes are fair to the poor.
:::
