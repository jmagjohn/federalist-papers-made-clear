:::summary
Hamilton finishes his discussion of appointments and then wraps up his long study of the presidency. He claims that the Senate's consent would be needed to remove officials as well as to appoint them, which would keep a new president from firing good officials just to reward friends. (Congress and, later, the Supreme Court rejected that view.) He answers critics who say the Senate's role would give the president too much influence over the Senate, or the Senate too much over the president. He compares the Constitution's open system with New York's secretive Council of Appointment, which hides responsibility and invites deal-making. He briefly reviews the president's other powers, such as reporting on the state of the Union and receiving ambassadors. Finally, he concludes that the presidency combines the energy good government needs with the safety a republic requires.
:::

:::keypoints
- Hamilton says the Senate's consent would be needed **to remove officials as well as to appoint them**, which would make the government more **stable**. This view was later rejected in practice.
- The critics can't agree whether the Senate's role helps the president control the Senate or helps the Senate control the president. That is **strong proof that neither is true**.
- The power to give out offices is more likely to attract influence than the power merely to **block** them.
- In the Constitution's plan, the public will know **who is to blame**: the president for a bad nomination, the Senate for rejecting a good one, and both for a bad appointment.
- New York's **Council of Appointment** meets in secret, so no one knows who is responsible. Any small council invites **vote trading** and favoritism.
- The **House of Representatives** is too large and changes too often to share in appointments.
- The president's remaining powers, like the **State of the Union** report and **receiving ambassadors**, have drawn few serious objections.
- The presidency is **safe** because the president is elected every four years, can be **impeached**, and must share key powers with the Senate.
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of Hamilton's eleven essays on the presidency, which began with [No. 67](/papers/67/). In [No. 70](/papers/70/), he listed what makes an executive strong (unity, duration, support, and competent powers) and what makes it safe (dependence on the people and responsibility). In [No. 76](/papers/76/), he explained why the president nominates officials and the Senate confirms them. Here he finishes that topic and sums up.

Some Anti-Federalists, including George Mason of Virginia, complained that the Constitution gave the president no executive council. They wanted a small council to share in appointments, like the one New York already had. Hamilton, a New Yorker, had seen how that council worked, and he did not like it.
:::

## The Senate and the removal of officials {orig=1}

I mentioned that one advantage of the Senate's role in appointments is that it would help make the administration stable. The Senate's consent would be needed to remove officials as well as to appoint them. So a change of president would not cause as sweeping a turnover of government officials as we might expect if he alone controlled all offices.

Suppose an official had given good proof that he was fit for his job. A new president would hesitate to replace him with someone he liked better. He would fear that the Senate's disapproval might defeat his attempt and bring him some disgrace. People who best understand the value of a steady administration will be the most eager to value this rule. It ties the careers of public officials to the approval or disapproval of the Senate. Because its membership changes slowly, the Senate will probably be less changeable than any other part of the government.

:::quote
The consent of that body would be necessary to displace as well as to appoint.

Meaning: Hamilton claims the Senate would have to approve not only hiring officials but also firing them.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Hamilton's best-known mistakes, or at least one of his most debated claims. The Constitution says nothing about how officials are removed. Hamilton read the Senate's role in appointments to cover removals too.

The First Congress soon rejected that view. In 1789, while creating the first executive departments, members debated who could remove a department head. James Madison argued that the president must be able to remove officials on his own, or he could not be held responsible for how the laws were carried out. Congress wrote the laws in a way that assumed the president had this power, and presidents have used it ever since. Later, in 1867, Congress passed the Tenure of Office Act, which required the Senate's consent to remove certain officials. President Andrew Johnson's firing of his secretary of war in defiance of that law was the main charge in his 1868 impeachment. In *Myers v. United States* (1926), the Supreme Court, in an opinion by Chief Justice (and former president) William Howard Taft, held that the president may remove executive officers he has appointed without the Senate's consent.

Some scholars have argued that Hamilton meant something narrower: that the Senate would have to approve a replacement, not the removal itself. Either way, the question of how far Congress may limit the president's power to fire officials is still argued today. In *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935), the Court allowed Congress to protect members of certain independent agencies from being fired at will, and the Court has continued to revisit that issue in recent years.
:::

:::vocab
Administration: The day-to-day running of the government, and the officials who do it.
Approbation: Approval.
:::

## Who would influence whom? {orig=2-4}

Critics have made two opposite claims about joining the Senate with the president in appointments. Some say it would give the president too much influence over the Senate. Others say it would do the reverse. That is strong proof that neither claim is true.

To state the first claim correctly is to disprove it. It comes down to this: the president would have improper influence over the Senate *because the Senate would have the power to restrain him*. That makes no sense. There can be no doubt that complete power of appointment would let him build a dangerous control over the Senate far more effectively than a mere power to nominate, subject to the Senate's approval.

Now look at the opposite claim: "the Senate would influence the president." As I have noted in other cases, the objection is so vague that it is hard to answer precisely. How would this influence be used? About what? The power to influence a person, in this sense, must mean the power to give him some benefit. How could the Senate benefit the president by the way it used its right to reject his nominees?

Someone might say the Senate could sometimes please him by agreeing to a favorite choice of his, when the public good called for a different decision. I answer that there would be too few cases in which the president had a personal interest in the result for the Senate's favors to sway him much. The POWER that can hand out honors and salaries is more likely to attract others than to be attracted by the POWER that can only block them.

If "influencing" the president means restraining him, then that is exactly what was intended. And I have shown that this restraint would be healthy. It would not destroy a single advantage we could expect from letting the president act without any control. The right to nominate would produce all the good of the power to appoint, and would largely avoid its evils.

:::note A note on the original text
Early editions end ¶4 in two slightly different ways. One says the right of nomination "would produce all the good, without the ill." The other says it would produce "all the good of that of appointment, and would in a great measure avoid its evils." Our plain version follows the second.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton enjoys pointing out that his opponents contradict each other. If some critics say a plan helps the president too much, and others say it helps the Senate too much, perhaps it is balanced about right. This is a clever debating move, though it doesn't prove that both critics are wrong.
:::

## An open system versus a secret council {orig=5-7}

When we compare the Constitution's plan for appointing officers with the one in New York's constitution, we must strongly prefer the former. In the Constitution's plan, the power to nominate clearly belongs to the president. And since every nomination must be submitted to the judgment of a whole branch of the legislature, the details of each appointment will naturally become publicly known. The public will easily see what part each actor played.

The blame for a bad nomination will fall on the president alone and completely. The blame for rejecting a good one will lie entirely with the Senate. It will be made worse by the fact that the Senate blocked the president's good intentions. If a bad appointment is made, the president for nominating, and the Senate for approving, will share the shame and disgrace, though in different degrees.

In New York, appointments work in the opposite way. The Council of Appointment has three to five members, one of whom is always the governor. This small group shuts itself up in a private room, hidden from the public eye, to carry out its duty. It is known that the governor claims the right to nominate, based on some unclear wording in the state constitution. But no one knows how far, or in what way, he uses that right. No one knows when the other members contradict or oppose him. Since no one knows who is responsible for a bad appointment, there is no clear target for blame, and the blame has no sting and doesn't last. And while the field lies wide open for scheming and plotting, any idea of responsibility is lost.

The most the public can know is this:

- the governor claims the right to nominate;
- two of the small number of four other men can too often be managed without much trouble;
- if some members of a particular council happen to be uncooperative, their opposition can often be avoided by scheduling meetings at times when they find it hard to attend;
- and, for whatever reason, a great number of very improper appointments are made from time to time.

Does a governor of this state use his strong influence over this delicate and important part of government to give offices to the best-qualified men? Or does he abuse it to promote people whose main merit is their blind loyalty to him, and to support a contemptible and dangerous system of personal influence? Unfortunately for the community, we can only guess.

Every council whose only job is appointments, however it is designed, will be a closed meeting where scheming and plotting have full play. Without an unjustified increase in cost, its membership cannot be large enough to keep its members from easily joining together. And since each member will have friends and relatives to provide for, the wish to do each other favors will lead to a shameful trading of votes and bargaining for jobs. The private attachments of one man might easily be satisfied. But satisfying the private attachments of a dozen or twenty men would put all the main jobs in the government into the hands of a few families. That would lead more directly to an aristocracy or an oligarchy than any other plan we could invent.

To avoid piling up offices in a few hands, the council's members might be changed often. But then we would suffer all the harm of an unstable administration. Such a council would also be more open to the president's influence than the Senate, because it would be smaller and would work less in public view.

In short, if such a council replaced the Convention's plan, it would produce:

- more expense;
- more of the harm that comes from favoritism and scheming in handing out public honors;
- less stability in the government;
- and less security against improper influence by the executive.

And yet some have argued hard for such a council as an essential amendment to the Constitution.

:::commentary
Hamilton's attack on New York's Council of Appointment was also an attack on the state's governor, George Clinton, a leading opponent of the Constitution. Clinton and his allies were accused of using the council to reward supporters with offices. Hamilton is careful to say the public "can only guess" how the governor uses his influence, but his readers would have understood the hint.

The central point is about **transparency** and **responsibility**. Under the Constitution, a nomination is public, the Senate's vote is on the record, and voters know whom to credit or blame. In a closed council, responsibility disappears. New York later abolished its Council of Appointment in its 1821 constitution.
:::

:::vocab
Cabal: A small, secret group plotting together, usually to gain power.
Intrigue: Secret scheming.
Conclave: A private, closed meeting.
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged upper class.
Oligarchy: Government by a small group of people, usually the rich or powerful.
Opprobrium: Harsh criticism or public disgrace.
:::

## Why the House should not share in appointments {orig=8}

I can't properly finish my remarks on appointments without mentioning a plan that has a few supporters, though only a few. I mean the idea of letting the House of Representatives share in making appointments. I will do little more than mention it, because I can't imagine that any large part of the community will support it.

A body that changes so often and is also so large can never be suited to this power. Its unfitness will be obvious to everyone when we remember that in fifty years it may have three or four hundred members. Joining it to the process would destroy all the advantages of the stability of both the president and the Senate. It would also cause endless delays and difficulties. The example of most of the states, in their own constitutions, encourages us to reject the idea.

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction about the size of the House was close, though his timing was early. The House grew as the country grew, reaching 435 members in 1913, where it has stayed since, except briefly. The House today plays no role in confirming nominees, with one exception: under the 25th Amendment (1967), when the vice presidency is vacant, the president's choice for a new vice president must be approved by both houses of Congress.
:::

## The president's remaining powers {orig=9-10}

The only remaining powers of the executive are these:

- giving Congress information about the state of the Union;
- recommending for its consideration the measures he thinks necessary;
- calling both houses, or either one, into session on special occasions;
- adjourning them when they cannot agree on a time to adjourn;
- receiving ambassadors and other public ministers;
- faithfully carrying out the laws;
- and commissioning all the officers of the United States.

Apart from some petty complaints about the power to call either house into session and the power to receive ambassadors, no one has objected to these powers. Nor could anyone reasonably object. In fact, it took an endless appetite for criticism to find fault even with the parts that have been criticized.

As for the power to call either house into session, I will just say this. At least for the Senate, we can easily see a good reason for it. The Senate shares the power to make treaties with the president. So it might often be necessary to call the Senate together for that purpose when it would be unnecessary and improper to call the House. As for receiving ambassadors, what I said in an earlier paper is a sufficient answer.

:::commentary
These short clauses have grown into important parts of the modern presidency. The duty to give Congress information on "the State of the Union" became the yearly State of the Union address. George Washington and John Adams delivered it in person. Thomas Jefferson sent a written message instead, and presidents did the same for over a century until Woodrow Wilson returned to giving it in person in 1913. Presidents have called Congress into special session in emergencies, as Abraham Lincoln did in 1861 at the start of the Civil War. No president has ever used the power to adjourn Congress.

In [No. 69](/papers/69/), Hamilton said receiving ambassadors was "more a matter of dignity than of authority." It turned out to matter a great deal more. By deciding which foreign ambassadors to receive, the president decides which foreign governments the United States officially recognizes. In *Zivotofsky v. Kerry* (2015), the Supreme Court held that this recognition power belongs to the president alone. The duty to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" has also become the basis for many arguments about how much control the president has over the executive branch.
:::

:::vocab
State of the Union: The president's report to Congress on the condition of the country. Today it is usually given as a speech each year.
Adjourn: To end a meeting or session, either for a break or until the next session.
Ambassador: An official who represents a country in a foreign nation.
Treaty: A formal written agreement between nations.
Cavil: A petty or unnecessary objection.
:::

## Strong enough, and safe enough {orig=11}

We have now finished surveying the structure and powers of the executive branch. I have tried to show that, as far as republican principles allow, it combines all the ingredients of energy. The remaining question is this: does it also combine the ingredients of safety in a republican sense, a proper dependence on the people and a proper responsibility?

The answer has already been given in our study of its other features. It follows clearly from these facts:

- The president is elected once every four years by electors chosen directly by the people for that purpose.
- He can be impeached at any time and tried. If convicted, he can be removed from office and barred from holding any other office.
- After that, he can lose his life and property through prosecution in the ordinary courts.

But these precautions, great as they are, are not the only ones the Convention's plan provides for the public's safety. In the only cases where the abuse of executive power was seriously to be feared, the plan places the president under the control of one branch of the legislature, the Senate. What more could an enlightened and reasonable people want?

:::quote
What more could be desired by an enlightened and reasonable people?

Meaning: Hamilton closes his case by saying the presidency is both strong and safe, and he challenges readers to name anything more they could fairly ask for.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton ends where [No. 70](/papers/70/) began: a good executive must have both **energy** and **safety**. The "only instances" where he thinks abuse was seriously to be feared are appointments and treaties, which require the Senate's approval.

Hamilton assumed that the electors would be chosen directly by the people. In fact, the Constitution lets each state legislature decide how its electors are chosen. In the early years, several legislatures chose electors themselves. Over time, every state moved to letting the voters choose them.

Was the presidency as safe as Hamilton claimed? Supporters of his view point out that presidents have stood for election, faced investigations and impeachments, and handed over power peacefully. Critics argue that powers Hamilton treated as minor, like receiving ambassadors or directing the military, have grown far larger than he described, and that the checks he relied on, like the Senate, work differently in an age of strong political parties.
:::

:::today
The biggest legacy of this essay is a claim that didn't hold up. Whether the president can fire executive officials at will, or whether Congress can protect some of them, is one of the most important questions about presidential power today. Supporters of a "unitary executive" say the president must be able to remove officials in order to be responsible for the whole executive branch, which was Madison's argument in 1789. Others argue that Congress should be able to protect some officials, such as members of independent agencies, from being fired for political reasons, and some point to Hamilton's words here as support. Hamilton's larger point is less debated: appointments should be made in the open, so the public knows whom to hold responsible.
:::
