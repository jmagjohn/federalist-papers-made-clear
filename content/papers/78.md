:::summary
Hamilton explains why federal judges should serve "during good behavior," which in practice means for life unless they are impeached. The courts are the "least dangerous" branch, he says, because they control neither the army nor the money. They have "neither force nor will, but merely judgment." Because they are so weak, they need permanent jobs to stay independent. That independence matters, because courts have a crucial duty: when a law conflicts with the Constitution, judges must follow the Constitution and treat the law as void. This doesn't make judges superior to Congress. It means the people, who made the Constitution, are superior to both. This idea is now called **judicial review**.
:::

:::keypoints
- Federal judges serve **"during good behavior,"** so they can't be removed for unpopular decisions.
- The judiciary is the **weakest branch**. It has no army and no money, only the power to judge, and it relies on the executive to enforce its rulings.
- In a **limited Constitution**, courts must declare laws that violate the Constitution **void**. Otherwise, written limits on power mean nothing.
- This doesn't make judges more powerful than lawmakers. It makes **the people's will, expressed in the Constitution,** more powerful than the legislature's will.
- Judges must use **judgment, not will**. If they substituted their own preferences, that would be an abuse.
- Independent judges also protect individuals and minorities from **temporary waves of public anger**.
- Lifetime terms help attract **skilled lawyers**, since few people have the knowledge and character needed to be good judges.
:::

:::context Background
Nos. 78–83 cover Article III of the Constitution, which creates the federal courts. Unlike most of the series, these essays first appeared in a two-volume book edition of *The Federalist* in May 1788, before they were reprinted in newspapers.

Hamilton was responding mainly to the Anti-Federalist writer "Brutus," who warned that federal judges would be dangerously powerful. Brutus argued that judges serving for life, answerable to no one, would interpret the Constitution however they liked and slowly expand national power. Hamilton's reply became the most influential defense of judicial independence in American history.
:::

## What this essay covers {orig=1-5}

We now turn to the judicial branch of the proposed government.

Earlier essays, in explaining the defects of the current Confederation, already showed why a national court system is useful and necessary. There's no need to repeat those points, since no one really disputes that the nation should have courts. The only questions people have raised are about how the courts should be set up and how far their power should reach. So we will focus on those questions.

Setting up the courts involves three things: first, how judges are appointed; second, how long they serve; and third, how judicial power is divided among different courts and how those courts relate to each other.

First, the way judges are appointed is the same as for national officials in general. That was fully discussed in the last two essays, so anything more here would just be repetition.

Second, the terms on which judges hold their jobs. This mainly concerns how long they serve, how they are paid, and how they are held responsible.

:::commentary
Under the Constitution, the president nominates federal judges and the Senate must confirm them. Hamilton discussed this appointment process in [No. 76](/papers/76/) and [No. 77](/papers/77/). He covers judges' pay and accountability in [No. 79](/papers/79/), and the structure of the courts in Nos. 80–83.
:::

## Why judges serve "during good behavior" {orig=6}

Under the Convention's plan, all federal judges will hold their offices during good behavior. This matches the best state constitutions, including New York's. The fact that the plan's opponents have even questioned this rule shows how badly their urge to object has clouded their imagination and judgment.

The "good behavior" standard for judges is certainly one of the most valuable modern improvements in government. In a monarchy, it is an excellent barrier against the tyranny of the king. In a republic, it is just as excellent a barrier against attacks and oppression by the legislature. And it is the best method anyone could invent, in any government, to make sure the laws are applied in a steady, honest, and fair way.

:::vocab
Good behavior: The rule that federal judges keep their jobs as long as they behave properly. In practice it means a lifetime appointment, since the only way to remove them is impeachment.
Judiciary: The branch of government made up of courts and judges.
Tenure: The terms on which someone holds a job, especially how long they get to keep it.
:::

:::commentary
"Good behavior" tenure came from England. For centuries, English judges served "at the pleasure" of the king, who could fire them for ruling against him. After 1701, English judges kept their jobs during good behavior, which made them far more independent. The Declaration of Independence actually lists the opposite practice as a complaint: King George III "made Judges dependent on his Will alone, for the tenure of their offices."
:::

## The least dangerous branch {orig=7-8}

Anyone who carefully considers the different branches of power will see that, in a government where they are separated, the judiciary will always be the least dangerous to the political rights protected by the Constitution. That's because, by its nature, it is the branch least able to harm them.

The executive not only hands out honors and offices, but also holds the sword of the community. The legislature not only controls the purse, but also makes the rules that govern every citizen's rights and duties. The judiciary, by contrast, has no influence over either the sword or the purse. It doesn't direct the strength or the wealth of the society, and it can't take any active steps on its own. It truly has neither FORCE nor WILL, but only judgment. It even has to depend on the executive branch to carry out its decisions.

This simple observation leads to several important conclusions. First, it proves beyond question that the judiciary is by far the weakest of the three branches. It can never successfully attack either of the other two, and it needs every possible protection to defend itself against their attacks.

Second, it proves that even though courts may sometimes treat individuals unjustly, they can never endanger the general liberty of the people, as long as the judiciary stays truly separate from both the legislature and the executive. I agree with Montesquieu that "there is no liberty, if the power of judging be not separated from the legislative and executive powers."

Finally, it proves several connected points. Liberty has nothing to fear from the judiciary alone, but it would have everything to fear if the judiciary joined with either of the other branches. That kind of union would happen if judges depended on another branch, even if the branches looked separate on paper. Because the judiciary is naturally weak, it is always in danger of being overpowered, intimidated, or influenced by the other branches. And nothing can make it as firm and independent as permanent tenure. So permanent tenure is an essential part of the judiciary. It is, to a great degree, the fortress that protects public justice and public safety.

:::quote
It may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment; and must ultimately depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments.

Meaning: Courts can't make anyone obey them. They can only decide what the law means, and they rely on the president's branch to enforce those decisions.
:::

:::commentary
This is the source of the famous phrase "the least dangerous branch," which is now the title of a well-known book about the Supreme Court. Hamilton's point is that courts only have power if people accept their rulings. History offers examples on both sides. In 1832, President Andrew Jackson ignored a Supreme Court ruling protecting the Cherokee Nation, and the Cherokee were later forced from their land on the Trail of Tears. In 1957, President Eisenhower sent federal troops to Little Rock, Arkansas, to enforce the Court's school-desegregation ruling in *Brown v. Board of Education*. In both cases, the Court depended on "the executive arm."

Was Hamilton right that the courts would stay the weakest branch? Many people today think the Supreme Court has become extremely powerful, deciding questions about abortion, guns, elections, and health care. Brutus predicted exactly this. Others argue the Court is still limited because it can only decide cases brought to it, and its rulings can be undone by constitutional amendments.
:::

:::vocab
The sword and the purse: Shorthand for military power (controlled by the executive) and money (controlled by the legislature).
Montesquieu: A French philosopher (1689–1755) whose book *The Spirit of the Laws* made the idea of separation of powers famous. The founders quoted him often.
:::

## Why courts must strike down unconstitutional laws {orig=9-11}

The complete independence of the courts is especially essential in a limited Constitution. By a limited Constitution, I mean one that sets specific limits on the legislature's power. For example, it might say the legislature cannot pass bills of attainder or ex post facto laws. Limits like these can only be enforced in practice through the courts. It must be the courts' duty to declare void any act that clearly goes against the Constitution. Without this, all the protections for particular rights and privileges would mean nothing.

Some people are confused about the courts' right to declare laws void because they violate the Constitution. They imagine it would make the judiciary superior to the legislature. They argue that any authority that can cancel another's acts must be superior to it. Since this idea is very important in all the American constitutions, it is worth briefly explaining what it rests on.

No principle is clearer than this: any act by an agent that goes against the terms of the authority given to it is void. So no law that goes against the Constitution can be valid. To deny this would be to say that the deputy is greater than the boss, that the servant is above the master, that the people's representatives are superior to the people themselves, and that people acting under granted powers may do not only what those powers don't allow, but what they forbid.

:::vocab
Limited Constitution: A constitution that puts specific limits on what the government, especially the legislature, may do.
Bill of attainder: A law that declares a specific person or group guilty of a crime and punishes them without a trial. The Constitution forbids these.
Ex post facto law: A law that makes something a crime after it was done, or increases the punishment for a past act. Latin for "after the fact." The Constitution forbids these.
Void: Having no legal force at all, as if it never existed.
:::

:::commentary
This passage contains the core argument for what we now call **judicial review**: the power of courts to strike down laws that violate the Constitution. Notice that the Constitution never says courts have this power. Hamilton argues it follows logically from having a written constitution with limits. If Congress could ignore the limits whenever it wanted, the limits would be meaningless.

Fifteen years later, in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), Chief Justice John Marshall used nearly the same reasoning to establish judicial review as part of American law. Many scholars believe Marshall borrowed directly from this essay.
:::

## Judges aren't superior. The people are. {orig=12-13}

Someone might say that the legislature itself is the constitutional judge of its own powers, and that its interpretation must be accepted by the other branches. The answer is that this can't be the natural assumption, unless some specific part of the Constitution says so. We shouldn't suppose the Constitution meant to let the people's representatives replace the people's will with their own.

It makes far more sense to assume the courts were designed to stand between the people and the legislature, partly to keep the legislature within the limits of its authority. Interpreting the laws is the proper and special job of the courts. A constitution is, in fact, a fundamental law, and judges must treat it that way. So it is up to judges to work out its meaning, just as they work out the meaning of any law passed by the legislature. If the two conflict in a way that can't be reconciled, the one with greater authority must win. In other words, the Constitution should be preferred over the statute, and the intention of the people over the intention of their agents.

This conclusion doesn't mean the judiciary is superior to the legislature. It only means that the power of the people is superior to both. When the will of the legislature, expressed in its laws, conflicts with the will of the people, expressed in the Constitution, judges should follow the people's will. They should base their decisions on the fundamental laws, not on laws that are not fundamental.

:::quote
...the Constitution ought to be preferred to the statute, the intention of the people to the intention of their agents.

Meaning: The Constitution comes from the people. Ordinary laws come from the people's representatives. When the two clash, the people's own choice wins.
:::

:::vocab
Statute: A law passed by a legislature.
Constituents: The people a representative serves.
:::

## How judges choose between conflicting laws {orig=14-16}

Judges already do something similar in a familiar situation. Sometimes two laws exist at the same time and conflict, completely or partly, and neither one says it repeals the other. In that case, it is the courts' job to settle their meaning and effect. If the laws can be reconciled through any fair reading, reason and law both say the courts should do that. If that's impossible, the courts have no choice but to apply one and set aside the other.

The rule courts use is that the later law wins over the earlier one. But this rule doesn't come from any law. It comes from the nature of things and common sense. The courts adopted it themselves as interpreters of the law. They thought it reasonable that when two acts of an EQUAL authority conflict, its most recent expression of its will should win.

But when the conflicting acts come from a superior authority and a lower one, from an original power and a power that depends on it, common sense points to the opposite rule. The earlier act of the superior authority should win over the later act of the lower one. So whenever a particular law goes against the Constitution, the courts have a duty to follow the Constitution and disregard the law.

Some argue that courts, pretending to find a conflict, might put their own preferences in place of what the legislature constitutionally intended. But that could just as easily happen when two ordinary laws conflict, or whenever courts interpret any single law. The courts must declare what the law means. If they chose to exercise WILL instead of JUDGMENT, the result would be the same: their preferences would replace the legislature's. If this objection proved anything, it would prove that there should be no judges separate from the legislature at all.

:::commentary
Hamilton draws a sharp line between **judgment** (figuring out what the law requires) and **will** (deciding what you want the result to be). Judges are supposed to do the first, never the second. This distinction is at the center of today's debates about the courts. When people accuse judges of being "activists" or "legislating from the bench," they are accusing them of exercising will instead of judgment. People on the left and the right make this charge, usually about rulings they disagree with.

Hamilton's answer to the danger is honest: yes, judges could abuse their power, but so could any official. The risk doesn't disappear by getting rid of independent courts. It just moves somewhere else.
:::

## Independence protects the people from their own passing anger {orig=17-18}

If courts are to be the protectors of a limited Constitution against the legislature's attacks, that is a strong argument for permanent judicial tenure. Nothing will do more to build the independent spirit judges need to carry out such a hard duty faithfully.

Judicial independence is also needed to protect the Constitution and individual rights from bad moods that sometimes spread among the people themselves. These moods are stirred up by scheming individuals or by particular events. They usually give way quickly to better information and more careful thought. But in the meantime, they can lead to dangerous changes in government and serious oppression of the minority.

I trust that friends of the Constitution will never join its enemies in questioning a basic principle of republican government: the people have the right to change or abolish their Constitution whenever they find it no longer serves their happiness. But this principle doesn't mean the representatives may violate the Constitution whenever a passing wish grips a majority of voters. Nor are the courts any more obligated to look the other way at such violations than at violations cooked up by the legislature alone.

Until the people, by some formal and official act, have canceled or changed the existing Constitution, it binds them all, as a group and as individuals. No assumption about their feelings, or even knowledge of them, can justify their representatives in breaking from the Constitution before such an act. But it's easy to see that judges would need unusual courage to act as faithful guardians of the Constitution when the majority of the community was demanding that it be violated.

:::commentary
Here Hamilton makes a subtle but important point. The people *can* change their Constitution, but they have to do it the right way, through the formal amendment process in Article V. A temporary majority can't just ignore the Constitution because public opinion has shifted. Until the Constitution is officially changed, it binds everyone, including the majority.

This is why independent judges matter most when they are unpopular. A judge who can be fired by voters or politicians might give in to public pressure. Hamilton admits that standing up to an angry majority requires "an uncommon portion of fortitude," or courage.
:::

:::vocab
Fortitude: Courage in the face of pain, danger, or pressure.
Ill humors: Bad moods or passions. In the 1700s, "humors" also referred to the body's fluids, which were thought to control health and mood.
:::

## Courts also soften unjust laws {orig=19}

Judicial independence protects against more than violations of the Constitution. Sometimes these bad moods in society lead only to unjust and one-sided laws that harm the private rights of particular groups of citizens. Here, too, firm judges are very important. They can soften the harshness of such laws and limit how far they reach.

This doesn't just reduce the harm of unjust laws already passed. It also checks the legislature when it considers passing them. Lawmakers who know that the courts' sense of justice will stand in their way are, in a sense, forced by their own unjust motives to tone down their plans. This will shape the character of our governments more than most people realize.

The benefits of honest and moderate judges have already been felt in more than one state. Those judges may have displeased people whose unfair hopes they blocked. But they must have earned the respect and praise of all good and fair-minded people.

Thoughtful people of every kind should value anything that builds or strengthens that spirit in the courts. No one can be sure that he won't be tomorrow's victim of the same spirit of injustice that benefits him today. And everyone must see by now that such a spirit tends to destroy public and private trust, replacing it with widespread distrust and hardship.

:::commentary
"No man can be sure that he may not be to-morrow the victim of a spirit of injustice, by which he may be a gainer to-day." This is a powerful argument for protecting rights you don't personally need right now. The majority on one issue may be the minority on the next. Courts that protect today's unpopular group may protect you tomorrow.

Hamilton mentions state judges who had already resisted unjust laws. He probably had in mind cases like *Trevett v. Weeden* (1786), where Rhode Island judges refused to enforce a law punishing people who wouldn't accept the state's paper money without a jury trial.
:::

## Why temporary terms wouldn't work {orig=20}

The firm, consistent loyalty to the Constitution and to individual rights that we need from the courts can't be expected from judges who hold their jobs temporarily. Appointing judges for limited terms, however it was arranged and whoever made the appointments, would destroy the independence they need.

If the president or the legislature had the power to reappoint them, judges would be tempted to please whichever branch held that power. If both branches shared it, judges would be unwilling to anger either one. If the people, or people elected for this purpose, chose them, judges would be too tempted to seek popularity. We couldn't trust that they were following nothing but the Constitution and the laws.

:::commentary
Hamilton's argument here is relevant to a real modern debate. Most U.S. states today elect at least some of their judges, which is exactly what Hamilton warned against. Supporters of judicial elections say they keep judges accountable to the people. Critics say they force judges to raise campaign money and worry about how their rulings will play with voters. Some people have also proposed term limits for Supreme Court justices, such as 18-year terms. Hamilton would likely object that any fixed term creates pressure to please someone.
:::

## Good judges are hard to find {orig=21-22}

There is a further and even weightier reason for permanent judicial offices, based on the qualifications judges need. People have often pointed out that a large body of laws is one of the unavoidable costs of the benefits of free government. To keep courts from deciding cases however they please, they must be bound by strict rules and precedents that define their duty in each case. Human foolishness and wickedness create so many different kinds of disputes that the records of those precedents are bound to grow huge. Mastering them takes long and difficult study.

So only a few people in society will have enough legal skill to qualify as judges. And allowing for the ordinary flaws of human nature, even fewer will combine the necessary knowledge with the necessary honesty. This shows that the government won't have many qualified people to choose from. Temporary terms would discourage such people from giving up a well-paid law practice to become judges. That would tend to put the administration of justice into the hands of people less able and less qualified to handle it well and with dignity.

In this country's present situation, and for a long time to come, these disadvantages would be greater than they might first seem. But to be honest, they are much less serious than the other problems discussed above.

Overall, there is no doubt that the Convention acted wisely in copying the state constitutions that let judges serve during good behavior. Far from deserving blame for this, their plan would have been inexcusably flawed without this important feature of good government. Great Britain's experience is a shining example of how well it works.

:::vocab
Precedent: An earlier court decision that guides how later, similar cases are decided.
:::

:::today
Federalist No. 78 is one of the most-cited essays in Supreme Court opinions. Every major fight over the Court today connects to it. When people argue about whether justices should have term limits, whether the Court should be expanded, or whether a ruling was "judgment" or "will," they are arguing about the questions Hamilton raised. His key claim, that courts can strike down laws because the Constitution reflects the people's will, is still the main defense of judicial review. His prediction that the courts would remain the "least dangerous" branch is still debated.
:::
