:::summary
Hamilton turns from trade to taxes. Trade is the greatest source of national wealth, he says, and it helps farmers as much as merchants, because it raises the value of land. It also puts more money into circulation, which makes taxes easier to pay. In America, direct taxes on land and property have failed again and again, so the government must rely mainly on **duties on imports**. A single national government could collect those duties far better than separate states. If the states were divided, smugglers could easily slip goods across state lines and rivers. United, the country would have only one side to guard, the Atlantic coast. Without that revenue, the whole burden of taxes would fall on the owners of land.
:::

:::keypoints
- **Trade** is the most useful and productive source of national wealth.
- Farming and trade are not rivals. **When trade grows, land rises in value.**
- A country's ability to pay taxes depends on **how much money circulates** and how fast, and trade increases both.
- In America, **direct taxes** on land and property have failed. People won't accept **excise** inspectors either.
- So the government must depend mainly on **duties on imports**, as even wealthy Britain does.
- Separate states, with shared rivers, bays, and borders, could never stop **smuggling** between them, unless they used harsh methods like France's army of 20,000 patrols.
- One national government would have just **one side to guard**: the Atlantic coast. Distance from Europe makes direct smuggling hard.
- National import duties could be **tripled**. A tax on **liquor** alone could raise a large sum.
- Without this revenue, the tax burden would fall **oppressively on farmers** and the treasury would still be empty.
:::

:::context Background
Money was the national government's biggest problem in the 1780s. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress could not tax at all. It could only ask the states for money, and they often didn't pay (see [No. 15](/papers/15/)). Twice Congress asked the states for permission to collect a 5 percent tax on imports. Changing the Articles required every state to agree, and each time a single state blocked the plan: Rhode Island in 1782, and later New York.

Meanwhile, the states tried to raise money on their own, often through direct taxes on land and people. In Massachusetts, heavy taxes that had to be paid in scarce hard money helped spark Shays' Rebellion. This essay explains why Hamilton thought the answer was a national tax on imports, collected by a national government.
:::

## Trade creates wealth {orig=1-2}

I have described well enough what the Union would do for the states' trade. Now I will look at how it would help the government raise revenue.

All wise statesmen now see and admit that a prosperous trade is the most useful, as well as the most productive, source of national wealth, and so it has become one of their main political concerns. Trade multiplies the goods people can enjoy. It brings in gold and silver and keeps them moving, and these precious metals are the favorite objects of human greed and enterprise. In this way, trade brings life and energy to every channel of work and makes it flow more busily and more fully. The hardworking merchant, the farmer, the skilled worker, and the manufacturer, people of every kind, look forward eagerly to this welcome reward for their labor.

People used to argue often about whether farming or trade was more important. Clear experience has now settled that question. It has ended the old rivalry between them, and it has proven, to the satisfaction of their supporters, that their interests are closely woven together.

In many countries, land has risen in value as trade has grown. And how could it be otherwise? Trade gives farmers wider markets for their crops and new reasons to farm more land, and it is the most powerful way to increase the amount of money in a country. In short, it is the faithful servant of every kind of work and industry. How could it fail to raise the value of land, which is the source of most of the things that work and industry produce?

It is amazing that such a simple truth ever had opponents. It is one of many proofs of how easily people can be led away from the plainest truths of reason. Sometimes it happens through jealousy based on ignorance. Sometimes it happens through theories that are too abstract and too clever.

:::vocab
Revenue: Money a government collects, mainly through taxes, to pay for its work.
Husbandman: An old word for a farmer.
Mechanic: In 1787, a skilled worker who made things by hand, like a carpenter, blacksmith, or shoemaker.
:::

:::commentary
The "often-agitated question between agriculture and commerce" was a real argument in Hamilton's time and became more heated afterward. Some thinkers, especially a group of French economists called the Physiocrats, believed that land was the only true source of wealth. In America, many people saw farmers as the most virtuous citizens and distrusted merchants and bankers.

Hamilton insists that farming and trade help each other. Thomas Jefferson, who would become his great political rival, praised farmers as "the chosen people of God" in his *Notes on the State of Virginia*. The two men's different visions, an America of trade and industry versus an America of independent farmers, shaped the first political parties in the 1790s.
:::

## Trade makes taxes easier to pay {orig=3}

A country's ability to pay taxes must always depend largely on how much money is in circulation, and on how fast it moves from hand to hand. Trade increases both, so it must make taxes easier to pay and help supply the treasury with what it needs.

Look at the hereditary lands of the Emperor of Germany. They cover a large area of fertile, farmed, and well-populated territory, much of it in mild and rich climates. Some parts of it contain the best gold and silver mines in Europe. And yet, because he lacks the encouraging influence of trade, that ruler has only a small revenue. Several times he has had to depend on money from other nations to protect his vital interests, and he cannot fight a long or continued war with his own resources alone.

:::vocab
Circulation (of money): The movement of money from person to person as people buy, sell, and pay debts.
Emperor of Germany: Hamilton's name for the Holy Roman Emperor, who ruled a loose empire in central Europe. The emperors came from the Habsburg family of Austria.
Subsidy: Money one government pays to another, often to help it fight a war.
:::

:::commentary
In 1787, the emperor was Joseph II of the Habsburg family, whose own lands included Austria, Bohemia, and Hungary. Austria had received money from Britain during several wars against France and Prussia. Hamilton's point is that land and gold alone don't make a government rich. What matters is an active economy, with money moving and goods being traded, that a government can tax.
:::

## Why direct taxes have failed {orig=4-6}

But this is not the only way the Union would help raise revenue. In other ways, its influence will be more direct and decisive.

The condition of the country, the habits of the people, and our own experience all make it clear: it is impossible to raise large sums through direct taxes. Tax laws have been passed one after another, and new ways to enforce collection have been tried, but with no success. The public's hopes have always been disappointed, and the state treasuries have stayed empty.

Popular governments naturally run things in a way that pleases the people. Combined with the real shortage of money caused by our weak and damaged trade, this have so far defeated every attempt to collect large amounts of taxes, and they have finally taught the state legislatures how foolish it is to try.

No one who knows what happens in other countries will be surprised by this. Britain is a very rich nation, and there direct taxes on great wealth should be much easier to bear, and, because the government is strong, much easier to collect than in America. Yet by far the largest part of Britain's national revenue comes from indirect taxes, from imposts and excises, and duties on imported goods are a large part of these.

In America, it is clear that for a long time we will have to depend mainly on such duties for revenue. In most parts of the country, excises must be kept within narrow limits. Our people will not put up with the prying and bossy nature of excise laws. Farmers, on the other hand, will hand over only small amounts, and unwillingly, if they are taxed on their houses and lands. And personal property is too uncertain and too hard to see to be taxed in any other way than through the hidden workings of taxes on consumption.

:::vocab
Direct tax: A tax charged directly on people or their property, such as a tax on land or a "head tax" on each person.
Indirect tax: A tax built into the price of goods, which people pay without noticing when they buy them.
Impost: A tax on imported goods; a duty.
Excise: A tax on goods made or sold within a country, such as liquor. Collecting it often required inspectors to enter homes and businesses.
Personal property: Property other than land and buildings, such as money, livestock, tools, and goods.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's dislike of direct taxes comes from hard experience. In the 1780s, states that tried to collect heavy taxes on land and polls (individual people) faced angry resistance, especially from farmers who had little cash. Massachusetts is the clearest example.

There is a famous irony here. In this essay Hamilton says Americans won't accept excise laws. But as Secretary of the Treasury, he proposed a federal excise tax on whiskey, which Congress passed in 1791. Farmers in western Pennsylvania, who turned their grain into whiskey, rebelled in 1794. President Washington led a militia force of about 13,000 men to put down the **Whiskey Rebellion**. Hamilton's prediction about how people would react turned out to be accurate, even if he didn't follow his own advice.
:::

## Smuggling between separate states {orig=7-9}

If these points are sound, then the situation that best lets us develop and expand such a valuable source of revenue must be the one best suited to our political welfare. And there can be no serious doubt that this situation depends on a general Union.

The Union would help trade, and so it would increase the revenue that comes from trade. It would also make the rules for collecting duties simpler and more effective. That would let the same rate of duties bring in more money, and it would let the government raise the rate without harming trade.

Think about the situation of these states:

- how they lie next to each other;
- the many rivers that cross them and the bays along their shores;
- how easy it is to travel in every direction;
- their shared language and customs;
- their familiar habits of trading with each other.

All of these would make illegal trade between them easy, and they would ensure that people often got around each other's trade rules. Out of mutual jealousy, the separate states or confederacies would have to keep their duties low to avoid tempting smugglers.

For a long time to come, the character of our governments would not allow the strict measures that European nations use to guard every entry into their countries, by land and by water. Even in Europe, those measures are not enough to stop the bold tricks of greedy smugglers.

In France, there is an army of "patrols," as they are called, constantly at work protecting the tax laws from smugglers. Mr. Necker estimates that there are more than twenty thousand of them. This shows how hard it is to stop smuggling where countries share land borders. It also shows clearly how hard it would be to collect duties in this country if disunion put the states in the same position with each other that France is in with its neighbors. The harsh and arbitrary powers that these patrols must have would be intolerable in a free country.

:::vocab
Contraband: Goods brought into a country illegally, usually to avoid taxes; smuggled goods.
Illicit: Illegal.
Necker: Jacques Necker, a Swiss-born banker who served as France's chief finance minister under King Louis XVI and wrote a well-known book on French finances.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's list of rivers and bays describes a smuggler's dream. Think of the long Delaware River between New Jersey and Pennsylvania, or Long Island Sound between New York and Connecticut. If each state had its own import taxes, a merchant could land goods in the state with the lowest tax and quietly carry them across the river or the bay. Each state would then be pressured to lower its own tax to compete.

The French "patrols" were part of a hated tax system. Customs posts and guards stood not only on France's borders but even between French provinces, and agents had wide power to search people and goods. Anger at this system was one of many grievances that fed the French Revolution, which began two years after this essay.
:::

## One coast to guard {orig=10}

If, instead, one government covers all the states, there will be only ONE SIDE to guard for most of our trade: the ATLANTIC COAST.

Ships arriving straight from foreign countries with valuable cargoes would rarely risk the complicated and serious dangers of trying to unload before reaching port. They would have to fear both the dangers of the coast and the danger of being caught, both before and after reaching their final destination. An ordinary amount of watchfulness would be enough to prevent any serious cheating of the revenue. A few armed ships, wisely stationed at the entrances of our ports, could serve at little cost as useful guards of the laws. And since the government would have the same interest in preventing violations everywhere, its efforts in each state would work together and be much more effective.

Here, too, Union would let us keep an advantage that nature offers us, which separation would throw away. The United States lies far from Europe, and a good distance from all the other places we would trade with widely. Ships could not cross to us in a few hours or a single night, as they can between the coasts of France and Britain, or of other neighboring nations. This is a huge protection against smuggling directly from foreign countries.

But smuggling into one state by way of another would be both easy and safe. Any thoughtful person can see the difference. In one case, goods must come directly from abroad, while in the other they can be brought in through a neighboring state in small loads, whenever there is a chance, with the added ease of travel over land and rivers.

:::quote
If, on the contrary, there be but one government pervading all the States, there will be, as to the principal part of our commerce, but ONE SIDE to guard--the ATLANTIC COAST.

Meaning: With one national government, customs officers would only have to watch the seacoast, not every border between the states.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton put this idea into practice. In 1790, as Secretary of the Treasury, he asked Congress to create a fleet of small armed ships to enforce the customs laws. This **Revenue Cutter Service** is one of the ancestors of today's U.S. Coast Guard, which considers Hamilton its founder.
:::

## What a national tax on imports could raise {orig=11}

So it is clear that one national government could raise duties on imports far higher, and at much lower cost, than the states could separately or in partial confederacies.

I believe it is safe to say that, so far, these duties have not averaged more than three percent in any state. In France they are estimated at about fifteen percent, and in Britain they are higher than that. In fact, if I remember correctly, they reach twenty percent there. Nothing seems to prevent us from raising them in this country to at least three times their present level.

Liquor alone, under national regulation, could produce a large revenue. Judging by how much is imported into this state, the whole amount imported into the United States can be estimated at four million gallons. A duty of one shilling per gallon would produce two hundred thousand pounds. Liquor could easily bear this rate of tax, and if it led people to drink less, that would be good for the country's farming, its economy, its morals, and its health. There may be nothing on which our nation spends so wastefully as on liquor.

:::vocab
Ardent spirits: Strong liquor, such as rum, brandy, and whiskey.
Shilling: A British coin worth one-twentieth of a pound. Americans in 1787 still often counted money in pounds and shillings.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction about import duties came true. One of the first major laws passed by the new Congress was the Tariff of 1789, a tax on imports. For more than a hundred years, until the federal income tax was allowed by the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913, tariffs were usually the federal government's largest source of money.

His argument for taxing liquor is an early example of what we now call a "sin tax," a tax that raises money and also discourages unhealthy behavior. Today's taxes on cigarettes, alcohol, and sugary drinks are defended in a similar way. Supporters say they improve public health. Critics say they fall hardest on poorer people and let the government use taxes to control personal choices.
:::

## Without it, farmers carry the load {orig=12}

What will happen if we can't make full use of this source of revenue? A nation cannot survive long without revenue. Without this essential support, it must give up its independence and sink to the low position of a province ruled by others. No government will choose to reach that extreme, so revenue must be raised no matter what. In this country, if most of it does not come from trade, it must fall with crushing weight on land.

I have already said that true excises are too much against the feelings of the people to be used very much. Also, in the states where farming is almost the only work, there aren't enough goods suitable for excise taxes to raise much money that way.

As I said before, personal property is hard to track. So it can't be made to contribute much, except through taxes on consumption. In large cities, officials might be able to guess at it well enough to oppress individuals, without much benefit to the state as a whole. But outside the cities, it must largely escape the eyes and hands of the tax collector.

Still, since the needs of the state must be met somehow, the lack of other resources must put most of the tax burden on the owners of land. And on the other hand, the government can never get enough money unless all sources of revenue are open to it. Under these difficulties, the nation's finances could never be put in a condition that would earn respect or provide security.

So we would not even have the comfort of a full treasury to make up for the oppression of farmers, that valuable class of citizens who work the land. Instead, public and private suffering would grow together in gloomy partnership, and both would mourn the foolish advice that led to disunion.

:::quote
A nation cannot long exist without revenues. Destitute of this essential support, it must resign its independence, and sink into the degraded condition of a province.

Meaning: A country that can't raise money can't defend itself, and it will end up controlled by stronger nations.
:::

:::vocab
Province: Here, a territory ruled by another country, like a colony. Hamilton means a nation that has lost its independence.
Landholder: A person who owns land, usually a farmer in 1787.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton aims this closing argument at farmers, who made up most of the voters, and many of whom distrusted the Constitution. His message is: *you think a strong national government will tax you, but without it, you will be taxed even more heavily, because land will be the only thing left to tax.*

This was a clever political appeal, but critics at the time worried that the national government would tax both imports *and* land. That is why the Constitution's taxing power, and limits on "direct taxes," became one of the most debated topics of ratification. Hamilton takes up the national taxing power in detail in Nos. 30–36.
:::

:::today
The debates in this essay are still with us. Americans still argue over which kinds of taxes are fair: taxes on income, on property, on sales, or on imports. Tariffs, once the main source of federal money, now bring in only a small share, but they are again a major political issue, debated as a way to protect American industries or to pressure other countries. Supporters say tariffs protect jobs and give the country bargaining power. Critics say they raise prices for consumers and invite other countries to strike back. Hamilton's basic insight still holds, though: a government needs a reliable way to collect money, and how it does so shapes the whole economy.
:::
