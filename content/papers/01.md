:::summary
Hamilton opens the series by telling New York voters that the government under the Articles of Confederation has clearly failed, and they must now decide whether to adopt a new Constitution. The stakes are huge. America will show the world whether people can create good government by careful thought and free choice, or whether governments will always be the product of accident and force. Hamilton warns that the debate will be full of anger and bad motives on both sides, so readers should judge the arguments, not the people making them. He openly admits that he supports the Constitution, and he lays out the plan for the essays to come.
:::

:::keypoints
- The old national government has **failed**, and Americans must decide on a new one.
- The decision matters to the whole world: can people create good government through **"reflection and choice"** instead of **"accident and force"**?
- Some people will oppose the Constitution for selfish reasons, but many opponents will be honest people who are simply mistaken. Good people can be found on both sides.
- Strong government and liberty are **not enemies**. In fact, history shows that would-be tyrants usually start by flattering the people.
- Hamilton openly says he supports the Constitution and promises to give his reasons so readers can judge them.
- He lists the six topics the series will cover.
:::

:::context Background
In September 1787, the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia finished the new Constitution and sent it to the states. Nine of the thirteen states had to ratify it before it could take effect. New York was a key state, and its powerful governor, George Clinton, opposed the plan. Critics had already started publishing essays against it in New York newspapers under names like "Cato" and "Brutus."

Hamilton answered with this essay on October 27, 1787. He signed it "Publius," after Publius Valerius Publicola, who helped found the Roman Republic after the overthrow of Rome's last king. The name hinted that the Constitution, too, was a way to secure a republic.
:::

## A decision that matters to the world {orig=1-2}

The current national government has clearly failed. Now you are being asked to consider a new Constitution for the United States of America. The importance of this subject speaks for itself. What is at stake is nothing less than the survival of the Union, the safety and well-being of the states that make it up, and the fate of a nation that is in many ways the most interesting in the world.

People have often said that the American people seem to have been chosen, by their actions and example, to settle a very important question. Can human societies really create good government through careful thought and free choice? Or are they forever doomed to get their governments by accident and force?

If there is any truth to this, then we have reached the moment when that question will be decided. If we make the wrong choice, it could be a misfortune not just for us, but for all of humanity.

That thought should make every good and thoughtful person care even more about the outcome. It adds love of humanity to love of country. It would be wonderful if our choice were guided by a wise judgment of our true interests, without confusion or bias from things that have nothing to do with the public good. But that is more something to hope for than something to expect.

The proposed plan affects too many special interests and changes too many local institutions. So the debate will inevitably drag in many issues that have nothing to do with the plan's merits. It will also stir up views, passions, and prejudices that make it hard to find the truth.

:::quote
...whether societies of men are really capable or not of establishing good government from reflection and choice, or whether they are forever destined to depend for their political constitutions on accident and force.

Meaning: Throughout history, governments were usually created by conquest, revolution, or chance. America had the chance to prove that a people could sit down, think it through, and choose their own government peacefully.
:::

:::commentary
This opening is one of the boldest claims in the Federalist Papers. Hamilton tells ordinary New Yorkers that their vote is part of a world-changing experiment. Before this, very few governments had been created by people openly debating and voting on a written plan. Most had come from kings, conquests, or slow historical accident. Hamilton wants readers to feel the weight of the moment.
:::

## Who will oppose the Constitution, and why {orig=3-4}

Among the biggest obstacles the new Constitution will face, two groups stand out. The first is a certain kind of person in every state who holds office under the state government. These people have an obvious interest in resisting any change that might reduce their power, pay, and importance. The second group is people with twisted ambitions. They either hope to gain power from the confusion of their country, or they think they'll have better chances to rise if the nation splits into several smaller confederacies than if it stays united under one government.

But I don't intend to focus on observations like these. I know it would be unfair to assume that everyone in these groups opposes the plan for selfish reasons, just because their position makes them look suspicious. To be honest, we must admit that even such people may have good intentions. There is no doubt that much of the opposition, now and in the future, will come from innocent sources, or even respectable ones. It will come from the honest mistakes of people misled by fears and suspicions they already held.

In fact, there are so many powerful forces that can bias people's judgment that we often see wise and good people on both the wrong and the right side of the most important questions. If we keep this in mind, it should teach us to be modest, even when we are completely convinced we are right.

There is another reason for caution. We can't always be sure that the people arguing for the truth have purer motives than their opponents. Ambition, greed, personal grudges, party loyalty, and other motives no better than these can drive people on the right side of a question just as easily as people on the wrong side.

Even without these reasons for moderation, nothing could be more foolish than the intolerant spirit that political parties have always shown. In politics, as in religion, it is ridiculous to try to win converts by fire and sword. False beliefs in either area can rarely be cured by persecution.

:::quote
For in politics, as in religion, it is equally absurd to aim at making proselytes by fire and sword. Heresies in either can rarely be cured by persecution.

Meaning: You can't force people to change their political views any more than you can force them to change their religion. "Proselytes" are converts, and "heresies" are beliefs that go against accepted teaching.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton takes a shot at his opponents and then quickly backs off. The first group he describes, state officeholders protecting their own power, was a not-so-subtle reference to Governor George Clinton and his allies in New York. But then Hamilton makes a fair and generous point: good people end up on both sides of big questions, and people on the "right" side can have selfish motives too. Whether Hamilton fully lived up to this advice is another matter. He was famous for fierce political fights, and he died in a duel with Aaron Burr in 1804.
:::

:::vocab
Confederacy: A loose union of independent states. Some opponents suggested splitting the thirteen states into two or three regional confederacies.
Emolument: Salary or profit from holding an office.
:::

## The debate will get ugly {orig=5}

Still, however reasonable these feelings are, there are already signs that this debate will go the way every great national debate has gone before. A flood of angry and hateful passions will be let loose. Judging by how both sides are acting, each will try to prove it is right and win more followers by shouting louder and insulting more bitterly.

Those who sincerely want an energetic and effective government will be accused of loving tyranny and hating liberty. Those who worry too much about dangers to the people's rights, which is usually a mistake of the head rather than the heart, will be accused of faking their concern just to win popularity at the cost of the public good.

On one side, people will forget that jealousy usually goes along with love, and that a noble passion for liberty can easily turn into narrow, petty distrust. On the other side, people will forget that a strong government is essential to protecting liberty. Anyone with sound judgment can see that the two can never be separated.

They will also forget that dangerous ambition more often hides behind a fake show of concern for the people's rights than behind an open call for firm and effective government. History teaches that the first has been a much more common road to tyranny than the second. Of the men who have destroyed the liberties of republics, most began their careers by flattering the people. They started as demagogues and ended as tyrants.

:::quote
...of those men who have overturned the liberties of republics, the greatest number have begun their career by paying an obsequious court to the people; commencing demagogues, and ending tyrants.

Meaning: Most dictators in history started out by flattering ordinary people and promising to fight for them. "Obsequious" means overly eager to please.
:::

:::vocab
Demagogue: A leader who wins support by appealing to people's emotions, fears, and prejudices rather than to reason.
Despotism: Rule by a single person with unlimited power; tyranny.
Energy (in government): Strength, effectiveness, and the ability to act. The Federalists used this word often.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's central argument here is that **strong government and liberty work together**. Opponents of the Constitution feared that a powerful national government would crush freedom. Hamilton flips this around. A weak government can't protect anyone's rights, and the greater danger comes from smooth-talking politicians who pretend to defend "the people."

This was a clever move, but it is also persuasive writing. Anti-Federalists could reply that history also has plenty of strong governments that became tyrannies. The whole series is, in a sense, Hamilton and Madison's attempt to prove that *this* strong government has enough safeguards built in.
:::

## Where Publius stands {orig=6}

My fellow citizens, in these remarks I have tried to warn you against every attempt, from any side, to sway your decision on this very important matter by anything other than the evidence of the truth. At the same time, you have probably guessed from what I've said that I am not unfriendly to the new Constitution.

Yes, my countrymen, I admit it. After thinking about it carefully, I am clearly convinced that it is in your interest to adopt it. I am sure this is the safest path for your liberty, your dignity, and your happiness. I won't pretend to have doubts I don't have. I won't entertain you with a show of weighing both sides when I've already decided. I openly admit my convictions, and I will freely set out the reasons behind them.

People who know their intentions are good have no need to be vague. But I won't keep repeating how sincere I am. My motives must stay locked in my own heart. My arguments, though, will be open to everyone, and everyone may judge them. At the very least, I will present them in a way that does not disgrace the cause of truth.

:::commentary
"My motives must remain in the depository of my own breast. My arguments will be open to all." This is a good rule for reading any persuasive writing. You can't see into an author's heart, but you can test their arguments. It's also a reason the authors wrote anonymously as "Publius": they wanted readers to judge the ideas, not the men.
:::

## The plan for the series {orig=7-10}

In a series of essays, I plan to discuss the following topics:

1. How the Union benefits your political well-being.
2. Why the current Confederation is not enough to preserve the Union.
3. Why we need a government at least as energetic as the one proposed to achieve that goal.
4. How the proposed Constitution fits the true principles of republican government.
5. How it resembles your own state constitution.
6. The extra protection that adopting it will give to republican government, to liberty, and to property.

As the discussion goes on, I will try to give a satisfying answer to every objection that has been raised and that seems to deserve your attention.

It may seem unnecessary to argue that the Union is useful. Most people in every state surely hold this belief deep in their hearts, and you might think it has no opponents. But in fact, we already hear people whispering, in the private circles of those who oppose the new Constitution, that the thirteen states are too large for any single system. They say we will have to split into separate confederacies made of different parts of the country.

This idea will probably be spread little by little, until enough people believe it that someone can say it openly. To anyone who looks at the big picture, nothing could be clearer: the choice is between adopting the new Constitution and breaking up the Union. So it will be useful to begin by looking at the advantages of the Union, and at the certain evils and likely dangers every state would face if it fell apart. That will be the subject of my next essay.

:::commentary
Hamilton's outline shows how the series is organized. Topics 1 and 2 cover roughly Nos. 2–36. Topics 3 and 4 run through the long middle of the series, Nos. 37–84, which explains each part of the new government in detail. The series never got to "its analogy to your own State constitution" as a separate topic. Hamilton touches on it throughout, and he explains in [No. 85](/papers/85/) why he skipped it.

Notice also the either-or choice: adopt the Constitution or watch the Union break apart. Opponents said this was a false choice, since the Articles could be amended instead. Persuasive writers often frame a choice this way, so it's worth asking whether the two options really were the only ones.
:::

:::connection
The next several essays follow this plan closely. Jay's [No. 2](/papers/2/) through [No. 5](/papers/5/) discuss dangers from foreign nations. Hamilton's Nos. 6–8 discuss dangers from conflicts between the states. Then [No. 9](/papers/9/) and Madison's famous [No. 10](/papers/10/) explain how the Union protects against faction and rebellion.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's warnings about political debate sound very modern. He predicts that each side will try to win "by the loudness of their declamations and the bitterness of their invectives," and that supporters of strong government will be called tyrants while defenders of rights will be called fakes. Today's arguments on cable news and social media often follow the same pattern. His advice still holds up: good people can be found on both sides, people on the right side can have bad motives, and the best test is to judge the arguments themselves.
:::
