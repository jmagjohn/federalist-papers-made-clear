:::summary
Hamilton admits that the national government may sometimes have to use force. Rebellions happen in every society, and states like Massachusetts and Pennsylvania have already had to raise troops to deal with them. Splitting into smaller confederacies wouldn't solve this problem. The real protection for liberty is that the whole government will be in the hands of the people's representatives. And if those representatives ever betray the people, the people still have their "original right of self-defense." That right, Hamilton argues, has a far better chance of success in a federal system. The states can detect dangers early, organize resistance, and unite against the national government, while the nation's huge size makes it nearly impossible for any army to conquer everyone at once.
:::

:::keypoints
- Rebellions are as unavoidable in political bodies as illnesses in human bodies. The idea of governing **only by the force of law** is a dream.
- Small disturbances can be put down by the **militia**. Larger ones may require **other kinds of force**, as the states themselves have found.
- Smaller, separate confederacies would face the **same need**, so the objection applies to them too.
- The best safeguard is that **all the government's power** is in the hands of the **people's representatives**.
- If representatives betray the people, the people keep the **"original right of self-defense,"** which stands above all forms of government.
- Resisting tyranny is **harder in a small state** and **easier in a large country**.
- In a federal system, "**power being almost always the rival of power**," the national and state governments will check each other, and the people can use one against the other.
- The **state legislatures** can spot danger early, organize opposition, and coordinate with each other.
- The country's **size and limited resources** make a national army able to impose tyranny very unlikely.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues Hamilton's discussion of military force from [No. 27](/papers/27/). There, he argued that a well-run national government would rarely need force to enforce its laws. Here he admits it sometimes would, and he answers the fear that such force could be turned against liberty.

The recent memory of **Shays' Rebellion** (1786–87) in Massachusetts hangs over this essay. So does the long struggle between New York and Vermont, which had declared itself independent of New York's control. Hamilton's passage on the people's "original right of self-defense" is often quoted today in debates over the Second Amendment and the right of resistance to tyranny.
:::

## Every government sometimes needs force {orig=1-2}

It can't be denied that there may be cases in which the national government is forced to use military force. Our own experience has confirmed what the examples of other nations teach. Emergencies of this kind will sometimes arise in every society, however it is set up. Uprisings and rebellions are, unfortunately, diseases as inseparable from the body politic as tumors and rashes are from the human body. We have been told that governing at all times by the simple force of law is the only acceptable principle of republican government. But that idea belongs only in the daydreams of those political experts who are too clever to learn from experience.

If such emergencies ever happen under the national government, there will be no remedy but force. The means used must match the size of the trouble. If it is a small disturbance in a small part of a state, the militia of the rest of the state would be enough to put it down. And we can assume they would be ready to do their duty.

An uprising, whatever its immediate cause, eventually endangers all government. Concern for the public peace, if not for the rights of the Union, would lead citizens untouched by the disorder to oppose the rebels. And if the national government proves in practice to promote the prosperity and happiness of the people, it would be unreasonable to think they would be unwilling to support it.

:::vocab
Sedition: Speech or action that stirs up rebellion against the government.
Insurrection: A violent uprising against the government; a rebellion.
Body politic: The people of a nation or state, thought of as one organized group.
Republican government: Government by elected representatives of the people, rather than by a king or a hereditary ruling class.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's comparison of rebellions to illnesses is a direct answer to some Anti-Federalists, who said that a truly free government should rule only by the "force of law," never by the sword. Hamilton calls this a fantasy. Every government, even a democratic one, will sometimes face violent resistance.

The Constitution gives Congress power "to provide for calling forth the Militia to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections and repel Invasions." Hamilton's first answer is to rely on the militia, ordinary armed citizens. Only when an uprising is too large does he turn to "a different kind of force," meaning regular troops.
:::

## The states face the same need {orig=3}

But if the uprising spreads through a whole state, or most of it, a different kind of force may become unavoidable. Massachusetts, it seems, found it necessary to raise troops to put down the disorders within its borders. Pennsylvania, merely out of fear of disturbances among some of its citizens, has decided to do the same.

Suppose the State of New York had wanted to restore its lost authority over the people of Vermont. Could it have hoped to succeed with the militia alone? Wouldn't it have been forced to raise and maintain a more regular force to carry out its plan?

So we must admit that the states themselves may need a force other than the militia in extraordinary cases like these. Then why should the possibility that the national government might face the same need in similar emergencies be made an objection to its existence? Isn't it surprising that men who claim to love the Union in general would use this as an objection to the Constitution? The objection applies with ten times the force to the plan *they* favor. And, as far as it has any truth, it is an unavoidable result of civil society on a large scale. Who wouldn't prefer that possibility to the endless unrest and frequent revolutions that constantly torment small republics?

:::commentary
**Vermont** had been claimed by New York, but its settlers, led by figures like Ethan Allen and his Green Mountain Boys, refused to accept New York's rule. In 1777 they declared Vermont an independent republic. New York never managed to force them back. In 1791, after New York gave up its claim, Vermont joined the Union as the fourteenth state. Hamilton's point is that New York's militia alone could never have reconquered Vermont. Any government facing a large-scale revolt would need trained troops.
:::

## Smaller confederacies would have the same problem {orig=4}

Let's look at this from another angle. Suppose that instead of one general system, two, three, or even four confederacies were formed. Wouldn't each of them face the same difficulty? Wouldn't each be exposed to the same accidents? And when these happened, wouldn't each have to use the same methods to uphold its authority that people object to in a government for all the states? Would the militia be any more willing or able to support the government in that case than under a general union?

All fair and intelligent people must admit, after thinking it over, that the objection applies equally to both cases. We might have one government for all the states, different governments for different groups of them, or even complete separation of the states. In every case, there might sometimes be a need to use a force organized differently from the militia. Such a force would be needed to keep the peace of the community and to uphold the just authority of the laws against violent attacks on them that amount to uprisings and rebellions.

## The real safeguard: the people's representatives {orig=5}

Apart from all other arguments on this subject, there is a complete answer to those who want a stricter rule against military forces in peacetime. The whole power of the proposed government will be in the hands of the representatives of the people. This is the essential, and in the end the only effective, security for the rights and privileges of the people that can be achieved in civil society.

:::note
Hamilton's footnote says: "Its full efficacy will be examined hereafter." He means that later essays will examine how well representation actually protects the people. Madison takes up that subject in Nos. 52–58, on the House of Representatives.
:::

:::quote
...the whole power of the proposed government is to be in the hands of the representatives of the people. This is the essential, and, after all, only efficacious security for the rights and privileges of the people, which is attainable in civil society.

Meaning: No written rule can fully protect liberty. The real protection is that the people elect the officials who hold power and can vote them out.
:::

## The people's right of self-defense {orig=6-7}

If the representatives of the people betray those who elected them, there is no remedy left except to use that original right of self-defense, which stands above all written forms of government. And against the national rulers' seizure of power, that right can be used with far better hope of success than against the rulers of a single state.

In a single state, suppose the people trusted with supreme power become usurpers. The state's different parts, sections, or districts have no separate governments of their own, so they can't take organized steps for defense. The citizens must rush to arms in confusion, without coordination, without a plan, without resources, except their courage and despair. The usurpers, wearing the appearance of legal authority, can too often crush the opposition before it grows.

The smaller the territory, the harder it will be for the people to form a regular, organized plan of resistance, and the easier it will be to defeat their first efforts. News of their preparations and movements can reach the usurpers more quickly. And the usurpers' army can be sent more quickly against the place where resistance has begun. In this situation, an unusual combination of circumstances would be needed for the people's resistance to succeed.

The obstacles to seizing power, and the means of resisting it, both grow as the size of the state grows, as long as the citizens understand their rights and are willing to defend them. In a large community, the natural strength of the people, compared with the artificial strength of the government, is greater than in a small one. So it is better able to fight the government's attempts to set up a tyranny.

But in a confederacy, the people can be said without exaggeration to be entirely the masters of their own fate. Power is almost always the rival of power. So the national government will always stand ready to check the states' attempts to seize power, and the states will feel the same way toward the national government. By throwing their weight onto either side of the scale, the people will surely make that side win. If their rights are invaded by either government, they can use the other as the tool for setting things right. How wise it will be for them to preserve, by cherishing the Union, an advantage that can never be valued too highly!

:::quote
If the representatives of the people betray their constituents, there is then no resource left but in the exertion of that original right of self-defense which is paramount to all positive forms of government...

Meaning: If elected leaders turn against the people, the people still have a natural right to defend themselves, a right that comes before and stands above any written constitution.
:::

:::quote
Power being almost always the rival of power, the general government will at all times stand ready to check the usurpations of the state governments, and these will have the same disposition towards the general government.

Meaning: The national and state governments will each want to stop the other from growing too powerful. That rivalry protects the people, who can turn to one government when the other abuses them.
:::

:::vocab
Usurper: Someone who seizes power they have no right to.
Paramount: Higher than everything else; supreme.
Positive forms of government: Governments set up by human laws and constitutions, as opposed to natural rights that exist before any government.
Constituents: The people a government or representative serves.
:::

:::commentary
The "original right of self-defense" reflects an idea found in the writings of John Locke and in the Declaration of Independence: when a government becomes destructive of the people's rights, "it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it." Hamilton treats this as a last resort, after elections and representation have failed.

This passage is often cited in modern debates over the **Second Amendment**, and people read it in different ways. Some emphasize the idea that the people's ability to resist tyranny, including by armed force, is a final check on government, and they see this as part of why the founders valued an armed citizenry. In *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008), the Supreme Court held that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to keep and bear arms, and it discussed founding-era fears that the national government might disarm the people and destroy the militia. Others emphasize that Hamilton describes resistance that is *organized through the state governments*, not by individuals acting alone. They also note that the same essay defends the national government's power to use force to put down rebellions.

History adds another layer. In 1861, Southern states used their state governments to organize resistance to the national government, much as Hamilton described, but in order to protect slavery. The Union treated secession as a rebellion and used force to defeat it. That experience shows that the right of resistance can be claimed for very different causes, and that judging when it is justified is one of the hardest questions in politics.
:::

## The states as guardians of liberty {orig=8}

It can safely be accepted as a basic rule of our political system that the state governments will, in every possible situation, give complete security against attacks on public liberty by the national government. Plans to seize power can't be hidden under excuses that select bodies of men would miss as easily as the people at large might.

The state legislatures will have better sources of information. They can spot the danger from far off. They have all the tools of civil government and the people's trust. So they can at once adopt a regular plan of opposition, combining all the community's resources. They can easily communicate with one another across the different states and unite their forces to protect their common liberty.

:::commentary
Here Hamilton, the strongest nationalist among the authors, makes the same argument Madison makes in [No. 46](/papers/46/). The states are not just rivals of the national government. They are organized watchdogs, with legislatures, governors, and militias, ready to resist national overreach. Hamilton called the states the potential "ARM" of the people's discontent in [No. 26](/papers/26/).

Critics might note a tension in Hamilton's argument. In [No. 25](/papers/25/), he warned that state military forces could be used to destroy the national government's authority. Here, he praises the states' ability to organize resistance. His answer would likely be that the states should resist only *usurpation*, the abuse of power, not lawful national authority. But who decides which is which was never fully settled until the Civil War.
:::

## Size and limited resources protect liberty {orig=9-10}

The great size of the country is a further protection. We have already seen how useful it was against the attacks of a foreign power. It would have exactly the same effect against the schemes of ambitious leaders in the national government. If the national army managed to crush the resistance of one state, the distant states could rise up with fresh forces. Gains made in one place would have to be abandoned to defeat the opposition in others. And the moment the conquered area was left alone, its efforts would start again and its resistance would revive.

We should also remember that the size of the army must, in any case, be limited by the country's resources. For a long time to come, it won't be possible to maintain a large army. And as the means of supporting one increase, the population and natural strength of the community will increase in proportion.

When will the time come that the national government can raise and maintain an army able to set up a despotism over the great body of the people of an immense empire? These people, through their state governments, will be able to take measures for their own defense with all the speed, order, and organization of independent nations. This fear may be considered a disease that no argument or reasoning can cure.

:::vocab
Despotism: Rule by one person with unlimited power.
Celerity: Speed.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's point about the country's size draws on the Revolution. The British captured major cities like New York, Philadelphia, and Charleston, but they could never control the whole country. Whenever they moved their army to one region, resistance revived in another.

His prediction that the army would stay small for "a long time to come" held true for over a century. Except in wartime, the U.S. Army remained small until the 20th century. After World War II, and especially during the Cold War, the United States built and kept a very large permanent military. Some see this as Hamilton's worst fear for liberty coming true, while others argue that civilian control and elections have kept the military firmly under the people's authority, as Hamilton hoped.
:::

:::today
Federalist No. 28 raises questions that are still debated. When, if ever, is it justified for people to resist their own government? How should the national and state governments check each other? Today, states regularly push back against national policies through lawsuits, refusals to cooperate, and their own laws, the kind of organized, lawful resistance Hamilton described. At the same time, debates over the Second Amendment often turn on whether an armed citizenry is still a meaningful check on government power, given the size of the modern military. People on different sides of those debates continue to cite this essay.
:::
