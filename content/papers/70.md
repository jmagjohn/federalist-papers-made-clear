:::summary
Some people think a strong executive is a threat to republican government. Hamilton argues the opposite: "Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government." A weak executive means weak enforcement of the laws, which means bad government. The most important source of energy is **unity**, putting executive power in one person instead of a group or council. One person can act quickly, decisively, and in secret when needed. Just as important, one person can be **held responsible**. When power is shared, officials blame each other, and the public can't tell who is at fault.
:::

:::keypoints
- **Energy** in the executive is essential for national defense, steady enforcement of the laws, protection of property, and protection of liberty.
- "A feeble Executive implies a feeble execution of the government," and that means **bad government**.
- Energy comes from four things: **unity**, **duration** (a long enough term), **adequate support** (a secure salary), and **competent powers**.
- Safety in a republic comes from **dependence on the people** and **responsibility**.
- A single executive is better at **decision, activity, secrecy, and speed** than a group.
- Disagreement is useful in a legislature, where it promotes careful debate, but harmful in the executive, where it only causes delay.
- Most importantly, a plural executive or an executive council **hides who is responsible** for mistakes.
:::

:::context Background
At the Constitutional Convention, delegates argued about whether the executive should be one person or several. Edmund Randolph of Virginia called a single executive "the foetus of monarchy" and proposed a three-person executive instead. Many state constitutions in 1787 gave their governors little power and required them to share decisions with an executive council. Americans had just fought a war against a king, so a powerful single leader made many of them nervous. Hamilton, who had served as George Washington's aide during the Revolution and had seen the Continental Congress struggle to run the war, believed the country badly needed a strong, unified executive.
:::

## Strong government needs an energetic executive {orig=1-2}

Some people believe that a vigorous executive goes against the basic spirit of republican government. Anyone who truly supports republican government must hope this idea is false. If it were true, they would have to admit that their own principles are doomed.

Energy in the executive is a key part of what makes government good. It is essential to protect the community against foreign attack. It is just as essential for enforcing the laws steadily, for protecting property against the lawless and bullying mobs that sometimes interrupt the normal course of justice, and for protecting liberty against the schemes and attacks of ambition, faction, and anarchy.

Anyone who knows even a little Roman history knows how often that republic had to turn to the absolute power of a single man, called a Dictator. It did this to fight ambitious men trying to become tyrants, to stop rebellions by whole classes of society that threatened all government, and to defend against foreign enemies who threatened to conquer and destroy Rome.

But there's no need for more arguments or examples on this point. A feeble executive means feeble enforcement of the government's decisions. Feeble enforcement is just another way of saying bad enforcement. And a government that is badly carried out, no matter how good it looks on paper, is in practice a bad government.

:::quote
Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government.

Meaning: A government can't be good unless its executive is strong and effective enough to actually carry out the laws and protect the country.
:::

:::quote
A feeble Executive implies a feeble execution of the government. A feeble execution is but another phrase for a bad execution; and a government ill executed, whatever it may be in theory, must be, in practice, a bad government.

Meaning: It doesn't matter how good the plan is if no one can carry it out.
:::

:::vocab
Executive: The branch of government that carries out ("executes") the laws. In the national government, this is the president.
Energy: In the Federalist Papers, this means strength, effectiveness, and the ability to act firmly and quickly.
Dictator: In the Roman Republic, a temporary leader given total power during an emergency, usually for six months at most.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's mention of Roman dictators might seem like a strange way to calm fears about a powerful president. His point is that even a republic sometimes needs firm, concentrated leadership in a crisis, and Rome had to suspend its normal government to get it. A well-designed executive, he suggests, would give a republic the energy it needs *without* resorting to a dictator.

The "irregular and high-handed combinations which sometimes interrupt the ordinary course of justice" are a reference to Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts in 1786–87, when armed farmers shut down courts to stop foreclosures on their farms. The weak national government could do nothing about it.
:::

## The ingredients of energy and safety {orig=3-6}

So, assuming every sensible person agrees that we need an energetic executive, the remaining questions are these. What ingredients create this energy? How well can they be combined with the ingredients that make the executive safe for a republic? And how well does the Convention's plan combine them?

The ingredients that create energy in the executive are: first, unity; second, duration; third, adequate provision for its support; and fourth, competent powers.

The ingredients that create safety in a republic are: first, a proper dependence on the people; and second, proper responsibility.

The politicians and statesmen most respected for their sound principles and fair views have supported a single executive and a large legislature. They rightly considered energy the most necessary quality of the executive, and believed it works best when power is in one person's hands. They also rightly considered a large legislature best suited for careful debate and wisdom, and best able to win the people's trust and protect their rights and interests.

:::commentary
This paragraph is a roadmap for Hamilton's next several essays. This essay covers **unity**. [No. 71](/papers/71/) and [No. 72](/papers/72/) cover **duration** (the four-year term and re-election). [No. 73](/papers/73/) covers **support** (the president's salary) and the veto. Nos. 73–77 cover the president's **powers**. Throughout, he also tries to show how the plan keeps the president **dependent on the people** and **responsible** to them.
:::

:::vocab
Duration: How long someone holds office; the length of the term.
Competent powers: Powers that are adequate, or sufficient, for the job.
Responsibility: Being answerable for one's actions, so that one can be blamed, voted out, or punished.
:::

## Why one person acts better than many {orig=7-8}

No one will deny that unity helps create energy. Decision, activity, secrecy, and speed will usually mark the actions of one person far more than the actions of a larger group. The more people involved, the weaker these qualities become.

Unity can be destroyed in two ways. One is by giving the power to two or more officials of equal rank and authority. The other is by giving it, on paper, to one person, but requiring that person to get the approval and cooperation of others who serve as advisers. The two Consuls of ancient Rome are an example of the first. Several of our state constitutions provide examples of the second. If I remember correctly, New York and New Jersey are the only states that have given executive power entirely to one person. Both ways of dividing the executive have supporters, but the fans of an executive council are more numerous. Both methods face similar, if not equal, objections, so for the most part they can be examined together.

:::vocab
Plurality (in the executive): Having more than one person share executive power.
Consuls: The two top officials of the Roman Republic, elected each year with equal power. Each could block the other.
Executive council: A group of advisers whose approval the executive must get before acting.
:::

## What history shows {orig=9-10}

The experience of other nations doesn't teach us much on this subject. But as far as it teaches anything, it teaches us not to fall in love with a divided executive. We have seen that the Achaeans, after trying two leaders, decided to get rid of one. Roman history records many cases where the republic was harmed by disputes between the two Consuls, and between the military Tribunes who sometimes replaced them. But it gives no examples of any special benefit the state got from having several leaders.

It is surprising that the disputes between the Consuls weren't more frequent or more deadly, until we consider Rome's unusual situation. The patricians, Rome's noble class, were in a constant struggle with the plebeians, the common people, to hold onto their old powers and privileges. The Consuls were usually chosen from the patricians, so their shared interest in defending their class's privileges usually kept them united. Also, after Rome's empire grew, it became a custom for the Consuls to divide the work by drawing lots. One stayed in Rome to govern the city and the surrounding area, while the other commanded the army in the distant provinces. This arrangement surely did a lot to prevent the clashes and rivalries that might otherwise have disrupted the peace of the republic.

But if we leave the dim light of historical research and rely purely on reason and good sense, we will find much stronger reasons to reject a divided executive, in any form, than to accept it.

:::commentary
Hamilton's point about Rome is clever. Rome's two Consuls didn't fight constantly only because they had a common enemy (the common people) and because they usually worked in different places. In other words, the two-person executive worked only when it stopped acting like a two-person executive.
:::

## Disagreement helps a legislature but hurts an executive {orig=11-14}

Whenever two or more people work together on a common project, there is always a risk they will disagree. If it's a public office where they have equal rank and authority, there's a special risk of personal rivalry and even hostility. These causes can lead to the bitterest disputes. When this happens, it lowers the public's respect for those leaders, weakens their authority, and disrupts their plans and actions. If it happened to the top executive of a country, made up of several people, it could block the government's most important measures in its most dangerous emergencies. Even worse, it could split the community into violent, bitter factions, each loyal to a different member of the executive.

People often oppose something just because they had no part in planning it, or because it was planned by people they dislike. But if they were consulted and happened to disagree, they come to see opposing it as a duty to their own pride. They seem to think their honor, and their belief that they couldn't possibly be wrong, require them to make sure the decision fails. Good and kind people have seen, with horror, how far this attitude is sometimes taken. They have seen how often society's great interests are sacrificed to the vanity, conceit, and stubbornness of individuals with enough influence to make their passions and whims matter to everyone. Perhaps the question now before the public, the Constitution itself, will offer sad proof of this contemptible weakness, or rather this hateful vice, in human character.

In a free government, we must put up with these problems when we design the legislature. But it is unnecessary, and therefore unwise, to build them into the executive. That's also where they would do the most harm. In the legislature, quick decisions are more often bad than good. Differences of opinion and clashes between parties may sometimes block helpful plans, but they often encourage careful debate and caution, and they help check excesses by the majority. Also, once the legislature makes a decision, the opposition must end. That decision is a law, and resisting it can be punished.

But nothing good comes from disagreement in the executive branch to make up for the harm. There, the harm is pure and unmixed. It never stops. Disagreement weakens and gets in the way of a plan from the first step to the last. It constantly works against the most necessary qualities in an executive, vigor and speed, without any good to balance it out. In wartime, when the executive's energy is the nation's main defense, we would have everything to fear from a divided executive.

These points apply most strongly to the first kind of divided executive, several officials with equal rank and authority. Not many people support that idea. But they also apply, though less strongly, to the idea of a council whose approval the Constitution would require for the executive's actions. A scheming group within that council could disrupt and weaken the whole administration. Even without such a group, the simple variety of views and opinions would be enough to make the executive habitually weak and slow.

:::commentary
Hamilton makes a smart distinction here. Disagreement and delay are *good* in a legislature, because laws should be carefully debated and checked. That's why Congress has two houses and hundreds of members. But once a law is passed, someone has to carry it out, and that job needs speed and decisiveness.

His comment about people who oppose a plan simply because they weren't consulted, or because their pride is hurt, is a sharp jab at some opponents of the Constitution. He hints that some of them, like New York's Governor Clinton, opposed it partly out of wounded pride.
:::

:::vocab
Cabal: A small, secret group plotting together, usually to gain power.
Dilatoriness: Slowness; a habit of delaying.
:::

## The biggest problem: no one is responsible {orig=16-20}

But one of the weightiest objections to a divided executive, which applies to both forms of it, is that it tends to hide mistakes and destroy responsibility.

Responsibility comes in two kinds: being subject to blame and being subject to punishment. The first is more important, especially in an elected office. People in public office will much more often act in ways that make them unworthy of being trusted again than in ways that make them guilty of a crime. But having several people in the executive makes it harder to uncover either kind of wrongdoing.

Amid all the finger-pointing, it often becomes impossible to tell who really deserves the blame or the punishment for a harmful decision or series of decisions. The blame is shifted from one person to another so skillfully, and with such believable excuses, that the public can't figure out who was really responsible. The events that lead to a national failure or disaster are sometimes so complicated that, when many people played different roles, we can clearly see there was mismanagement but still can't say who is truly at fault.

"I was overruled by my council." "The council was so divided that it was impossible to get a better decision." Excuses like these are always ready, whether true or false. And who will take the trouble, or risk the hostility, of a careful investigation into what really happened behind the scenes? Even if some citizen were eager enough to take on this hopeless task, if the people involved were working together, it would be easy to wrap the facts in so much confusion that no one could tell exactly what anyone did.

In the one case where the governor of New York shares power with a council, in appointing officials, we have seen exactly these problems. Scandalous appointments to important offices have been made. Some were so outrageous that ALL PARTIES agreed they were wrong. When people asked what happened, the governor blamed the council members, and they blamed the governor for nominating the person. Meanwhile, the people were left completely unable to figure out whose influence had put their interests in the hands of such unqualified and obviously unsuitable people. Out of kindness to the individuals involved, I won't go into details.

These points make it clear that a divided executive tends to take away the two greatest protections the people have that their officials will use power faithfully. The first is the pressure of public opinion. That pressure loses its force when blame for bad decisions is split among many people, and when no one is sure who deserves it. The second is the people's ability to easily and clearly discover the misconduct of the officials they trust, so that they can remove them from office or, when appropriate, punish them.

:::quote
"I was overruled by my council. The council were so divided in their opinions that it was impossible to obtain any better resolution on the point."

Meaning: Hamilton imagines the excuses a leader would make if power were shared. When everyone is in charge, no one is to blame.
:::

:::note A note on the original text
Our source edition prints this passage twice, in two slightly different versions (original ¶15, and ¶16–17), because different early editions worded it differently. Our plain version follows ¶16–17.
:::

:::commentary
This is probably the strongest argument in the essay, and it's about **accountability**, not just efficiency. Hamilton argues that a single president is actually *safer* for liberty because the people always know whom to blame. If something goes wrong, there's one name on the door.

President Harry Truman later captured this idea with a sign on his desk that read "The buck stops here." It meant he couldn't pass responsibility to anyone else. Hamilton's point also helps explain why the Constitution doesn't require the president to follow the advice of the cabinet. Cabinet members advise, but the president decides and takes the blame.

Notice that Hamilton thinks blame matters more than punishment: "Man, in public trust, will much oftener act in such a manner as to render him unworthy of being any longer trusted, than in such a manner as to make him obnoxious to legal punishment." Most bad leadership isn't a crime. The main remedy is the next election, which only works if voters know who did what.
:::

:::vocab
Censure: Public blame or criticism.
Odium: Hatred or disgust directed at someone, often because of something they did.
:::

## Why Britain's council doesn't fit America {orig=21-22}

In England, the king holds office for life. For the sake of public peace, there is a rule that he cannot be held responsible for how he governs, and that his person is sacred. So it is very wise in that kingdom to give the king an official council whose members can be held responsible to the nation for the advice they give. Without this, there would be no responsibility at all in the executive branch, which is unacceptable in a free government. But even there, the king doesn't have to follow his council's decisions, although its members are answerable for their advice. He is the absolute master of his own conduct in office and can follow or ignore their advice as he alone chooses.

But in a republic, every official ought to be personally responsible for his conduct in office. So the reason for having a council in the British system not only disappears, but actually turns against the idea. In Britain's monarchy, the council is a substitute for the responsibility the king can't have. It serves, to some extent, as a hostage that guarantees his good behavior to the nation. In the American republic, a council would destroy, or greatly weaken, the intended and necessary responsibility of the president himself.

:::commentary
In Britain, the rule was "the king can do no wrong." So when the government did something bad, Parliament blamed and punished the king's ministers instead. This is still basically how Britain works, where the prime minister and cabinet are responsible to Parliament. Hamilton says America doesn't need this workaround, because the president *can* be blamed directly, voted out, and even impeached.
:::

## One person is easier to watch {orig=23-25}

The idea of an executive council, which is common in the state constitutions, comes from a rule of republican suspicion: that power is safer in the hands of many people than in the hands of one. Even if this rule applied here, I would argue that its advantages wouldn't outweigh the many disadvantages on the other side. But I don't think the rule applies to executive power at all.

On this point, I fully agree with a writer whom the famous Junius called "deep, solid, and ingenious," who said that "the executive power is more easily confined when it is ONE." It is far safer to have a single target for the people's suspicion and watchfulness. In short, adding more people to the executive is more dangerous to liberty than friendly to it.

A little thought will show that the kind of security people hope to get from a multi-person executive can't be achieved. Either there must be so many people that it's hard for them to unite, or they will be a source of danger rather than security. The combined reputation and influence of several individuals is more dangerous to liberty than the reputation and influence of any one of them alone.

So when power is placed in the hands of a small group whose interests and goals can easily be combined in a common plan by a clever leader, it is more likely to be abused, and more dangerous when abused, than if it were placed in one person. A single person, precisely because he is alone, will be watched more closely and suspected more readily. And he can't build up as much influence as he could by joining with others.

The Decemvirs of Rome, whose name refers to their number, ten, were more to be feared when they seized power than any ONE of them would have been. No one would propose an executive much larger than that. Six to twelve members have been suggested for the council. Even the largest of these numbers is not too large for its members to unite easily. And America would have more to fear from such a combination than from the ambition of any single person. Advisers to an official who is himself responsible for his actions are generally just an obstacle to his good intentions. They are often the tools and partners of his bad ones. And they are almost always a cover for his faults.

I won't dwell on the cost. But clearly, a council large enough to serve its main purpose would require paying salaries to members who would have to leave their homes and live at the seat of government. That would be too big an expense for something of doubtful value.

I will only add this. Before the Constitution appeared, I rarely met an intelligent person from any state who didn't admit, based on experience, that the UNITY of New York's executive was one of the best features of our state constitution.

:::quote
...the executive power is more easily confined when it is ONE...

Meaning: It's easier for the public to keep an eye on one leader than on a group of leaders who can protect and cover for each other.
:::

:::vocab
Decemvirs: A board of ten men given total power in ancient Rome around 451 BC to write a code of laws. Their second board refused to give up power and ruled as tyrants until they were overthrown.
Junius: The pen name of an anonymous writer whose fierce political letters were famous in 1760s–70s England. The writer he praised was Jean-Louis de Lolme, a Swiss author who wrote about the British constitution.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton ends by turning his opponents' fear on its head. Anti-Federalists thought a single executive was dangerous because one person might become a king. Hamilton says a small group is *more* dangerous, because its members can combine their influence and cover for each other, while a lone president is the center of everyone's attention.

Was he right? Defenders of the presidency point out that the U.S. has never had a president seize permanent power, and that accountability has worked: presidents have been voted out, investigated, and impeached. Critics argue that the modern presidency has grown far more powerful than Hamilton imagined, especially in war and foreign affairs, and that "energy in the executive" has become an excuse for presidents to act without Congress. The debate over how much power one person should hold is still going on.
:::

:::today
Federalist No. 70 is often quoted in modern debates about presidential power. Supporters of a strong presidency cite it to argue that the president should control the whole executive branch, including the power to fire officials. This idea is sometimes called the "unitary executive" theory. Critics reply that Hamilton was arguing for a *single* executive, not an *unlimited* one, and that he also stressed responsibility and dependence on the people. Whenever a president acts quickly in a crisis, or whenever officials in an administration blame each other for a failure, Hamilton's arguments about energy and accountability are being tested.
:::
