:::summary
In No. 48, Madison showed that written limits alone can't keep the branches of government in their places. So what can? Thomas Jefferson had proposed an answer in his draft constitution for Virginia: when two branches agreed that the constitution had been broken, a convention of the people would be called to fix it. Madison respects the idea, since all power comes from the people. But he finds serious problems with relying on it. Frequent appeals would weaken the respect that gives a government stability, and they would stir up dangerous public passions. Worst of all, the legislature would usually win, because its members are more numerous, better known, and more popular. Such appeals would be decided by the **passions** of the public, when it is the public's **reason** that should control the government.
:::

:::keypoints
- Jefferson proposed that when **two of the three branches** agreed, by two-thirds votes, a **convention of the people** would be called to correct violations of the constitution.
- Since the people are the source of all power, appealing to them makes sense in theory, and a path to them should stay open for **great and extraordinary occasions**.
- But the plan doesn't help if **two branches team up** against the third.
- Frequent appeals would suggest the government is defective and would rob it of the **veneration** (deep respect) that time gives.
- Such appeals would stir up **public passions** and disturb the peace. The early state constitutions were written in unusual times of unity that won't come again.
- The **legislature** would usually win, because its members are numerous, live among the people, and are well connected. They would probably even sit in the convention and judge their own case.
- "The PASSIONS, therefore, not the REASON, of the public would sit in judgment."
:::

:::context Background
This is the third of Madison's five essays on separation of powers (Nos. 47–51). In [No. 48](/papers/48/), he quoted Jefferson's *Notes on the State of Virginia* to show how Virginia's legislature had taken over power. At the end of that book, Jefferson included a draft constitution he had written for Virginia in 1783, when he expected the state to hold a convention to write a new one. (The convention never met.) Here Madison examines one of the safeguards Jefferson proposed. Madison never names Jefferson, but readers would have known exactly who "the author of the *Notes*" was.

Madison and Jefferson were close friends and political allies. This essay is a polite but firm disagreement between them.
:::

## Jefferson's proposal {orig=1-2}

The author of the *Notes on the State of Virginia*, quoted in the last paper, added to that valuable work a draft of a constitution. He prepared it to be presented to a convention that the legislature was expected to call in 1783 to write a constitution for Virginia.

Like everything from the same pen, the plan shows a way of thinking that is original, broad, and accurate. It deserves attention all the more because it shows both a deep devotion to republican government and a clear understanding of the dangerous tendencies that government must be guarded against. One of the safeguards he proposes is perhaps entirely his own idea. He seems to rely on it in the end as the main protection for the weaker branches against attacks by the stronger. Since it relates directly to our present subject, it shouldn't be overlooked.

His proposal is this:

> "that whenever any two of the three branches of government shall concur in opinion, each by the voices of two thirds of their whole number, that a convention is necessary for altering the constitution, or CORRECTING BREACHES OF IT, a convention shall be called for the purpose."

In other words, if two of the three branches each voted by a two-thirds majority that a convention was needed, a convention of the people would be called to change the constitution or to correct violations of it.

:::vocab
Palladium: A safeguard or protection. (Madison calls Jefferson's plan a "palladium" for the weaker branches.)
Convention: A special assembly elected by the people to write, change, or interpret a constitution, separate from the regular legislature.
:::

## The case for asking the people {orig=3-4}

The people are the only rightful source of power. The constitution, which gives each branch its power, comes from them. So it seems to fit republican theory perfectly to go back to that same original authority. We should do this not only when we need to expand, reduce, or reshape the powers of the government. We should also do it whenever one branch invades the powers the constitution gave to the others.

Under the constitution, the branches are completely equal in rank. So clearly none of them can claim a sole or superior right to settle the boundaries between their powers. How, then, can we stop the stronger branches from invading the weaker, or correct the wrongs done to the weaker, without an appeal to the people themselves? The people granted the powers in the first place. Only they can declare what the grant really means and make sure it is obeyed.

There is certainly great force in this reasoning. It must be admitted that it proves one thing: a constitutional road to a decision by the people should be marked out and kept open for certain great and extraordinary occasions. But there seem to be overwhelming objections to going back to the people as the regular way, in all cases, to keep the branches within their constitutional limits.

:::commentary
Madison accepts Jefferson's principle but rejects his practical plan. He agrees the people are the final authority and that there should be a way to reach them on rare, major occasions. The U.S. Constitution provides one: Article V, which explains how the Constitution can be amended. Article V even allows a national convention to propose amendments if two-thirds of the state legislatures ask for one. That convention method has never been used. All 27 amendments have been proposed by Congress.

What Madison rejects is using the people as the regular referee in everyday fights between branches.
:::

:::vocab
Coordinate: Equal in rank or authority. Madison says the branches are "perfectly co-ordinate."
:::

## First problem: two branches against one {orig=5}

First, the plan doesn't cover the case of two branches teaming up against the third. The legislature has many ways to influence the motives of the other branches. Suppose it wins over either of the others, or even just one-third of that branch's members. Then the remaining branch would get no help from this remedy, because it could never gather the votes needed to call a convention.

I won't dwell on this objection, however. It might be considered an objection to the details of the plan rather than to the principle itself.

## Second problem: losing respect for government {orig=6}

Next, there is an objection built into the principle itself. Every appeal to the people would suggest that something is wrong with the government. So frequent appeals would largely rob the government of the veneration that time gives to everything. Without that respect, perhaps even the wisest and freest governments would not have the stability they need.

It is true that all governments rest on opinion. But it is just as true that how strongly each person holds an opinion, and how much it affects his actions, depends a great deal on how many other people he thinks share it. Human reason, like a human being, is timid and cautious when left alone. It becomes firm and confident as more people join it. And when the examples that support an opinion are *old* as well as *numerous*, they have a double effect.

In a nation of philosophers, this point could be ignored. The voice of enlightened reason would be enough to teach respect for the laws. But a nation of philosophers is as unlikely as the race of philosopher-kings that the ancient Greek thinker Plato wished for. And in every other nation, even the most reasonable government will find it useful to have the settled habits and feelings of the community on its side.

:::quote
...a nation of philosophers is as little to be expected as the philosophical race of kings wished for by Plato.

Meaning: In his book *The Republic*, Plato imagined an ideal state ruled by wise "philosopher-kings." Madison says a whole nation of perfectly reasonable citizens is just as much a fantasy. Real governments must work with ordinary people.
:::

:::vocab
Veneration: Deep respect or reverence.
Prejudices: In 1787, this often meant settled opinions and habits held without much thought, not necessarily bigotry. Madison means people's long-held feelings of loyalty to their government.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Madison's most interesting ideas about human nature. He argues that a constitution needs more than logic to survive. It needs people's *habit* of respect, and that habit grows stronger the longer a constitution lasts. If people are constantly asked whether to rewrite it, they will start to see it as temporary and flawed. The idea that "all governments rest on opinion" comes from the Scottish philosopher David Hume, whom Madison had read closely.

This was a real disagreement with Jefferson. In 1789, Jefferson wrote to Madison that the earth belongs to the living generation, and suggested that every constitution and law should expire after about nineteen years, so each generation could make its own. Madison wrote back disagreeing, using reasoning much like this essay's. Today, Americans still debate the two views. Some praise the Constitution's long life and the stability it brings. Others argue it is too hard to change and gives too much weight to the past.
:::

## Third problem: stirring up passions {orig=7}

An even more serious objection to frequently sending constitutional questions to the whole society is the danger of disturbing the public peace by stirring up public passions too strongly.

Our revisions of our forms of government have been successful, and they do great honor to the virtue and intelligence of the American people. Still, we must admit that these experiments are too delicate to repeat more often than necessary. We should remember the special conditions under which all our existing state constitutions were written:

- They were written in the middle of a danger, the war, which held down the passions most hostile to order and harmony.
- The people had an enthusiastic confidence in their patriotic leaders, which quieted the usual differences of opinion on great national questions.
- There was a universal eagerness for new forms of government, the opposite of the old ones, which grew out of universal anger against the old British government.
- No party spirit, tied to the changes being made or the abuses being reformed, could mix itself into the process.

The situations we can usually expect to be in from now on offer no such protection against the danger I fear.

:::commentary
Madison is pointing out that the state constitutions written during the Revolution (mostly in 1776–1780) were produced under unusual conditions. Americans faced a common enemy, trusted their leaders, and agreed that they wanted to get rid of British-style government. In normal times, he warns, a constitutional convention would turn into a battle between parties.

This worry helps explain why the Constitutional Convention of 1787 met in secret, and why many Federalists feared calling a second convention to revise the Constitution, as some Anti-Federalists proposed.
:::

## The biggest problem: the legislature would win {orig=8-9}

But the greatest objection of all is that the decisions likely to come from such appeals would not keep the government in its constitutional balance.

We have seen that in republican governments, the legislature tends to grow at the expense of the other branches. So appeals to the people would usually be made by the executive and the judiciary. But whichever side made the appeal, would each side have an equal chance in the contest? Let's look at their different situations.

The members of the executive and judicial branches are few, and only a small part of the people can know them personally. Judges, because of how they are appointed and how long they serve, are too far removed from the people to share much in their feelings and opinions. Executive officials are usually viewed with suspicion, and their actions can always be twisted and made unpopular.

The members of the legislature, on the other hand, are numerous. They are spread out and live among the people. Their family ties, friendships, and acquaintances reach a large share of the most influential people in society. The nature of their job gives them personal influence among the people, and makes them seem the most direct and trusted guardians of the people's rights and liberties. With these advantages, the other side could hardly expect an equal chance of winning.

But the legislature wouldn't just be able to plead its case most successfully to the people. Its members would probably become the judges themselves. The same influence that got them elected to the legislature would get them elected to the convention. If this weren't true of all of them, it would probably be true of many. It would almost certainly be true of the leading figures, on whom everything depends in such bodies. In short, the convention would be made up mainly of men who had been, who were now, or who expected to be members of the very branch whose conduct was on trial. So they would be parties to the very question they were supposed to decide.

:::commentary
This argument connects to a principle Madison stated in [No. 10](/papers/10/): "No man is allowed to be a judge in his own cause." A convention filled with current and former lawmakers could hardly judge fairly whether the legislature had overstepped.

Madison is also making a realistic point about popularity. Legislators live in every county and know local leaders. Judges and executive officials are few and distant. In any popular contest, the legislature starts with a big advantage. That is why Madison thinks the weaker branches need their own built-in defenses, which he describes in [No. 51](/papers/51/).
:::

## Passion, not reason, would judge {orig=10}

It might sometimes happen that appeals would be made under conditions less unfavorable to the executive and judicial branches. The legislature's power grabs might be so obvious and sudden that they couldn't be disguised. A strong group of legislators might side with the other branches. Or the executive power might be in the hands of a special favorite of the people. In such a situation, the public's decision might be less influenced by its preference for the legislature.

But even then, the decision could never be expected to turn on the true merits of the question. It would inevitably get tangled up with the spirit of existing parties, or of new parties that grew out of the question itself. It would be tied to famous and influential people in the community. And it would be made by the very men who had supported or opposed the measures being judged.

So the PASSIONS of the public, not its REASON, would sit in judgment. But it is the reason of the public alone that ought to control and regulate the government. The passions ought to be controlled and regulated by the government.

:::quote
The PASSIONS, therefore, not the REASON, of the public would sit in judgment. But it is the reason, alone, of the public, that ought to control and regulate the government. The passions ought to be controlled and regulated by the government.

Meaning: The people's calm, considered judgment should guide the government. Their momentary emotions should not. In fact, one job of government is to hold back those passions.
:::

:::commentary
This is the essay's most famous line, and it captures a key idea of the whole Constitution. Madison trusts the people's *reason*, expressed over time through elections and representatives. He distrusts their *passions*, the sudden anger or enthusiasm of a particular moment. Many features of the Constitution, like the Senate's six-year terms and the indirect election of the president, were designed to slow down decisions and give reason time to win out.

Critics, then and now, have asked who gets to decide which public opinions count as "reason" and which as "passion." Supporters of Madison's view say that slowing down decisions protects minorities and prevents mistakes. Critics say it can also block the will of the people for too long.
:::

## Conclusion {orig=11}

We found in the last paper that simply making declarations in the written constitution is not enough to keep the branches within their legal rights. This paper shows that occasional appeals to the people would be neither a proper nor an effective way to do it.

I won't examine how well the other kinds of safeguards in the plan I quoted would work. Some of them are certainly based on sound political principles, and all of them are designed with remarkable skill and precision.

:::connection
In [No. 50](/papers/50/), Madison considers a different version of the idea: appeals to the people at regular, scheduled times, like Pennsylvania's Council of Censors. His own solution comes in [No. 51](/papers/51/). For the problem he is trying to solve, see [No. 48](/papers/48/).
:::

:::today
Americans still argue about how often, and how directly, the people should decide constitutional questions. Many states let voters change their state constitutions directly through ballot measures, which is close to Jefferson's spirit. Some people have called for a national convention under Article V to propose amendments, while others warn that such a convention would be unpredictable and divisive, much as Madison feared. And the question Madison raised, who settles disputes between the branches, is mostly answered today by the courts, through judicial review. Supporters say judges are better placed than a heated popular vote to decide calmly. Critics say this gives unelected judges too much power over the Constitution.
:::

:::questions
- Madison agrees the people are the source of all power. Why, then, does he not want to appeal to them to settle every conflict between branches?
- Do you agree that a constitution needs "veneration," or respect that grows over time, to survive? Can too much respect be a problem?
- What is the difference between the public's "reason" and its "passions"? Can you think of a decision that might have turned out differently if people had waited and thought longer?
- Should constitutions be easy or hard to change? Explain your answer.
:::
