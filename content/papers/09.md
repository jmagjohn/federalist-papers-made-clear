:::summary
Hamilton argues that a strong Union will protect the states from factions and rebellions. He admits that the small republics of ancient Greece and Italy were constantly torn apart by conflict, and that enemies of liberty have used their failures to attack all free government. But the "science of politics" has improved. Modern inventions like separation of powers, checks and balances, independent judges, and elected representatives can fix the old weaknesses. To these he adds one more: **enlarging** the republic. He then turns the famous writer Montesquieu against the Constitution's critics. Montesquieu did say republics should be small, but he also praised a **confederate republic**, a union of smaller states, as the best way to get the benefits of both.
:::

:::keypoints
- The history of small ancient republics is a story of constant swings between **tyranny and anarchy**.
- Defenders of monarchy used that history to argue that **free government can't work**.
- But the "science of politics" has improved. Five modern tools help republics survive: **separation of powers**, **checks and balances**, **judges serving during good behavior**, **elected representatives**, and **a larger territory**.
- Critics quote **Montesquieu** saying republics must be small. But by his standard, even most individual states are too big.
- Montesquieu himself recommended a **confederate republic** as the solution, a union that has the inner happiness of small republics and the outer strength of a large monarchy.
- In such a union, if one state is taken over by a would-be tyrant or a rebellion, the others can help restore order.
- The Constitution still counts as a confederacy, since the states keep their own governments, an equal voice in the Senate, and important powers.
:::

:::context Background
The Constitution's opponents relied heavily on Montesquieu, the most respected political writer of the age. In *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), he argued that republics must be small, because in a large territory citizens lose touch with the public good and ambitious men can take over. Anti-Federalist writers like "Cato" and "Brutus" quoted him to argue that a single republic covering all thirteen states was bound to fail.

This essay and Madison's [No. 10](/papers/10/), published the next day, form a pair. Both argue that a large republic is actually *safer* from faction than a small one. Hamilton focuses on history and on Montesquieu. Madison builds a new theory.
:::

## The sad history of small republics {orig=1-2}

A firm Union will be extremely important to the peace and liberty of the states as a barrier against faction and rebellion at home.

It's impossible to read the history of the small republics of ancient Greece and Italy without feeling horror and disgust. They were constantly disrupted by conflict. One revolution followed another so quickly that they swung back and forth between the extremes of tyranny and anarchy.

When they had a moment of calm, it was only a brief contrast to the violent storms that followed. When we see a happy period in their history, we feel regret, knowing those pleasant scenes will soon be swept away by waves of rebellion and party rage. When moments of glory shine through the gloom, they dazzle us briefly. But they also make us sad that bad government twisted and tarnished the brilliant talents those famous lands produced.

Supporters of tyranny have used the disorders in the history of those republics to argue against more than just republican government. They have argued against the very principles of civil liberty. They have condemned all free government as incompatible with social order, and they have gloated over its friends and supporters.

Luckily for humanity, a few great free nations, built on the foundation of liberty and lasting for centuries, have proven their gloomy arguments wrong. And I trust that America will be the broad and solid foundation of other free nations, just as magnificent, that will stand as lasting proof of their errors.

:::vocab
Faction: A group, majority or minority, united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community. Madison explains it in detail in No. 10.
Insurrection: A violent uprising against the government; a rebellion.
Anarchy: A condition with no effective government, where disorder rules.
Despotism: Rule by one person with unlimited power.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is thinking of city-states like Athens, Sparta, Florence, and Genoa, which were famous for brilliant culture but also for coups, civil wars, and mob violence. Writers who favored kings often pointed to them as proof that ordinary people couldn't govern themselves.

The "stupendous fabrics reared on the basis of liberty" probably refers to examples like the Roman Republic, which lasted for centuries, and possibly Britain and the Dutch Republic. Hamilton's hope that America would inspire other free nations proved true. Many countries have since modeled constitutions partly on the American one.
:::

## The science of politics has improved {orig=3}

But we can't deny that the critics' portraits of republican government were all too accurate copies of the real thing. If it had been impossible to design better models, the wise friends of liberty would have had to give up republican government as impossible to defend.

The science of politics, however, has greatly improved, like most other sciences. We now understand how well various principles work that the ancients either didn't know at all or understood poorly:

- the regular division of power into separate branches;
- the introduction of legislative balances and checks;
- courts made up of judges who hold office during good behavior;
- the representation of the people in the legislature by delegates they elect.

These are either completely new discoveries or have made most of their progress in modern times. They are powerful tools for keeping the strengths of republican government while reducing or avoiding its flaws.

To this list of improvements in popular government, I'll dare to add one more, though it may seem new to some. It's based on a principle that has actually been used as an objection to the new Constitution. I mean the ENLARGEMENT of the ORBIT in which these systems operate, either by making a single state larger, or by combining several smaller states into one great Confederacy. The second is what matters for our present topic. But it will be useful to examine the principle as it applies to a single state, and that will be done elsewhere.

:::quote
The science of politics, however, like most other sciences, has received great improvement.

Meaning: Just as people have made progress in science and medicine, they have learned how to design better governments.
:::

:::vocab
Good behavior: The rule that judges keep their jobs as long as they behave properly, which in practice means for life.
Orbit (of a government): Hamilton's image for the territory a government covers. "Enlarging the orbit" means making the republic bigger.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the most optimistic ideas in the Federalist Papers. Hamilton treats government almost like engineering. People had tried republics and seen them fail, but now they understood *why* they failed and how to build them better. His list is basically a summary of the Constitution's design:

- **Separation of powers** is explained in [No. 47](/papers/47/).
- **Checks and balances** are explained in [No. 51](/papers/51/).
- **Independent judges** are explained in [No. 78](/papers/78/).
- **Representation** is explained in [No. 10](/papers/10/) and [No. 14](/papers/14/).
- **Enlarging the orbit** is the "examination in another place" he promises, which Madison delivers the next day in [No. 10](/papers/10/).
:::

## What Montesquieu actually said {orig=4-7}

The idea that a confederacy is useful, both to suppress faction and keep peace inside its states and to increase their outside strength and security, isn't new. It has been used in different countries and ages, and the most respected political writers have approved it.

The opponents of the Constitution have worked hard to quote and spread Montesquieu's comments about the need for a small territory in a republic. But they seem unaware of what that great man wrote in another part of his work. They also seem not to have thought about where the principle they so readily accept would lead.

When Montesquieu recommends a small size for republics, the examples he had in mind were far smaller than almost any of our states. Virginia, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, New York, North Carolina, and Georgia can't be compared to the models he reasoned from. So if we take his ideas on this point as the test of truth, we face two choices. We could run straight into the arms of monarchy. Or we could split ourselves into countless little, jealous, clashing, unruly republics. They would be miserable breeding grounds of endless conflict, and the world would pity or despise them.

Some writers on the other side seem to have noticed this problem. They have even boldly hinted that dividing the larger states would be a good thing. Such a foolish, desperate policy might serve men who lack the ability to extend their influence beyond small circles of personal scheming, by creating more minor offices for them to fill. But it could never advance the greatness or happiness of the American people.

I'll examine the principle itself elsewhere, as I said. Here, it's enough to point out that, in the sense meant by the author critics quote most, it would only call for reducing the SIZE of the larger STATES in the Union. It wouldn't argue against including them all in one confederate government. And that is the real question we're discussing.

In fact, far from opposing a general union of the states, Montesquieu explicitly discusses a confederate republic as the way to extend the reach of popular government, and to combine the advantages of monarchy with those of a republic.

:::vocab
Montesquieu: Charles de Montesquieu (1689–1755), French author of *The Spirit of the Laws*. Both sides in the ratification debate quoted him constantly.
Confederate republic: Montesquieu's term for a union of several smaller republics into a larger one.
Monarchy: Government by a king or queen.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's move here is sharp. If Montesquieu meant that republics must be as small as ancient Athens or Sparta, then even New York and Virginia were too big. Taken seriously, the Anti-Federalist argument would mean breaking up the states themselves, which almost nobody wanted.

His jab at people who want "petty offices" is aimed at state politicians who, in his view, opposed the Union because they would be big fish in small ponds.
:::

## Montesquieu's own words {orig=8-14}

"It is very probable," Montesquieu says, "that mankind would have been obliged at length to live constantly under the government of a single person, had they not contrived a kind of constitution that has all the internal advantages of a republican, together with the external force of a monarchical government. I mean a CONFEDERATE REPUBLIC."

He goes on to explain:

- A confederate republic is an agreement by which several smaller states agree to become members of a larger one. It can grow by adding new members until it is strong enough to provide for the security of the whole.
- A republic of this kind can resist outside force and can stay free of internal corruption.
- If one member tried to seize supreme power, he couldn't have equal authority and reputation in all the states. If he gained too much influence over one, that would alarm the rest. If he conquered part of the union, the part still free could fight him with its own forces and defeat him before he was settled in power.
- If a popular uprising broke out in one state, the others could put it down. If abuses crept into one part, the healthy parts could correct them.
- Because it is made up of small republics, it enjoys the inner happiness of each. And because of the union, it has all the outside advantages of large monarchies.

I have quoted these interesting passages at length because they contain a clear summary of the main arguments for the Union. They should correct the false impressions created by misusing other parts of Montesquieu's work. They also connect closely to the main point of this essay: showing how the Union tends to suppress faction and rebellion at home.

:::quote
"...a kind of constitution that has all the internal advantages of a republican, together with the external force of a monarchical government. I mean a CONFEDERATE REPUBLIC."

Meaning: Montesquieu thought a union of small republics could give people both the freedom of a small republic and the military strength of a large kingdom.
:::

:::commentary
This was a real gotcha. The Anti-Federalists' favorite authority turned out to support a union of republics. Montesquieu's point that the other states could "quell" a rebellion in one state would have reminded readers of **Shays' Rebellion** in Massachusetts in 1786–87. The weak national government couldn't help, and Massachusetts had to raise a privately funded army to stop the rebels.

The Constitution builds this idea in directly. Article IV, Section 4 promises that the United States will guarantee every state a republican government and protect it against "domestic violence."
:::

## Is the new plan still a confederacy? {orig=15-17}

Some people draw a distinction, more clever than accurate, between a CONFEDERACY and a CONSOLIDATION of the states. They say a confederacy's defining feature is that its authority reaches only the member states as collective bodies, not the individual people in them. They argue that the national council should have nothing to do with any internal matters. And they insist that strictly equal voting among the members is a key feature of a confederate government.

These claims are mostly arbitrary. Neither principle nor history supports them. It's true that governments of this kind have usually worked the way this distinction assumes. But most of them had large exceptions, which prove, as far as examples can, that there is no fixed rule. And as this series will clearly show, wherever the principle they defend has been followed, it has caused incurable disorder and weakness in government.

The definition of a CONFEDERATE REPUBLIC seems to be simply "an assemblage of societies," or a union of two or more states into one state. The extent, form, and purposes of the central authority are matters of choice. As long as the separate governments of the members are not abolished, and as long as they exist by constitutional necessity for local purposes, it is still, in fact and in theory, a union of states, or a confederacy. That's true even if the members are completely subordinate to the general authority of the union.

The proposed Constitution, far from abolishing the state governments, makes them essential parts of national sovereignty. It gives them direct representation in the Senate, and it leaves them certain exclusive and very important parts of sovereign power. In every reasonable sense of the words, this fits the idea of a federal government.

The Lycian confederacy was made up of twenty-three CITIES or republics. In its COMMON COUNCIL, the largest cities had THREE votes, the middle-sized ones TWO, and the smallest ONE. The COMMON COUNCIL appointed all the judges and officials of the cities. That was certainly the most sensitive kind of interference in their internal affairs. If anything seems to belong only to local governments, it is appointing their own officials. Yet Montesquieu, speaking of this union, says: "Were I to give a model of an excellent Confederate Republic, it would be that of Lycia." So we can see that the distinctions the critics insist on never occurred to this wise thinker. We must conclude that they are new inventions of a mistaken theory.

:::vocab
Consolidation: Merging the states into a single government that erases them as separate political units.
Suffrage: The right to vote. Here it means how many votes each member state gets.
Lycian confederacy: An ancient league of cities in what is now southwestern Turkey.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is answering a definition the Anti-Federalists liked: a true confederacy (1) acts only on states, (2) stays out of internal affairs, and (3) gives every member an equal vote. The Constitution breaks all three rules, so critics said it wasn't a confederacy at all but a "consolidated" national government.

Hamilton's reply is that these rules are made up. Montesquieu's favorite confederacy, Lycia, broke rules 2 and 3: bigger cities got more votes, and the central council appointed local officials. Madison takes a more careful approach to the same question in [No. 39](/papers/39/), concluding that the Constitution is partly national and partly federal.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's belief that the "science of politics" can improve is still the foundation of how people think about designing governments. When new democracies write constitutions, they choose among the same tools he lists: separation of powers, checks and balances, independent courts, elected representatives, and federal systems that divide power between national and regional governments. Countries like Germany, India, Canada, and Brazil all use some form of federalism. Hamilton's point that a union lets healthy states help a troubled one also still applies. The national government has stepped in to protect people's rights when a state failed to, as during the civil rights movement.
:::
