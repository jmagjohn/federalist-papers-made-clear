:::summary
Madison turns to the Senate. He explains why senators must be older and citizens longer than House members, why state legislatures chose them (at the time), and why each state gets two senators regardless of size, which he admits was a compromise, not a theory. Then he explains why a republic needs a Senate at all. A second, smaller chamber with longer terms guards against corrupt representatives, sudden passions, and lawmakers who don't know enough. Most of all, it provides **stability**. Laws that constantly change hurt a nation's reputation, confuse ordinary people, reward insiders who can profit from every change, discourage business, and erode respect for government.
:::

:::keypoints
- Senators must be **30 years old** and citizens for **9 years**, compared with 25 and 7 for House members, because the job needs more knowledge and stability.
- The nine-year rule balances welcoming immigrants with guarding against **foreign influence**.
- Choosing senators by **state legislatures** (as originally designed) gave the states a role in the national government.
- **Equal representation** of states was a compromise between large and small states, not a matter of theory.
- A second chamber **doubles the security** against corrupt or power-hungry lawmakers.
- It checks the tendency of large assemblies to give in to **"sudden and violent passions."**
- Longer terms help senators gain **knowledge** of laws and national affairs.
- Most important, the Senate provides **stability**. Unstable laws hurt the nation's reputation abroad and its people at home.
- "It will be of little avail to the people, that the laws are made by men of their own choice, if the laws be so voluminous that they cannot be read, or so incoherent that they cannot be understood."
:::

:::context Background
The Senate was at the center of the biggest fight at the Constitutional Convention. Large states like Virginia wanted both houses of Congress based on population. Small states like Delaware and New Jersey wanted every state to have an equal vote, as under the Articles of Confederation. The Convention nearly broke up over the issue in the summer of 1787. The result was the **Great Compromise** (also called the Connecticut Compromise): the House would be based on population and the Senate would give each state two votes.

Madison, a Virginian, actually opposed equal representation in the Senate and fought against it at the Convention. Here he has to defend a compromise he had voted against.
:::

## Five topics about the Senate {orig=1}

Having examined the House of Representatives and answered the objections to it that seemed worth noticing, I now turn to the Senate. It can be considered under five headings:

1. The qualifications of senators.
2. Their appointment by the state legislatures.
3. Equal representation of the states in the Senate.
4. The number of senators and the length of their terms.
5. The powers given to the Senate.

## Qualifications: older and longer citizens {orig=2}

The qualifications for senators differ from those for representatives in two ways: senators must be older, and they must have been citizens longer. A senator must be at least thirty years old, while a representative must be twenty-five. A senator must have been a citizen for nine years, while a representative needs seven.

These differences make sense because of the nature of a senator's job. It requires broader knowledge and steadier character, so a senator should have reached an age more likely to provide them. Senators also take a direct part in dealings with foreign nations. So the job should be held only by people who have fully left behind the attachments and habits that come with foreign birth and upbringing.

The nine-year requirement seems a sensible middle ground. It avoids completely shutting out adopted citizens, whose merits and talents may earn them public trust. And it avoids letting them in carelessly and quickly, which might open a channel for foreign influence in the national government.

:::vocab
Adopted citizen: An immigrant who has become a citizen; a naturalized citizen.
Prudent mediocrity: Madison's phrase for a sensible middle point. In 1787, "mediocrity" could mean "a middle position," not "poor quality."
:::

:::commentary
Madison is balancing two values. He wants to welcome immigrants into public life. Several framers, including Hamilton himself, were born outside the thirteen states. But he also worries that someone with strong ties to a foreign country might serve that country's interests. Today, senators still must be 30 years old and citizens for nine years. The president has a stricter rule: he or she must be a "natural born citizen."
:::

## Appointment by state legislatures {orig=3}

There's also little need to say much about senators being appointed by state legislatures. Of all the ways this branch could have been set up, the Convention's plan is probably the one that best fits public opinion. It has two advantages. It favors the selection of well-qualified people. And it gives the state governments a role in forming the national government, which protects the states' authority and creates a useful link between the two systems.

:::commentary
This changed in 1913, when the **17th Amendment** gave the people the power to elect senators directly. By then, the old system had serious problems. State legislatures often deadlocked for months over who to send to the Senate, leaving seats empty, and there were well-known cases of bribery. Reformers in the Progressive Era pushed for direct election to make the Senate more accountable to voters.

Some people today argue the change weakened the states, since senators no longer answer to state governments. Others say direct election was a clear improvement in democracy.
:::

## Equal votes for every state: a compromise {orig=4-6}

Equal representation in the Senate is another point that needs little discussion. It clearly came from a compromise between the competing demands of the large and small states.

There is some logic to it. Among a people fully combined into one nation, every district should have a PROPORTIONAL share in the government. Among independent and sovereign states joined in a simple alliance, every member, however unequal in size, should have an EQUAL share in their common councils. So in a compound republic that is partly national and partly federal, it makes some sense for the government to mix both principles, proportional and equal representation.

But it's pointless to judge by theory a part of the Constitution that everyone agrees came not from theory, but "from a spirit of amity, and that mutual deference and concession which the peculiarity of our political situation rendered indispensable." America's voice, and even more its situation, demands a common government with powers equal to its responsibilities. The smaller states are not likely to agree to a government built on principles closer to what the larger states want. So the larger states' only choice is between the proposed government and one that's even worse. Given that choice, common sense says to accept the lesser evil. Instead of imagining all the harm that might follow, we should think about the benefits that may make up for the sacrifice.

In that spirit, we can note that giving each state an equal vote both officially recognizes the share of sovereignty the states keep, and helps preserve it. So the large states should find this equality just as acceptable as the small ones. The large states are no less eager to guard in every way against an improper merging of the states into a single republic.

Another benefit of this feature of the Senate is that it adds another obstacle to bad laws. No law or resolution can be passed without the agreement, first, of a majority of the people, and then of a majority of the states.

It must be admitted that this complicated check on lawmaking may sometimes do harm as well as good. And the special protection it gives the smaller states would make more sense if they had shared interests, different from the other states', that would otherwise be in special danger. But the larger states will always be able to block unreasonable uses of this power by the smaller states, through their control over money. And the ability and tendency to make too many laws seem to be the diseases our governments suffer from most. So this part of the Constitution may work better in practice than many people expect.

:::quote
...the result, not of theory, but "of a spirit of amity, and that mutual deference and concession which the peculiarity of our political situation rendered indispensable."

Meaning: Equal votes in the Senate weren't based on a political theory. They were a compromise, made in a spirit of friendship, because there was no other way to get agreement. Madison is quoting the letter George Washington sent to Congress with the Constitution.
:::

:::vocab
Great Compromise: The 1787 agreement that the House would be based on population and the Senate would give each state an equal number of seats.
Proportional representation: Giving each area seats according to its population.
Residuary sovereignty: The power the states keep after giving some powers to the national government.
:::

:::commentary
This passage is unusually honest. Madison basically says: this isn't the system I would have chosen, but it was the price of union, and here is the best case for it. His point that the large states can block the small ones through "their power over the supplies" refers to the rule that all tax bills must start in the House, where large states have more votes.

His prediction that small states wouldn't have special shared interests was partly right. Senators rarely vote as a "small state" bloc. But the imbalance has grown far larger than in 1787. Then, the largest state (Virginia) had about 13 times the population of the smallest (Delaware). Today, California has about 68 times the population of Wyoming, yet both have two senators. Critics say this gives voters in small states far more power. Defenders say it protects regional diversity and the states' role in the federal system. The Constitution makes it almost impossible to change: Article V says no state can lose its equal vote in the Senate without its own consent.
:::

## Why a republic needs a Senate {orig=7-9}

Next come the number of senators and the length of their terms. To judge both correctly, we should ask what purposes a senate serves. And to find that out, we should look at the problems a republic would suffer without one.

**First**, it's a misfortune of republican government, though less than of other governments, that those who run it may forget their duties to the people and betray their trust. Seen this way, a senate, as a second branch of the legislature that is separate from the first and shares power with it, must always be a healthy check on the government. It doubles the people's security, because any scheme to grab power or betray the people would need the agreement of two separate bodies, when otherwise the ambition or corruption of one would be enough.

This precaution rests on such clear principles, and is so well understood in the United States now, that saying more would be pointless. I'll only note that bad alliances between the two bodies will be less likely the more different their character is. So it's wise to make them as different as possible, while still allowing harmony on proper measures and staying true to republican principles.

**Second**, a senate is needed because all single, large assemblies tend to give in to sudden and violent passions, and to be led by factional leaders into rash and harmful decisions. Countless examples could be given, from the United States as well as from other nations' histories. But a point no one will deny doesn't need proving. All that needs saying is that a body meant to correct this weakness should itself be free of it. So it should be smaller. It should also be very firm, so its members should hold office for a fairly long time.

:::commentary
A famous story, possibly made up, illustrates this idea. Thomas Jefferson supposedly asked George Washington why the Convention created a Senate. Washington asked, "Why did you pour that coffee into your saucer?" "To cool it," Jefferson replied. "Even so," said Washington, "we pour legislation into the senatorial saucer to cool it." Whether or not it really happened, the story captures Madison's point: the Senate is meant to slow down and cool off hasty decisions from the House.
:::

## Lawmakers need knowledge {orig=10-11}

**Third**, a senate makes up for a lack of familiarity with the subjects and principles of lawmaking. Consider an assembly made up mostly of people pulled from private jobs, serving for a short time, with no lasting reason to spend their time between sessions studying the laws, affairs, and broad interests of their country. Left entirely on their own, they can't avoid making many important mistakes.

There's good reason to say that a large share of America's current troubles comes from the blunders of our governments, and that these came from the heads rather than the hearts of most of the people who made them. What are all the laws that repeal, explain, and amend earlier laws, which fill and disgrace our thick law books, but monuments to a lack of wisdom? Each one is a charge brought by one session against the last. Each one reminds the people how valuable a well-designed senate could be.

Good government requires two things. First, faithfulness to the purpose of government, which is the happiness of the people. Second, knowledge of the best ways to achieve that purpose. Some governments lack both. Most governments lack the first. I don't hesitate to say that American governments have paid too little attention to the second. The national Constitution avoids this mistake. And, notably, it provides for knowledge in a way that also increases the security of faithfulness.

:::quote
A good government implies two things: first, fidelity to the object of government, which is the happiness of the people; secondly, a knowledge of the means by which that object can be best attained.

Meaning: Good government needs both good intentions and know-how. Leaders have to want what's best for the people and also know how to achieve it.
:::

:::vocab
Fidelity: Faithfulness or loyalty.
Voluminous codes: Very long collections of laws.
:::

## The dangers of unstable laws {orig=12-14}

**Fourth**, the constant change in government that comes from a rapid turnover of members, however qualified they may be, shows very strongly why some stable institution is needed. Every new election in the states changes about half of the representatives. A change of people brings a change of opinions, and a change of opinions brings a change of policies. But constantly changing even good policies goes against every rule of good sense and every hope of success. This is true in private life, and it's even more true, and more important, for nations.

Describing all the harm caused by an unstable government would fill a book. I'll mention just a few problems, each of which leads to countless others.

First, it loses the respect and trust of other nations, along with all the advantages that come from a good national reputation. Think of a person who keeps changing his plans, or who seems to run his affairs with no plan at all. Every sensible person sees right away that he will soon be ruined by his own unsteadiness and foolishness. His friendlier neighbors may pity him, but none will tie their fortunes to his, and quite a few will take the chance to profit from him.

One nation is to another what one person is to another, with one sad difference. Nations feel less kindness toward each other than individuals do, so they also hold back less from taking advantage of each other's mistakes. So any nation whose affairs show a lack of wisdom and stability can expect to suffer every loss that its wiser, more organized neighbors can inflict.

Sadly, America's own situation provides the best lesson on this. Its friends don't respect it. Its enemies mock it. And every nation that sees a chance to profit from its changing policies and troubled affairs takes advantage of it.

:::commentary
Madison is describing America under the Articles of Confederation. Each state passed its own trade laws, often changing them frequently, and Congress couldn't keep its treaty promises. European nations didn't take the United States seriously. John Adams, then America's representative in London, struggled to get Britain even to discuss a trade treaty.
:::

## "Little avail" to have laws no one can understand {orig=15-16}

The effects of unstable policy at home are even worse. It poisons the blessing of liberty itself.

It will do the people little good that their laws are made by people they chose, if the laws are so long that no one can read them, or so confused that no one can understand them. Or if they are repealed or revised before they are even published. Or if they change so constantly that no one who knows what the law is today can guess what it will be tomorrow. Law is defined as a rule of action. But how can something be a rule if it is barely known and even less fixed?

Another effect of instability is the unfair advantage it gives to the clever, the bold, and the wealthy few over the hardworking and uninformed majority of the people. Every new regulation about trade or taxes, or anything that affects the value of different kinds of property, offers a new harvest to those who watch the changes and can figure out their effects. It is a harvest that they didn't grow themselves. It was grown by the work and worry of the great body of their fellow citizens. In this situation, it can truly be said that laws are made for the FEW, not for the MANY.

:::quote
It will be of little avail to the people, that the laws are made by men of their own choice, if the laws be so voluminous that they cannot be read, or so incoherent that they cannot be understood...

Meaning: Electing your lawmakers doesn't help much if the laws they make are too long to read or too confusing to understand.
:::

:::quote
This is a state of things in which it may be said with some truth that laws are made for the FEW, not for the MANY.

Meaning: When laws keep changing, insiders who follow every change can profit, while ordinary people fall behind.
:::

:::commentary
This passage sounds remarkably modern. Federal laws today can run thousands of pages, and the tax code and federal regulations fill many thousands more. Madison's warning that constant changes reward "the sagacious, the enterprising, and the moneyed few" describes what happens when lobbyists, lawyers, and investors who track every new rule gain an edge over ordinary people who can't.

Interestingly, this is one of the few places where Madison sounds like he's defending the "many" against the wealthy "few." In [No. 10](/papers/10/), his main worry was the opposite: majorities threatening the property of the few.
:::

:::vocab
Sagacious: Wise and shrewd, especially in practical matters.
Promulgated: Officially announced or published.
:::

## Instability discourages business and destroys trust {orig=17-18}

An unstable government causes great harm in another way. Without confidence in the government, people are discouraged from any useful project whose success and profit depend on current rules staying in place.

What sensible merchant will risk his fortune in a new line of trade when he doesn't know whether his plans will be made illegal before he can carry them out? What farmer or manufacturer will invest in a crop or business that the government is encouraging, when he has no guarantee that all his preparation and spending won't be wasted by a government that changes its mind? In short, no great improvement or worthy enterprise can go forward if it needs a steady national policy behind it.

But the worst effect of all is the loss of affection and respect that slowly creeps into the people's hearts toward a political system that shows so many signs of weakness and disappoints so many of their hopes. No government, any more than an individual, will be respected for long without truly deserving respect. And it won't truly deserve respect without a certain amount of order and stability.

:::quote
No government, any more than an individual, will long be respected without being truly respectable; nor be truly respectable, without possessing a certain portion of order and stability.

Meaning: People will only respect a government that earns respect, and a government can't earn respect if it is chaotic and always changing.
:::

:::commentary
Madison ends with his deepest worry: that people will lose faith in self-government itself. If laws keep changing and government keeps failing, people may start to wish for a strong ruler who can bring order. Republics depend on the trust of their citizens, and that trust depends on government working reasonably well.

Madison continues his discussion of the Senate's purposes in [No. 63](/papers/63/), where he adds that the Senate can protect the people "against their own temporary errors and delusions."
:::

:::today
Debates about the Senate are as lively as ever. Critics point out that senators representing a minority of Americans can block laws supported by most of the country, and that the filibuster, a Senate rule that usually requires 60 votes to pass most bills, adds another barrier Madison never imagined. Defenders say the Senate does exactly what Madison wanted: it slows things down, forces compromise, and keeps laws from swinging wildly every time power changes hands. His warnings about long, confusing, constantly changing laws that favor insiders also remain a common complaint about modern government.
:::
