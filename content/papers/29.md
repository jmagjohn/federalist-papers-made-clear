:::summary
Hamilton defends the Constitution's militia clauses. Congress gets power to organize, arm, and discipline the militia, and to command it when it is called into national service. The states keep the power to appoint its officers and to train it by the rules Congress sets. Uniform rules would make the militia far more effective, and a strong militia is the best way to make a standing army unnecessary. Hamilton then makes a frank admission: training *every* citizen to a high standard is impossible. The practical plan is to keep the people at large armed and equipped, and to train a smaller **"select corps"** well. He mocks the opponents' fears of the militia as monster stories, since the militia is made up of "our sons, our brothers, our neighbors," and its officers are chosen by the states.
:::

:::keypoints
- Power over the militia naturally belongs to the government responsible for **common defense** and **internal peace**.
- **Uniform** organization and discipline would make the militia far more effective in war.
- Congress may organize, arm, and discipline the militia; **the states appoint the officers** and do the training.
- If a **"well-regulated militia"** is the natural defense of a free country, the national government should be able to use it. "To render an army unnecessary" beats "a thousand prohibitions upon paper."
- The claim that Congress can't call out the **posse comitatus** is false. The **necessary and proper** clause covers it.
- Training the **whole militia** well is impossible and would be a heavy burden. The people at large should be **armed and equipped** and gathered once or twice a year.
- A **select corps** of moderate size could be well trained, reducing the need for an army and guarding against one.
- Fear of the militia is absurd: it is made up of fellow citizens, and the **states choose its officers**.
- Sending one state's militia to help another in an emergency is a **main purpose of the Union**.
:::

:::context Background
Americans in 1787 thought of the **militia** as the citizens themselves, organized for defense. Most states required able-bodied free men to enroll, bring their own weapons, and gather for occasional drills. Many saw the militia as the free alternative to a professional standing army, which they feared as a tool of tyranny.

The Constitution gives Congress power to call forth the militia "to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections and repel Invasions," and to provide for "organizing, arming, and disciplining" it. Anti-Federalists worried that Congress might neglect the militia, leaving the country to rely on a standing army, or turn it into a weapon against the people. This essay answers them. It is one of the most cited Federalist papers in modern debates over the Second Amendment.
:::

## Why the Union should regulate the militia {orig=1-2}

The Union must provide for the common defense and watch over the peace at home. The power to regulate the militia, and to command it in times of uprising and invasion, comes naturally with those duties.

It takes no skill in the science of war to see that uniform organization and discipline would greatly benefit the militia whenever it was called into service to defend the public. It would let militiamen carry out their duties in camp and on the battlefield with shared understanding and coordination. That is an especially important advantage when an army is in action. It would also help them gain much sooner the military skill they would need to be useful.

Only the national government can create this uniformity, so it must direct the rules for the militia. So it is entirely proper that the Convention's plan gives the Union power:

> "to provide for organizing, arming, and disciplining the militia, and for governing such part of them as may be employed in the service of the United States, RESERVING TO THE STATES RESPECTIVELY THE APPOINTMENT OF THE OFFICERS, AND THE AUTHORITY OF TRAINING THE MILITIA ACCORDING TO THE DISCIPLINE PRESCRIBED BY CONGRESS."

:::vocab
Militia: A military force made up of ordinary citizens, rather than professional soldiers, who train part-time and can be called up in emergencies.
Discipline: In military terms, the system of drills, rules, and training that makes soldiers act together effectively.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is quoting Article I, Section 8, Clause 16. The clause just before it lets Congress call the militia into national service. Together, they create a shared system. Congress sets the rules and can command the militia when it is in national service. The states appoint the officers and run the training.

Hamilton's point about uniformity came from experience. During the Revolution, militia units from different states used different drills, weapons, and organization. That made it hard for them to fight together or alongside the Continental Army.
:::

## A strong militia makes an army unnecessary {orig=3}

Of all the grounds on which the Convention's plan has been attacked, none was less expected, or is weaker in itself, than the attack on this provision.

- If a well-regulated militia is the most natural defense of a free country, then the guardian of national security should certainly control and use it.
- If standing armies are dangerous to liberty, then the body that protects the nation should have real power over the militia. That would, as far as possible, remove both the motive and the excuse for an army.
- If the national government can call on the militia in emergencies when the civil authorities need military support, it will have less need for a different kind of force. If it can't use the militia, it will have to turn to an army.

Making an army unnecessary will be a surer way to prevent one than a thousand bans written on paper.

:::quote
To render an army unnecessary, will be a more certain method of preventing its existence than a thousand prohibitions upon paper.

Meaning: The best way to avoid a dangerous standing army isn't to ban it in the Constitution. It's to have a militia strong enough that no army is needed.
:::

## The "posse comitatus" objection {orig=4}

Some opponents want to make the power to call out the militia look bad. So they point out that the Constitution never mentions calling out the POSSE COMITATUS to help officials do their duty. From this they conclude that military force was meant to be the officials' only help.

The objections that have appeared are strikingly inconsistent, sometimes even when they come from the same writers. That doesn't inspire much confidence in their sincerity or fairness. The same people who tell us one moment that the national government's powers will be despotic and unlimited tell us the next that it doesn't even have enough authority to call out the posse comitatus. Luckily, the second claim falls as far short of the truth as the first goes beyond it.

The national government has a right to pass all laws NECESSARY AND PROPER to carry out its listed powers. It would be absurd to doubt that this includes the power to require citizens to help the officers who enforce those laws. It would be just as absurd to believe the opposite extreme. No one thinks a right to make laws needed for collecting taxes would let Congress change the rules for inheriting and selling land. Nor would it let Congress abolish jury trials in cases about land.

So the claim that the government lacks power to call for the posse comitatus has no basis at all. It follows that the conclusion drawn from it about the national government's power over the militia is as unfair as it is illogical. Why assume that force was meant to be the *only* tool of authority, just because there is a power to use it when necessary? What should we think of the motives of sensible men who reason this way? How can we keep our wish to judge them kindly from clashing with what we actually believe?

:::vocab
Posse comitatus: Latin for "the power of the county." The ordinary citizens a sheriff could call on to help enforce the law.
Necessary and proper clause: The last clause of Article I, Section 8. It lets Congress pass all laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out its other powers.
Despotic: Having unlimited power, like a tyrant.
:::

:::commentary
The posse comitatus is the old English idea behind the "posse" of Western movies: a sheriff gathering local citizens to help catch a criminal. Hamilton's point is that the national government, like any government, could call on ordinary citizens to help its officers. It wouldn't have to rely on soldiers.

The phrase later took on a different meaning. The Posse Comitatus Act of 1878, passed after the end of Reconstruction, limits the use of the U.S. Army to enforce domestic laws. Hamilton's argument about the "necessary and proper" clause also previews his later, broader reading of that clause as Treasury Secretary, when he argued it allowed Congress to create a national bank.
:::

## The fear of a "select corps," and my advice {orig=5-8}

By a strange stretching of republican suspicion, we are even told to fear the militia itself in the hands of the national government. Some say that select corps might be formed out of young and eager men, who could be made to serve the purposes of arbitrary power.

What plan the national government will adopt for regulating the militia is impossible to predict. But I do not see the matter the way those who object to select corps as dangerous do. If the Constitution were ratified, and I were giving my views on the militia to a member of Congress from this state, I would say to him, in substance, the following:

"The plan of training the entire militia of the United States is as useless as it would be harmful, even if it could be carried out. Reasonable skill in military movements takes time and practice. A day, or even a week, is not enough to gain it.

"Think about forcing most farmers and other citizens to take up arms and practice military drills. They would have to drill often enough to earn the name of a *well-regulated militia*. That would be a real hardship to the people and a serious public inconvenience and loss. It would take away, every year, an amount of productive work that, at our present population, would come close to the whole cost of running all the state governments. It would be unwise to attempt something that would cut so deeply into the country's labor and industry. And if it were tried, it couldn't succeed, because people would not put up with it for long.

"With respect to the people at large, we can reasonably aim for little more than having them properly armed and equipped. To make sure this isn't neglected, it will be necessary to assemble them once or twice a year.

"So the plan to train the whole nation must be dropped as harmful or unworkable. But it is still extremely important to adopt a careful plan for organizing the militia, and to do it soon. The government should especially focus on forming a select corps of moderate size, organized on principles that will truly prepare them for service when needed. By narrowing the plan this way, we can have an excellent body of well-trained militia, ready to take the field whenever the defense of the country requires it.

"This will not only reduce the need for military forces. It will also mean that, if circumstances ever force the government to form an army of any size, that army can never threaten the liberties of the people. There will be a large body of citizens, little if at all inferior to the soldiers in discipline and the use of arms, ready to defend their own rights and those of their fellow citizens. This seems to me the only possible substitute for a standing army, and the best possible protection against one, if it should exist."

That is how differently from the Constitution's opponents I would reason on the same subject. I draw arguments for safety from the very sources they describe as full of danger and ruin. But how Congress will reason on the point is something neither they nor I can foresee.

:::quote
Little more can reasonably be aimed at, with respect to the people at large, than to have them properly armed and equipped...

Meaning: It's not realistic to train every citizen to a high military standard. But the government can at least make sure the people as a whole are armed and equipped.
:::

:::quote
This appears to me the only substitute that can be devised for a standing army, and the best possible security against it, if it should exist.

Meaning: A well-trained select militia, backed by an armed population, is the best replacement for a standing army, and the best protection against one.
:::

:::vocab
Select corps: A smaller, specially chosen group within the militia that receives more training. Anti-Federalists feared a "select militia" could become a private force for the government.
Yeomanry: Farmers who own and work their own land; ordinary rural citizens.
Well-regulated militia: A militia that is properly organized, trained, and disciplined. The phrase appears in the Second Amendment.
:::

:::commentary
This passage is central to modern arguments about the Second Amendment, which begins: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed."

People read Hamilton's words in different ways. Some focus on his view that "the people at large" should be "properly armed and equipped," and on his picture of "a large body of citizens" who can match an army in "the use of arms." They see this as evidence that the founders valued an armed citizenry in general, a view in line with the Supreme Court's ruling in *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008) that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to keep and bear arms. Others focus on his emphasis on organization, training, and a "select corps" under government rules. They see this as evidence that the founding generation linked arms to organized militia service, a view the four dissenting justices in *Heller* took.

Hamilton's plan was not adopted. The **Militia Act of 1792** required free, able-bodied white male citizens between 18 and 45 to enroll in the militia and supply their own musket or rifle and equipment, but it created no well-trained select corps, and training was left mostly to the states. Over time, much of the militia system fell into neglect. Note also that the 1792 law limited militia service to white men, a reminder of who was counted as part of "the people" at the time.
:::

## Why there is nothing to fear from the militia {orig=9}

There is something so far-fetched and extreme in the idea that the militia endangers liberty that one hardly knows whether to treat it seriously or with ridicule. Is it just a show of skill, like the paradoxes of debaters? Is it a dishonest trick to plant prejudices at any cost? Or is it the serious product of political fanaticism?

In the name of common sense, where are our fears to end if we can't trust our sons, our brothers, our neighbors, our fellow citizens? What shadow of danger can come from men who mix with the rest of their countrymen every day, and who share their feelings, opinions, habits, and interests?

The Union will have power to make rules for the militia and to command it when necessary. But the states will have the SOLE AND EXCLUSIVE APPOINTMENT OF THE OFFICERS. So what reasonable cause for alarm can there be? If anyone could seriously distrust the militia under any possible national arrangement, the fact that the states appoint the officers should end that distrust at once. This fact will always give the states the dominant influence over the militia.

:::quote
Where in the name of common-sense, are our fears to end if we may not trust our sons, our brothers, our neighbors, our fellow-citizens?

Meaning: The militia is made up of ordinary people from every community. It makes no sense to fear that they would be turned against their own families and neighbors.
:::

## Monster stories {orig=10-13}

When you read many of the publications against the Constitution, you might think you were reading some badly written tale or romance. Instead of natural and pleasant images, it shows nothing but frightening and twisted shapes:

> "Gorgons, hydras, and chimeras dire";

Such a tale distorts whatever it describes. It turns everything it touches into a monster.

One example is the exaggerated and unlikely claims made about the power to call out the militia. New Hampshire's militia, we're told, will be marched to Georgia, and Georgia's to New Hampshire. New York's will be sent to Kentucky, and Kentucky's to Lake Champlain. The debts we owe the French and the Dutch will even be paid in militiamen instead of gold coins! One moment there is to be a large army to crush the people's liberties. The next moment, Virginia's militia are to be dragged five or six hundred miles from their homes to tame the stubborn republican spirit of Massachusetts. And Massachusetts's militia will be carried the same distance to subdue the proud, aristocratic Virginians. Do the people who rave like this think their skill or their speeches can fool the people of America into accepting such absurd fantasies as unquestionable truths?

:::commentary
The line "Gorgons, hydras, and chimeras dire" comes from John Milton's epic poem *Paradise Lost* (1667). Gorgons, hydras, and chimeras are monsters from Greek mythology. Hamilton is saying the Anti-Federalists' fears are as imaginary as these creatures.

Some of his references need explaining. Kentucky was still part of Virginia in 1788; it became a separate state in 1792. Lake Champlain lies on the border between New York and Vermont. Louis d'ors were French gold coins, and ducats were Dutch gold coins. The United States owed large debts to France and to Dutch bankers from loans that helped pay for the Revolutionary War.
:::

:::vocab
Gorgons, hydras, and chimeras: Monsters from Greek mythology. Gorgons, like Medusa, could turn people to stone; the hydra had many heads; the chimera was part lion, part goat, and part snake.
Contumacy: Stubborn refusal to obey authority.
Refractory: Stubborn and hard to control.
:::

## Usurpers don't start by angering their own tools {orig=14-15}

If there were an army ready to be used as a tool of tyranny, why would anyone need the militia? And if there were no army, where would the militia go? Angered at being ordered on a distant and hopeless march to fasten the chains of slavery on some of their countrymen, they would turn toward the capital of the tyrants who had planned such a foolish and wicked scheme. They would crush the tyrants in their imagined fortresses of power and make them an example of the just revenge of an abused and furious people.

Is this how usurpers rise to power over a large and enlightened nation? Do they begin by making the very instruments of their plans hate them? Do they usually start their careers with reckless and disgusting acts of power that serve no purpose except to bring universal hatred and curses down on themselves? Are such suggestions the sober warnings of wise patriots to a wise people? Or are they the inflammatory ravings of troublemakers or unbalanced fanatics? Even if we imagined the national rulers driven by the most uncontrollable ambition, it is impossible to believe they would use such ridiculous means to reach their goals.

In times of rebellion or invasion, it would be natural and proper for one state's militia to march into a neighboring state. It might go to resist a common enemy, or to protect the republic against the violence of faction or revolt. The first of these happened often during the last war. This kind of mutual help is, in fact, one of the main purposes of our union. If the Union directs this power, there will be no danger that a state will lazily ignore its neighbor's trouble. Without it, a state might wait until the danger came close to home. Only then would fear for its own safety add to its weak sense of duty and sympathy.

:::vocab
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction about militia crossing state lines was tested within a few years. In 1794, during the Whiskey Rebellion, President Washington called up militia from Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Maryland, and Virginia to march into western Pennsylvania. Most militiamen answered the call, as Hamilton expected, and the rebels gave way without a battle.

Notice that Hamilton's image of a militia turning against tyrants fits Madison's argument in [No. 46](/papers/46/). Both founders saw an armed citizenry, organized by the states, as a check on a national government that might go bad. They also both assumed that militiamen would share the views of their communities. That assumption is part of what makes this essay so important to both sides of the modern gun debate.
:::

:::today
The militia system Hamilton described has changed greatly. The **Militia Act of 1903** created the modern **National Guard**, a trained, federally funded force organized by the states, much like the "select corps" Hamilton recommended. Federal law still defines the militia as including both the National Guard (the "organized militia") and most other able-bodied men of military age (the "unorganized militia"). Governors command their Guard units in normal times, but the president can call them into national service, just as the Constitution's militia clauses allow. Meanwhile, Americans continue to debate what Hamilton's "well-regulated militia" means for the right to bear arms. Some see the National Guard as today's militia, while others argue the right belongs to the people as a whole, whether or not they serve in any organized force.
:::
