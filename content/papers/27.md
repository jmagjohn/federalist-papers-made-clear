:::summary
Opponents claimed the new national government would need an army to force people to obey its laws. Hamilton disagrees. People obey a government roughly in proportion to how well it is run, and there are good reasons to expect the national government to be run well. Troublemakers are also less likely to defy a government that can call on the strength of the whole Union. Most importantly, the more the national government becomes part of people's everyday lives, the more familiar it will feel and the more they will respect it. Because the Constitution acts on individuals and makes national laws the **supreme law of the land**, state judges and officials will help enforce them. That makes peaceful enforcement far more likely than under the old league, which could only use force against whole states.
:::

:::keypoints
- The claim that the Constitution will need **military force** to carry out its laws is just a bare assertion.
- People's **trust and obedience** usually match how **well or badly** a government is run.
- The national government will likely be run **better**: larger districts offer more choice, the Senate will be chosen carefully, and national leaders will be less swayed by local passions.
- A rebellious group might take on one state, but not the **combined strength of the Union**.
- "**Man is very much a creature of habit.**" A government people see and deal with every day will win their attachment.
- So handling **internal matters** will make the national government stronger and less in need of force.
- The old league could enforce its decisions only by **war against the states**.
- National laws are the **supreme law of the land**, and state officials take an **oath** to support them, so state courts and officers will help enforce them.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues Hamilton's answer to fears about military force. Anti-Federalists warned that a national government reaching into people's daily lives, for example by collecting taxes, would be resented. To make people obey, they said, it would need an army. That army would then threaten liberty.

Hamilton's answer builds on [No. 15](/papers/15/) and [No. 16](/papers/16/), where he argued that a government acting on *states* can only enforce its will by war, while a government acting on *individuals* can use ordinary courts.
:::

## Will the new government need an army to enforce its laws? {orig=1}

It has been argued, in different forms, that a Constitution like the one the Convention proposes can't work without an army to carry out its laws. But like most claims from that side, this rests on a bare assertion. No clear or exact reasons are given for it.

As far as I can guess the hidden meaning of the objectors, it seems to come from an assumption: that the people will dislike any use of national power in *internal* matters. I won't stop to point out how unclear the line between "internal" and "external" is. Instead, let us ask what grounds there are for assuming the people will feel this dislike.

Unless we also assume that the national government will be run worse than the state governments, there seems to be no reason to expect ill will, discontent, or resistance from the people. I believe a general rule can be stated here. People's trust in a government, and their obedience to it, will usually match how good or bad its administration is. There are exceptions to this rule, I admit. But they depend so completely on chance causes that they have nothing to do with the real strengths or weaknesses of a constitution. Those can only be judged by general principles.

:::quote
I believe it may be laid down as a general rule that their confidence in and obedience to a government will commonly be proportioned to the goodness or badness of its administration.

Meaning: People trust and obey a government about as much as it deserves. A well-run government earns obedience; a badly run one loses it.
:::

## Why the national government may be run better {orig=2}

In these papers I have given several reasons to expect that the national government will be run better than the state governments. The main ones are these:

- Larger election districts will give the people a wider range of candidates to choose from.
- The national Senate will be chosen by the state legislatures, which are themselves select bodies of men. So we can expect the Senate to be put together with special care and judgment.
- For these reasons, the national government should have greater knowledge and broader information.
- It will be less likely to be poisoned by the spirit of faction. And it will be further out of reach of the passing bad moods, short-term prejudices, and impulses that often corrupt public councils in smaller communities. Those impulses cause injustice and oppression of part of the community. They produce schemes that satisfy a momentary desire but end in widespread distress, discontent, and disgust.

More strong reasons will appear when we look more carefully at the inner structure of the building we are being asked to put up. Here it is enough to say this. Until someone gives good reasons to think the national government will be run so badly that the people will hate or despise it, there is no reasonable basis for assuming that its laws will meet more resistance than state laws. Nor is there any reason to think they will need any other means of enforcement.

:::commentary
Hamilton is summarizing ideas from [No. 10](/papers/10/), where Madison argued that a large republic would elect better representatives and be less vulnerable to faction than a small one. The point about the Senate refers to the original Constitution, under which state legislatures chose senators. The 17th Amendment (1913) changed this so that voters elect senators directly.

Whether the national government is in fact "better administered" than the states is still debated. Supporters of national action point to areas like civil rights, where the national government protected people the states had failed. Supporters of state power point to the states' closeness to the people and their ability to try new ideas.
:::

:::vocab
Faction: A group of people united by a shared interest or passion that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
Administration: The way a government carries out its work day to day.
:::

## Lawbreakers will fear the whole Union {orig=3}

The hope of getting away with it is a strong encouragement to rebellion. The fear of punishment is an equally strong discouragement. A national government with enough power can call on the combined resources of the whole country. Won't it be more likely to crush the first feeling and inspire the second than a single state, which can only use its own resources?

A violent faction within a state may easily think it can fight the government's supporters in that state. But it could hardly be foolish enough to believe it could match the combined efforts of the Union. If this is right, there is less danger of resistance from unlawful groups of individuals to the national government than to a single state.

:::vocab
Sedition: Speech or action that stirs up rebellion against the government.
Impunity: Freedom from punishment.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton surely had Shays' Rebellion (1786–87) in mind, when Massachusetts struggled on its own to put down an uprising (see [No. 25](/papers/25/)). His prediction was tested in 1794, during the **Whiskey Rebellion**. Farmers in western Pennsylvania violently resisted a national tax on whiskey, a tax Hamilton himself had proposed as Treasury Secretary. President Washington called up about 13,000 militiamen from several states and marched toward the region, with Hamilton riding along. The rebels scattered without a battle. Supporters saw this as proof of Hamilton's point. Critics saw a huge show of force against poor farmers, which seemed to confirm the Anti-Federalists' fear that the national government would use armed force to collect taxes.
:::

## Familiarity builds attachment {orig=4}

I will risk an observation here. It is no less true for seeming new to some people. The more the national government's work is mixed into the ordinary business of government, the more citizens will grow attached to it. The more often they meet it in the everyday events of political life, the more familiar it will become to their eyes and their feelings. And the further it reaches into the things that touch people's deepest feelings and move their hearts most strongly, the more likely it is to win the community's respect and loyalty.

Man is very much a creature of habit. A thing that rarely reaches his senses will usually have little influence on his mind. A government that is always far away and out of sight can hardly be expected to stir the people's feelings.

So the national government's authority, and the citizens' affection for it, will be strengthened, not weakened, if it extends to what are called internal matters. And the more familiar and wide-reaching its work becomes, the less often it will need to turn to force. The more it flows through the channels where human feelings naturally run, the less it will need the violent and dangerous methods of compulsion.

:::quote
Man is very much a creature of habit. A thing that rarely strikes his senses will generally have but little influence upon his mind.

Meaning: People care about what they see and deal with regularly. A government that seems distant will never win their loyalty, but one that is part of daily life will.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of Hamilton's most interesting predictions. It is the opposite of what Madison argued in [No. 46](/papers/46/) and what Hamilton himself said in [No. 17](/papers/17/): that people would stay more attached to their states because the states handle daily life. Here Hamilton suggests that if the national government handles daily matters too, people's loyalty will shift toward it.

In many ways this came true. Today, Americans deal with the national government constantly, through Social Security, the Postal Service, federal highways, and national news. Many people know far more about the president than about their governor. Whether that growing attachment is good or bad is a question Americans still argue about.
:::

## The old league could only use force {orig=5}

One thing, at any rate, must be clear. A government like the one proposed is far more likely to avoid using force than the kind of league most of its opponents argue for, whose authority would act only on the states as political bodies. I have shown that in such a confederacy, the only way to enforce the laws is force. Frequent failures by the states to do their duty are the natural result of the government's very design. And whenever these failures happen, they can only be corrected, if at all, by war and violence.

:::vocab
Confederacy: A loose union of states.
Sanction: A penalty used to make people obey a law.
:::

## State officials will help enforce national laws {orig=6}

The Convention's plan extends the national government's authority to the individual citizens of each state. This will let the national government use each state's ordinary officials to carry out its laws. It's easy to see that this will tend to erase, in people's minds, any difference between laws coming from the state and laws coming from the nation. It will give the national government the same advantage in winning obedience that each state government enjoys. On top of that, public opinion will be shaped by the important fact that the national government can call on the resources of the whole Union for help and support.

This point deserves special attention. The laws of the Union, on the LISTED and PROPER subjects within its power, will become the SUPREME LAW of the land. Every legislative, executive, and judicial officer in each state will be bound by a sacred oath to obey them. In this way, the legislatures, courts, and officials of the states will be brought into the work of the national government AS FAR AS ITS JUST AND CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY EXTENDS. They will become helpers in enforcing its laws.

:::note
Hamilton's footnote says that the misleading argument claiming this arrangement will destroy the state governments "will, in its proper place, be fully detected." He returns to the question of national and state power in [No. 31](/papers/31/) through [No. 33](/papers/33/).
:::

Anyone who thinks through the results of this situation will see good reason to expect the regular and peaceful enforcement of the Union's laws, if its powers are used with ordinary good sense. If we choose to assume the opposite, without any reason, we can draw whatever conclusions we like. After all, it is certainly possible to provoke the people into the wildest excesses through the unwise use of power by the best government that ever was or ever could be created.

But suppose the Constitution's opponents assume that the national leaders won't care about the public good or the duties of their office. I would still ask them this: how would such behavior serve their ambition or their desire to grab power?

:::vocab
Supreme law of the land: The Constitution's phrase, in Article 6, for the Constitution and the national laws and treaties made under it. They win out over any state law that conflicts with them.
Enumerated powers: The specific powers the Constitution lists for Congress, mainly in Article I, Section 8.
Magistrate: A government official who enforces the law.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton is describing Article VI of the Constitution. It makes the Constitution and national laws "the supreme Law of the Land" and says state judges are bound by them. It also requires state legislators and executive and judicial officers to take an oath to support the Constitution. Notice that Hamilton is careful to say this applies only to laws within the national government's "just and constitutional authority." A national law outside those limits would not be supreme.

How far state officials must *help* enforce national law became a major legal question. In *Printz v. United States* (1997), the Supreme Court ruled that Congress could not order local sheriffs to run background checks for gun purchases. The justices disagreed sharply over what Hamilton meant in this passage. The majority read it as saying only that state officials must respect national law. The dissenters read it as suggesting state officers could be required to help carry it out. Today the Court's "anti-commandeering" rule says Congress can't force states to carry out national programs, though state courts must still apply national law.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's idea that people trust a government in proportion to how well it is run is still a basic idea in politics. Polls showing low trust in Congress or the national government often follow scandals, gridlock, or economic trouble. His prediction that familiarity would build loyalty also raises a modern question: as the national government has grown more involved in daily life, has that made Americans more attached to it, or more frustrated with it? And the question of how much state officials must help enforce national law comes up today in debates over immigration, gun laws, and drug laws.
:::
