:::summary
Hamilton begins a group of essays on the power to tax. Money, he says, is "the vital principle of the body politic," the lifeblood that keeps a government alive. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress could ask the states for any amount of money, but it couldn't make them pay, and the result was near collapse. Some critics offered a compromise: let the national government tax imports but leave all other taxes to the states. Hamilton says this would not be enough. Import taxes couldn't cover the debt and the government's ordinary costs, let alone a war. Worse, a government without a full power to tax couldn't borrow, because no one lends to a government that can't be sure of paying them back.
:::

:::keypoints
- The national government must pay for more than the military. It also needs money for **officials' salaries**, the **national debt**, and every other public expense.
- **Money is the lifeblood of government.** Without a steady supply, either the people get plundered or the government slowly dies.
- The Articles gave Congress unlimited power to *request* money, but the states treated the requests as optional.
- The only cure is to let the national government **raise its own revenue** through ordinary taxes.
- Critics wanted to limit the national government to **"external" taxes** (duties on imports). Hamilton says import duties alone could never meet the nation's needs.
- "The necessities of a nation, in every stage of its existence, will be found at least equal to its resources."
- In a war, a government with limited taxing power would have to raid money promised to its lenders. That would **destroy its credit** exactly when it most needed to borrow.
- A full power to tax would let the government **borrow** as much as it needed, because lenders could trust it.
:::

:::context Background
This essay opens the part of the series on taxes, Nos. 30–36. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress had no power to tax at all. It could only send "requisitions," or requests for money, to the states, and the states often paid little or nothing. Congress twice tried to get the states to approve a small national tax on imports, first in 1781 and again in 1783. Both times the plan failed, because changing the Articles required every state to agree. Rhode Island refused the first plan. New York, the state Hamilton is writing to, was the main obstacle to the second.

Hamilton knew these problems firsthand. He served in the Confederation Congress in 1782 and 1783 and pushed hard for the import tax. Two years after writing these essays, he became the first Secretary of the Treasury.
:::

## Taxes must cover more than defense {orig=1}

I have already argued that the national government should have the power to pay for the national forces. By that I meant the cost of raising troops, of building and equipping navies, and every other expense connected in any way with military plans and operations.

But these are not the only things the Union's power over revenue must cover. It must also provide for the national civil list, meaning the salaries of government officials. It must pay the national debts, both those already owed and those that may be taken on in the future. And in general, it must cover every matter that will require spending from the national treasury. The conclusion is that a general power of taxation, in one form or another, must be woven into the structure of the government.

:::vocab
Revenue: Money a government collects, mainly through taxes, to pay for its work.
Civil list: The salaries and expenses of a government's non-military officials.
Treasury: The government's supply of money, or the department that manages it.
:::

## Money is the lifeblood of government {orig=2-3}

Money is rightly seen as the vital principle of the body politic. It is what keeps the nation alive and moving, and what lets it carry out its most essential tasks. So a complete power to get a regular and adequate supply of money, as far as the community's resources allow, may be seen as a necessary part of every constitution.

If a constitution lacks this power, one of two evils must follow. Either the people will be subjected to constant plunder, as a substitute for a better way of meeting public needs. Or the government will sink into a deadly weakness and, before long, die.

Consider the Ottoman, or Turkish, empire. Its ruler is in other ways the absolute master of his subjects' lives and fortunes. Yet he has no right to impose a new tax. As a result, he allows the bashaws, or governors of provinces, to rob the people without mercy. Then, in turn, he squeezes out of the governors the money he needs for his own wants and those of the state.

In America, a similar cause has slowly brought the Union's government into a state of decay that is close to destruction. Who can doubt that the people of both countries would be happier if the proper authorities had enough power to raise the revenue the public's needs require?

:::quote
Money is, with propriety, considered as the vital principle of the body politic; as that which sustains its life and motion, and enables it to perform its most essential functions.

Meaning: Money is to a government what blood is to a body. Without it, nothing else the government is supposed to do can happen.
:::

:::vocab
Body politic: A nation or people thought of as a single living body.
Bashaw: An old English spelling of "pasha," the title of a provincial governor in the Ottoman Empire.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's comparison is meant to shock. Americans saw the Ottoman Empire as the very picture of tyranny. He points out that its ruler, for all his power, couldn't raise taxes in the normal way, so the money got squeezed out of the people by force instead. His point is that a government that can't tax openly and fairly doesn't leave the people better off. Either they get robbed some other way, or the government falls apart.

Hamilton's description of the Ottoman system is simplified, and it is the kind of general picture of a foreign country that writers of his time often used to make a point. The American half of the comparison was real. By 1787, Congress could not even pay the interest on the money it had borrowed during the Revolution.
:::

## Unlimited power to ask, no power to collect {orig=4}

The present Confederation, weak as it is, was meant to give the United States an unlimited power to provide for the Union's money needs. But it was built on a mistaken principle. It went about this in a way that completely defeated its purpose.

As I have said before, the Articles of the Confederation allow Congress to decide on and call for any sums of money that, in its judgment, the United States needs. As long as its requisitions follow the rule for dividing the amount among the states, they are binding on the states in every constitutional sense. The states have no right to question whether a demand is proper. Their only choice is how to raise the sums demanded.

All of this is strictly true. Claiming a right to question these demands would violate the Articles of Union. Hardly any state has ever openly claimed such a right. Yet in practice, the states have constantly acted as if they had it. And they will keep doing so, as long as the Confederacy's revenue must pass through its member states.

Everyone with even a little knowledge of our public affairs knows what this system has led to. Earlier essays have described it at length. More than anything else, it has brought us to a situation that gives us every reason to feel ashamed, and gives our enemies every reason to celebrate.

:::vocab
Requisition: A formal request from Congress to the states for money or soldiers.
Apportionment: Dividing something among the states by a set rule. Under the Articles, each state's share of a requisition was based on the value of its land and buildings.
:::

## The only remedy {orig=5}

What remedy can there be for this situation except a change in the system that produced it? We must change the false and misleading system of quotas and requisitions. This system is like the strange lights over a swamp that lead travelers astray. What could replace it, except allowing the national government to raise its own revenue by the ordinary methods of taxation that every well-ordered government uses?

Clever people can argue convincingly about almost anything. But no human cleverness can point to any other way to rescue us from the troubles and embarrassments that naturally come from an underfunded public treasury.

:::vocab
Ignis fatuus: Latin for "foolish fire." A flickering light seen over swamps at night, also called a will-o'-the-wisp. In old stories, it lured travelers off the path. Hamilton uses it for a hope that leads people astray.
Quota: A fixed share that each state was supposed to contribute.
:::

## Why import taxes alone are not enough {orig=6}

The smarter opponents of the new Constitution accept this reasoning, but only up to a point. They draw a line between what they call INTERNAL and EXTERNAL taxes. They would keep internal taxes for the state governments. External taxes, which they explain as commercial imposts, or duties on imported goods, they are willing to give to the national government.

This line, however, would break the rule of good sense and sound policy that every POWER should be in proportion to its OBJECT, or goal. It would also leave the national government under a kind of guardianship by the state governments, which goes against every idea of strength or effectiveness.

Who can claim that import duties are, or ever would be, enough by themselves for the Union's present and future needs? Consider the existing debt, both foreign and domestic. Suppose it is paid off under any plan that a person who cared even moderately about public justice and public credit could approve. Add the basic services that everyone agrees are necessary. We could not reasonably expect this one source of revenue, even at its very best, to cover even our present needs.

The Union's future needs can't be calculated or limited. So, by the principle I have mentioned more than once, the power to provide for them as they arise should be just as unlimited. I believe the history of mankind supports this rule: IN THE USUAL COURSE OF THINGS, A NATION'S NEEDS, AT EVERY STAGE OF ITS LIFE, WILL TURN OUT TO BE AT LEAST AS GREAT AS ITS RESOURCES.

:::quote
...IN THE USUAL PROGRESS OF THINGS, THE NECESSITIES OF A NATION, IN EVERY STAGE OF ITS EXISTENCE, WILL BE FOUND AT LEAST EQUAL TO ITS RESOURCES.

Meaning: However much money a nation has, it will always find a need for all of it. So it makes no sense to cap what the government can raise.
:::

:::vocab
Impost: A tax on imported goods; a duty.
Duties: Taxes on goods, especially goods coming into or leaving the country.
Internal tax: A tax collected inside the country, such as a tax on land, on property, or on goods made and sold at home.
External tax: A tax on trade with other countries, mainly duties on imports.
Public credit: A government's ability to borrow money, which depends on lenders trusting it to repay.
:::

:::commentary
The line between "internal" and "external" taxes was an old one for Americans. Before the Revolution, some colonists had argued that Parliament could regulate trade with duties but had no right to tax them internally, as the Stamp Act of 1765 did. Some Anti-Federalists now wanted to apply the same line to the new national government. Let it collect duties at the ports, they said, but keep it away from people's farms and homes.

Hamilton's prediction about import duties turned out to be partly wrong in the short run and right in the long run. For most of the 1800s, tariffs and other duties on imports supplied most of the national government's money in peacetime. But in wartime they were never enough. During the Civil War, Congress taxed incomes for the first time. In 1913, the **16th Amendment** gave Congress clear power to tax incomes, and the income tax soon became the government's main source of money.
:::

## Falling back on requisitions won't work {orig=7}

Some say that any shortfall could be made up by requisitions on the states. To say this is to admit, on one hand, that the system can't be trusted. On the other hand, it means trusting that same system for everything beyond a certain point.

Anyone who has carefully studied the system's flaws, as experience has shown them and as these essays have described them, must strongly refuse to trust the national interests to it in any degree. Whenever it is used, it must tend to weaken the Union. It will plant the seeds of conflict between the national government and the states, and among the states themselves.

Can we expect the shortfalls to be paid any better this way than the Union's entire needs have been paid this way until now? Remember that if less is asked of the states, they will also have less means to pay it.

If we took the views of those who argue for this distinction as proof, we would have to believe something strange. We would have to believe that there is some known point in managing a nation's affairs where it is safe to stop and say: "Up to here, meeting the government's needs will promote the public happiness. Anything beyond this point isn't worth our care or worry."

How can a government that is only half supplied and always in need do what it was created to do? How can it provide for security, advance prosperity, or protect the nation's reputation? How can it ever have strength or stability, dignity or credit, trust at home or respect abroad? How can it be anything but a string of quick fixes that are weak, short-term, and shameful? How will it avoid often breaking its promises to meet some urgent need? How can it plan or carry out any generous or far-reaching plan for the public good?

:::commentary
Notice the point about smaller requests: "if less will be required from the States, they will have proportionably less means to answer the demand." The critics' plan would let the national government take the easiest source of money, import duties, and then send requisitions to the states for the rest. But the states would already have lost those import duties. So they would have less money of their own to answer the requests. Hamilton argues the plan would combine the worst of both systems.
:::

## What would happen in a war {orig=8}

Let's think about how this would play out in the very first war we might have to fight. For the sake of argument, suppose the money from import duties is enough to cover the public debt and the Union's peacetime costs. Then a war breaks out. What would the government probably do in such an emergency?

Experience has taught it that it can't depend on requisitions. It has no power of its own to tap new sources of money. And it is pressed by national danger. Wouldn't it be driven to take the funds already set aside for other purposes, like paying lenders, and use them for defense? It's hard to see how such a step could be avoided.

If it were taken, it would clearly destroy the public credit at the very moment when credit was becoming essential to public safety. To imagine that we could do without credit at such a time would be the height of foolishness. In modern warfare, even the wealthiest nations have to take out large loans. A country as far from rich as ours must feel this need even more strongly.

But who would lend to a government that began its request to borrow by doing something that proved it couldn't be relied on to pay? Any loans it could get would be as small in amount as they were harsh in their terms. They would be made the way loan sharks lend to bankrupt and dishonest borrowers: in small amounts and at enormous interest.

:::vocab
Credit: Trust that a borrower will repay. A government with good credit can borrow more easily and at lower interest.
Usurer: Someone who lends money at very high interest; a loan shark.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton understood something many of his readers did not. Wars are usually paid for with borrowed money, not just taxes. And lenders will only lend to a government they trust to pay them back. During the Revolution, Congress printed huge amounts of paper money, called Continentals, that quickly became nearly worthless. The phrase "not worth a Continental" came from that experience.

As Treasury Secretary, Hamilton later put this idea into practice. He convinced Congress to pay the old Revolutionary War debts in full and to have the national government take over the war debts of the states. His goal was to make the United States a borrower that lenders at home and abroad would trust.
:::

## A full power to tax makes borrowing possible {orig=9-10}

Someone might think that, because the country has so few resources, the government would still need to raid its existing funds in a war, even with an unlimited power to tax. But two points should calm any fear about this. First, we can be sure that the community's resources, in their full extent, will be put to work for the Union. Second, any shortfall that remains can easily be covered by loans.

The power to create new funds, by taxing new things on its own authority, would allow the national government to borrow as much as it needed. Then foreigners, as well as Americans, could reasonably trust its promises.

But think about depending on a government that must itself depend on thirteen other governments for the means to keep its contracts. Once its situation was clearly understood, lending to it would take a kind of trust rarely found in money matters. Such trust would hardly fit with the sharp eye that greed usually has for its own interests.

:::commentary
Hamilton turns the critics' worry around. They feared that a full taxing power would let the national government take too much from the people. He argues that the power itself would often mean the government didn't have to use it fully. Lenders would trust a government that *could* raise taxes, so it could borrow in an emergency and spread the cost over many years. A government that couldn't tax, by contrast, would have no credit and no options.
:::

## Facing the world as it is {orig=11}

Thoughts like these may carry little weight with people who hope to see America live out the peaceful golden age of the poets and legends. But some of us believe we are likely to suffer our ordinary share of the ups and downs and disasters that have come to other nations. To us, these thoughts must deserve serious attention. Such people must look at their country's actual situation with painful concern. And they must dread the harm that ambition or revenge might, all too easily, do to it.

:::vocab
Halcyon: Calm and peaceful. "Halcyon days" means a happy, untroubled time.
Vicissitudes: Changes of fortune, especially from good to bad.
:::

:::today
Hamilton's link between taxes and borrowing is still at the heart of how the United States pays its bills. The national government borrows by selling Treasury bonds, and investors around the world buy them because they trust the government to repay. That trust rests on exactly what Hamilton described: a government with the power to raise the money it owes. Today the big debates run the other way from Hamilton's. Many people worry that the national debt has grown too large. Some argue for higher taxes, some for lower spending, and some say the debt is manageable as long as the economy grows. All sides, though, take for granted the power Hamilton was fighting for.
:::
