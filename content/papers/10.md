:::summary
People will always split into competing groups. Madison calls them **factions**. A faction is dangerous when it tries to take away other people's rights or harm the country as a whole. You can't get rid of factions without destroying freedom, so the real question is how to keep them from doing damage. Madison's answer is surprising: make the republic *bigger*. A large republic contains so many different groups that no single one can easily become a majority and oppress everyone else.
:::

:::keypoints
- A **faction** is any group, majority or minority, that acts against other people's rights or the long-term good of the community.
- Factions grow out of human nature, especially differences in opinion and in how much property people own.
- You can't remove the *causes* of faction without destroying liberty. You can only control the *effects*.
- A small faction can be outvoted. The real danger is a faction that makes up a **majority**.
- A **republic**, where voters elect representatives, handles factions better than a direct democracy.
- A **large** republic handles them best, because it contains so many competing interests that it is hard for any one of them to dominate.
:::

:::context Background
Madison wrote this essay in November 1787. Americans were worried about what they saw happening in their state governments. In several states, majorities had passed laws that canceled debts or printed paper money that quickly lost value. These laws hurt people who had lent money. In Massachusetts, armed farmers had rebelled in 1786-87 (Shays' Rebellion). Many people believed the lesson of history was that republics could only survive if they were *small*. Madison set out to prove the opposite.
:::

## The biggest danger to popular government {orig=1}

One of the greatest benefits of a well-built Union is that it can break and control the violence of faction. People who support popular government, meaning government by the people, worry about factions more than anything else. So anyone who cares about popular government should value a plan that offers a cure for this problem without giving up the principles of self-government.

Throughout history, popular governments have died from the same diseases: instability, injustice, and confusion in how they make decisions. The enemies of liberty love to point to these failures as proof that people can't govern themselves.

The American state constitutions have improved on the old models, ancient and modern. But it would be dishonest to claim they have solved this problem. Thoughtful, good citizens everywhere complain that our governments are too unstable. They say the public good is ignored in the fights between rival parties. They say decisions are too often made not by the rules of justice and respect for the rights of the minority, but by the raw power of a self-interested, overbearing majority.

We may wish these complaints were false, but the facts show they are at least partly true. Some of our troubles have been wrongly blamed on our governments. Still, other causes can't explain our worst problems, especially the growing distrust of government promises and the fear for private rights heard across the country. These problems come mainly from the unsteady and unjust way that a spirit of faction has infected our public affairs.

:::vocab
Faction: A group of citizens, either a majority or a minority, united by a shared passion or interest that goes against the rights of others or the good of the whole community.
Popular government: Government by the people, either directly or through elected representatives.
:::

:::commentary
Notice that Madison isn't attacking the people or popular government. He is a *friend* of popular government who is worried about its most famous weakness. His complaint about "an overbearing majority" refers to the debt-relief and paper-money laws passed by state legislatures in the 1780s. To lenders and merchants, these laws were legalized theft. To struggling farmers, they were a lifeline. Keep both views in mind as you read.
:::

## What is a faction? {orig=2}

By a faction, I mean a number of citizens, whether a majority or a minority of the whole, who are united and driven by some shared passion or interest that goes against the rights of other citizens, or against the permanent and overall interests of the community.

:::commentary
This definition is the key to the whole essay, so read it carefully. Two details matter most.

First, a faction can be a **majority**. Most people in 1787 thought of factions as small groups of plotters. Madison's big worry is the opposite: a majority that uses its votes to trample everyone else.

Second, not every group is a faction. A group becomes a faction when its goals go against *other people's rights* or *the long-term good of the whole community*. A neighborhood association or a political party is not automatically a faction. It becomes one when it tries to win at the expense of other people's rights.
:::

## Two ways to deal with factions {orig=3-5}

There are two ways to cure the harm caused by factions. One is to remove their causes. The other is to control their effects.

There are also two ways to remove the causes of faction. The first is to destroy the liberty that factions need to exist. The second is to give every citizen the same opinions, the same passions, and the same interests.

The first cure is truly worse than the disease. Liberty is to faction what air is to fire: fire can't survive without air. But it would be foolish to abolish liberty, which political life needs, just because it feeds faction. That would be like wishing to get rid of air, which every living animal needs, just because air also lets fire burn.

:::quote
Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an aliment without which it instantly expires.

Meaning: Factions need freedom to exist, the way fire needs air. An "aliment" is a kind of food or fuel.
:::

## Why we can't make everyone think alike {orig=6-7}

The second cure is as impossible as the first is unwise. As long as human reason can make mistakes, and people are free to use it, they will form different opinions. And as long as people's reasoning is connected to their self-interest, their opinions and their passions will shape each other.

People also have different abilities, and this is where property rights come from. Protecting those different abilities is the first goal of government. When government protects people's different and unequal abilities to earn property, people end up owning different amounts and kinds of property. That, in turn, affects how they think and feel, and it divides society into different interests and parties.

So the hidden causes of faction are planted in human nature. We see them at work everywhere, more or less active depending on the situation. People have split into parties over religion, over government, and over many other questions, both theoretical and practical. They have split over loyalty to ambitious leaders competing for power, and over other famous people whose fortunes stirred up strong feelings. These divisions have made people angry at each other and much more likely to harass and oppress each other than to work together for the common good.

Humans are so likely to fall into hostility that when there is no real reason to fight, the silliest and most imaginary differences have been enough to spark hatred and violent conflict.

But the most common and lasting source of factions has always been the different and unequal ownership of property. People who own property and people who don't have always had different interests. So have creditors, who lend money, and debtors, who owe it. A farming interest, a manufacturing interest, a trading interest, a banking interest, and many smaller ones grow up naturally in civilized nations. They divide people into classes with different feelings and goals. Regulating all these competing interests is the main job of modern lawmaking. That means party spirit and faction are part of the government's normal, everyday work.

:::vocab
Creditor: A person or business that has lent money and is owed repayment.
Debtor: A person who owes money.
Mercantile interest: The interests of merchants and people in trade. Madison also lists the "landed" (farming), "manufacturing," and "moneyed" (banking and lending) interests.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the most quoted passages in American political thought. Madison is saying something realistic, even a little pessimistic: disagreement is built into human nature. Even if every serious issue disappeared, people would find "frivolous and fanciful" reasons to fight. Think about sports rivalries or online arguments over trivial things.

Madison also points to economic differences as the deepest source of political conflict. In the early 1900s, the historian Charles Beard used this passage to argue that the Constitution was mainly designed to protect the property of the wealthy. Many historians have since challenged Beard's argument, but the debate over how much the founders' economic interests shaped the Constitution continues.
:::

## Why lawmakers can't be fair judges {orig=8-9}

No one is allowed to be the judge in his own case, because self-interest would bias his judgment and might even corrupt his honesty. For the same reason, or an even stronger one, a group of people should not be both judges and parties in the same case.

Yet many of the most important laws are really decisions like this. They don't settle the rights of single people, but they do settle the rights of large groups of citizens. And lawmakers are not neutral judges. They are advocates for the sides they represent.

Is there a proposed law about private debts? Creditors are on one side and debtors are on the other. Justice should balance the two. But the parties themselves are the judges, and the largest party, in other words the most powerful faction, will probably win.

Should we protect American manufacturing by restricting foreign goods? Farmers and manufacturers would answer differently, and probably neither would think only about justice and the public good. Deciding how to divide taxes among different kinds of property seems to demand perfect fairness. Yet maybe no other law gives a ruling party a greater chance and temptation to ignore justice. Every dollar they overcharge the weaker side is a dollar saved for themselves.

Some say that wise leaders will be able to balance these competing interests and make them all serve the public good. But wise leaders will not always be in charge. And in many cases, balancing interests requires thinking about indirect, long-term effects. Those considerations rarely win out over the immediate gain one party sees in ignoring the rights of others or the good of the whole.

:::quote
Enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm.

Meaning: We can't count on wise, good leaders always being in charge, so the system has to work even when they aren't.
:::

:::commentary
This is a central idea of the whole Constitution. Madison doesn't trust that good people will always be in power. He wants a system that works even when leaders are ordinary or bad. He makes the same point in [No. 51](/papers/51/): "If men were angels, no government would be necessary."
:::

## So we must control the effects {orig=10-12}

The conclusion is that the *causes* of faction cannot be removed. Relief can only come from controlling its *effects*.

If a faction is smaller than a majority, the republican principle solves the problem: the majority can simply vote down the faction's bad plans. A minority faction may slow down the government or even shake up society, but it can't carry out its plans while hiding behind the Constitution.

But when a faction includes a majority, popular government lets it sacrifice both the public good and other citizens' rights to its own passion or interest. So our great goal is this: protect the public good and private rights from a majority faction, while still keeping the spirit and form of popular government. This is what would finally rescue popular government from the bad reputation it has carried for so long, and convince the world to admire and adopt it.

How can we do this? There are only two ways. Either we prevent a majority from having the same harmful passion or interest at the same time, or, if a majority does share one, we make it unable to work together and carry out its plans, because of its size and how spread out it is.

If the desire and the opportunity to oppress come together, we know that moral and religious beliefs can't be trusted to stop it. They don't reliably stop individuals from acting unjustly, and they work even less well on large groups. In other words, conscience gets weaker exactly when it is most needed.

:::commentary
Madison is making a tough point about human behavior: people do things in groups that they would be ashamed to do alone. "Everyone else is doing it" weakens individual conscience. That's why he won't rely on virtue alone and looks for a solution built into the structure of government.
:::

## Why a pure democracy fails {orig=13}

From this, we can see that a pure democracy can't cure the harm of factions. By pure democracy, I mean a small society where citizens gather and run the government in person. In almost every case, a majority will share some passion or interest. The form of government makes it easy for them to communicate and coordinate. Nothing holds them back from sacrificing the weaker party or an unpopular individual.

That is why such democracies have always been scenes of chaos and conflict. They have never protected personal safety or property rights well, and they have usually died young and violently. Some theorists have supported this type of government. They wrongly assumed that if people were made perfectly equal in political rights, they would also become equal and alike in their property, opinions, and passions.

:::vocab
Pure democracy: A system where citizens meet and make laws themselves, directly, as in ancient Athens or a New England town meeting.
:::

:::commentary
When Madison criticizes "democracy," he means *direct* democracy, where citizens vote on everything themselves. Today we often use "democracy" to describe any system where people elect their leaders, including the United States. Madison would have called that a "republic." Keep this difference in mind: much of the confusion about whether America is "a democracy or a republic" comes from these shifting meanings.

His claim about ancient democracies was also a matter of debate. Athens, his main example, lasted for almost two centuries and produced great achievements. But a jury of 500 citizens also sentenced the philosopher Socrates to death, and writers in Madison's time often pointed to Athens as an example of mob rule.
:::

## How a republic is different {orig=14-16}

A republic, by which I mean a government that uses representation, offers a different outlook and promises the cure we are looking for. Let's look at how it differs from pure democracy. Then we will understand both the cure and why it works better in a large Union.

There are two big differences between a democracy and a republic. First, in a republic, the people hand over the work of government to a small number of citizens they elect. Second, a republic can stretch over many more citizens and a much larger territory.

The first difference has two possible effects. On the good side, it can refine and enlarge public opinion by passing it through a chosen group of citizens. Their wisdom may help them see the country's true interest, and their patriotism and love of justice will make them less likely to sacrifice it for short-term or narrow goals. Under this system, the public voice expressed by the people's representatives may actually match the public good better than if the people met and spoke for themselves.

On the bad side, the effect could be reversed. People with selfish tempers, local prejudices, or evil plans might win votes through scheming, bribery, or other tricks, and then betray the people. So the question is whether small or large republics are better at electing good guardians of the public good. Two clear reasons show that large republics are better.

:::vocab
Republic: A government in which the people elect representatives to make decisions for them, instead of deciding everything directly.
Representation: The practice of electing a small number of people to act and speak on behalf of many.
:::

:::quote
...to refine and enlarge the public views, by passing them through the medium of a chosen body of citizens...

Meaning: Elected representatives can act as a filter, turning the public's raw opinions into wiser, broader decisions.
:::

## Why bigger republics choose better leaders {orig=17-19}

First, no matter how small a republic is, it needs a certain minimum number of representatives to keep a few people from plotting together. And no matter how large it is, it needs a maximum number to avoid the confusion of a crowd. So the number of representatives doesn't grow in proportion to the population. As a result, a small republic has more representatives per person. If the share of qualified people is the same in both, a large republic will have a bigger pool of good candidates to choose from, and a better chance of a good choice.

Second, in a large republic each representative is chosen by more voters. That makes it harder for bad candidates to win using the dirty tricks that so often decide elections. The people's votes will be freer, and more likely to go to candidates with real merit and a widespread, well-established reputation.

Of course, there is a happy middle here, as in most things. If districts are too large, representatives won't know enough about local conditions and smaller interests. If they are too small, representatives will be too focused on local matters and unable to understand and pursue national goals. The Constitution strikes a good balance: the big, shared national interests go to Congress, and local and particular interests go to the state legislatures.

:::commentary
Madison's argument here is an *expectation*, not a guarantee. Is it harder to fool a big district than a small one? Today, with expensive TV ads and social media, some people argue that big districts actually favor candidates with money and name recognition. Others say Madison was right that big districts make it harder for a single local boss to control an election. This is a good argument to test against what you see in modern elections.
:::

## Why bigger republics protect rights better {orig=20-22}

The other difference is that a republic can include far more citizens and much more territory than a democracy. This is the main reason why factions are less dangerous in a republic.

The smaller the society, the fewer distinct parties and interests it will probably have. The fewer the parties and interests, the more often one party will make up a majority. And the smaller the number of people in that majority, and the smaller the area they live in, the more easily they can plan and carry out oppression.

Expand the territory, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests. You make it less likely that a majority of the whole will share a reason to violate the rights of other citizens. Or, if they do share such a reason, it will be harder for everyone who feels it to discover how strong they are and to act together. There is also another obstacle. When people know their goals are unjust or dishonorable, they are slow to trust one another, and the more people they need to involve, the harder it gets.

So a large republic has the same advantage over a small one that a republic has over a democracy. And the Union has that advantage over the individual states. Does the advantage come from having representatives whose wisdom and good character lift them above local prejudices and unfair schemes? The Union's representatives are most likely to have these qualities. Does it come from greater security because there are so many parties that no single one can outnumber and oppress the rest? The Union, with its greater variety of parties, provides more of this security too. Does it come from the bigger obstacles to the secret plans of an unjust majority? Here again, the size of the Union gives it the clearest advantage.

Leaders who stir up factions may light a fire in their own states, but they won't be able to spread it across the others. A religious group might turn into a political faction in one part of the country. But the many different religious groups across the nation will protect the national government from that danger. A craze for paper money, for canceling debts, for dividing up property equally, or for any other bad or wicked project is less likely to spread through the whole Union than through one state. In the same way, such a disease is more likely to infect a single county than an entire state.

:::quote
Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens...

Meaning: In a big country with many different groups, it's hard for any one group to become a majority big enough to oppress the others.
:::

:::commentary
This was Madison's most original idea. The famous French writer Montesquieu, whom everyone in 1787 read, had argued that republics must be small. Opponents of the Constitution used that idea against it. Madison turned it upside down: a big, diverse republic is *safer* for rights, because it forces groups to build coalitions and compromise.

Notice his list of "improper or wicked" projects: paper money, canceling debts, and dividing property equally. These were mostly causes of poorer, indebted Americans. This shows that Madison was especially worried about protecting property owners from majorities. Whether that worry was wise, or a bias of his class, is still debated.
:::

:::connection
Madison's argument here and in [No. 51](/papers/51/) work as a pair. No. 10 explains how the *size and variety* of the country protect against majority factions. No. 51 explains how the *structure of the government*, including separation of powers and checks and balances, protects against abuse of power by officials.
:::

## The cure {orig=23}

So in the size and proper structure of the Union, we see a republican cure for the diseases that republican governments most often suffer. And the more pleasure and pride we feel in being republicans, the more eagerly we should support the Union and the cause of the Federalists.

:::today
Madison's ideas still shape how Americans think about politics. Political parties, interest groups, and lobbyists are all forms of what he called factions. People still argue about whether his "large republic" solution works. Some say the size and variety of the country has forced coalitions and compromise, just as he predicted. Others say national political parties and the media now let factions organize across the whole country in ways Madison never imagined, which weakens his main argument. Either way, his central question is as alive as ever: how can a government ruled by the majority still protect the rights of the minority?
:::

:::questions
- Madison says a faction can be a majority. Can you think of a time in American history when a majority used its power to violate the rights of a minority?
- Madison thought you could not remove the causes of faction without destroying liberty. Do you agree? Are there any causes of faction that could be reduced without hurting freedom?
- Does social media make it easier or harder for factions to "discover their own strength and act in unison," as Madison put it?
- Is a two-party system a solution to faction or an example of it?
:::
