:::summary
Madison reviews nine more powers of the new government, which he calls the "fourth class" of miscellaneous powers. They include copyrights and patents, a national capital, punishing treason, admitting new states, governing western lands, guaranteeing every state a republican government, honoring the debts of the old government, amending the Constitution, and putting the Constitution into effect once nine states approve it. For each one, he explains why the power is needed and how it is limited. The paper ends with two delicate questions: how nine states can replace the Articles of Confederation when the Articles required every state to agree, and what will happen to states that refuse to join.
:::

:::keypoints
- **Copyrights and patents** serve both creators and the public, and the states can't protect them well on their own.
- The national government needs full control over a **capital district** so it doesn't depend on any one state for protection.
- The Constitution **defines treason** narrowly and bars punishing a traitor's family, because invented treason charges are a favorite weapon of factions.
- Congress can **admit new states**, but no state can be split or merged without its own consent.
- The national government must **guarantee every state a republican government** and protect it from invasion and, when the state asks, from **domestic violence**.
- In real life, **force and right aren't always on the same side**. A minority can sometimes overpower a majority, so outside help can be needed.
- The new government will honor the **old government's debts**.
- **Amendments** are neither too easy nor too hard. Two subjects are protected from change: equal votes in the Senate, and (until 1808) the slave trade clause.
- Approval by **nine states** is enough. Madison argues that the states had already broken the Articles so often that they couldn't insist on its rule of unanimous consent.
:::

:::context Background
This is the third of Madison's papers reviewing the national government's powers. In [No. 41](/papers/41/) he sorted them into six classes. [No. 42](/papers/42/) covered foreign affairs and trade among the states. This paper covers a "miscellaneous" class: important powers that don't fit neatly anywhere else.

When this essay appeared in January 1788, five states had ratified the Constitution: Delaware, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Georgia, and Connecticut. The Constitution needed nine. New York had not yet held its ratifying convention, and many New Yorkers were strongly opposed.
:::

## Copyrights and patents {orig=1-3}

The *fourth* class includes the following miscellaneous powers.

**1. Copyrights and patents.** Congress may

> "promote the progress of science and useful arts, by securing, for a limited time, to authors and inventors, the exclusive right to their respective writings and discoveries."

Hardly anyone will question how useful this power is. In Great Britain, courts have formally ruled that an author's copyright is a right under the common law. The right to useful inventions seems, for equally good reasons, to belong to the inventors. In both cases, the public good fully matches what individuals claim. The states can't make effective rules for either case on their own. Most of them have already settled this point by passing laws at the request of Congress.

:::vocab
Copyright: The legal right of an author or artist to control copies of his or her work for a set time.
Patent: The legal right of an inventor to control the use and sale of an invention for a set time.
Common law: Law based on court decisions and custom built up over centuries, rather than on written statutes. American law grew out of English common law.
:::

:::commentary
This clause ties the rights of creators to a public purpose: to "promote the progress of science and useful arts." The deal is simple. Creators get control of their work "for a limited time," and then it becomes free for everyone to use. Congress passed the first national copyright and patent laws in 1790. The first copyright law protected a work for 14 years, with one chance to renew for 14 more.

Madison's claim about British law was simpler than the truth. British courts had disagreed about it, and in 1774 the House of Lords ruled that copyright lasted only as long as Parliament's law allowed. Today, the length of copyright is a lively debate. Most works are now protected for the author's life plus 70 years. Some people say that is far too long for a "limited time." Others say it is a fair reward for creative work.
:::

## A national capital and federal property {orig=4-6}

**2. A seat of government and federal property.** Congress may

> "exercise exclusive legislation, in all cases whatsoever, over such district (not exceeding ten miles square) as may, by cession of particular States and the acceptance of Congress, become the seat of the government of the United States; and to exercise like authority over all places purchased by the consent of the legislatures of the States in which the same shall be, for the erection of forts, magazines, arsenals, dockyards, and other needful buildings."

In other words, Congress will fully govern a district of up to ten miles by ten miles that a state gives up for the national capital. It will have the same power over forts, storehouses for weapons and gunpowder, shipyards, and other needed buildings, on land bought with the consent of the state.

The need for complete authority at the seat of government is obvious. Every legislature in the Union, and I could say in the world, has this power as a result of its general supremacy. Without it, people could insult the public authority and interrupt its work without being punished. Worse, members of the national government would depend on the state containing the capital to protect them while they did their duties. Then people might accuse the national government of acting out of fear of that state, or under its influence. That would be dishonorable to the government and would upset the other members of the Confederacy.

This point carries even more weight because public buildings and improvements will slowly pile up at the government's permanent home. They would be too valuable a public pledge to leave in the hands of a single state. And they would make it so hard for the government to move that it would lose even more of its needed independence.

The federal district is small enough to calm every fear on the other side. And consider the following:

- It will be set aside for this use only with the consent of the state that gives it up.
- That state will surely protect the rights and consent of the citizens living there in its agreement.
- The people living there will have good reasons of self-interest to agree to the transfer.
- They will have had their say in electing the government that will rule them.
- They will of course be allowed a local legislature, elected by themselves, for local matters.
- The authority of the state legislature, and of the people of the area, to agree to the transfer will come from the whole people of the state when they adopt the Constitution.

So every objection we can imagine seems to be answered.

The need for the same authority over forts, weapons storehouses, and so on, set up by the national government, is just as clear. The public money spent on such places, and the public property kept in them, require that they be free from the authority of any one state. And it wouldn't be proper for places that the security of the whole Union may depend on to be dependent in any way on a single member of it. Here too, all objections are answered by requiring the consent of the states involved for every such place.

:::vocab
Cession: The act of formally giving up land or rights to someone else. A state that gives up land "cedes" it.
Magazine: A building for storing weapons and gunpowder.
Arsenal: A place where weapons are made or stored.
Municipal: Having to do with a city or local government.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's readers would have remembered a real embarrassment. In 1783, unpaid soldiers surrounded the building where Congress was meeting in Philadelphia and demanded their pay. Pennsylvania's government refused to call out its militia to protect Congress, so Congress left the city and moved to Princeton, New Jersey. A capital under national control was meant to prevent anything like that from happening again.

The district became Washington, D.C., carved from land given by Maryland and Virginia (Virginia's part was later returned). The government moved there in 1800. Madison's promises to its residents were only partly kept. They were never given voting members in Congress, and they could not vote for president until the 23rd Amendment (1961). Congress allowed them an elected local government in 1973. Whether D.C. should become a state, or get voting representation in Congress, is still debated.
:::

## A careful definition of treason {orig=7-8}

**3. Treason.** Congress may

> "declare the punishment of treason, but no attainder of treason shall work corruption of blood, or forfeiture, except during the life of the person attainted."

Since treason can be committed against the United States, the United States should have the authority to punish it. But newly invented, artificial kinds of treason have been the main weapons that violent factions, which free governments naturally produce, have used to take turns attacking each other. So the Convention wisely built a barrier against this particular danger. It wrote a definition of the crime into the Constitution. It set the proof needed to convict someone of it. And it kept Congress, even when punishing treason, from extending the penalties of guilt beyond the guilty person himself.

:::vocab
Treason: Betraying one's country. The Constitution defines it as making war against the United States, or helping its enemies.
Attainder: In old English law, the loss of all rights and property that followed a sentence for a serious crime like treason.
Corruption of blood: An old English punishment that stopped a convicted traitor's children and family from inheriting his property or titles.
Forfeiture: The loss of property as a punishment.
:::

:::commentary
Article 3, section 3 defines treason as only "levying war" against the United States or "adhering to their enemies, giving them aid and comfort." A conviction requires two witnesses to the same act, or a confession in open court. These rules made treason hard to prove on purpose. In England, kings and rival parties had often used vague treason charges to destroy their opponents and their families. Hamilton discusses the treason clause too, in [No. 84](/papers/84/).

The strict rules have mattered in practice. In 1807, former Vice President Aaron Burr was tried for treason and acquitted, partly because the prosecution could not meet the Constitution's demanding standard of proof.
:::

## New states and western lands {orig=9-12}

**4. New states.** Congress may

> "admit new States into the Union; but no new State shall be formed or erected within the jurisdiction of any other State; nor any State be formed by the junction of two or more States, or parts of States, without the consent of the legislatures of the States concerned, as well as of the Congress."

The Articles of Confederation have nothing about this important subject. Under the Articles, Canada could join as a right, if it joined in the measures of the United States. The other *colonies*, which clearly meant the other British colonies, could join if nine states agreed. The writers of the Articles seem to have overlooked the creation of *new states* in the future. We have seen the problems this omission has caused, and the power Congress has been led to take because of it. So the new system very properly fills the gap.

The general rule is that no new state may be formed without the consent of both the national government and the states involved. That fits the principles that ought to govern such matters. The specific rule against forming new states by splitting up a state without its consent calms the fears of the larger states. The fears of the smaller states are calmed by a matching rule against merging states without their consent.

**5. Territory and property of the United States.** Congress may

> "dispose of and make all needful rules and regulations respecting the territory or other property belonging to the United States," with a proviso, that "nothing in the Constitution shall be so construed as to prejudice any claims of the United States, or of any particular State."

This is a very important power. It is needed for reasons similar to those that support the power to admit new states. The added proviso, which protects existing claims, is proper in itself. It was probably made absolutely necessary by the jealousies and disputes over the western territory, which the public knows well.

:::vocab
Proviso: A condition or exception attached to a rule.
Jurisdiction: The area of authority of a government or court.
:::

:::commentary
After the Revolution, several states, including Virginia, New York, and Massachusetts, claimed huge areas of land west of the Appalachian Mountains. Smaller states without such claims, especially Maryland, feared the big states would grow too powerful. Maryland refused to approve the Articles of Confederation until the big states agreed to give their western claims to Congress. Most of these lands were handed over in the 1780s.

The "power Congress has been led to take" refers to how the old Congress governed these western lands anyway, most famously through the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. That law set rules for turning territories into new states and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory. But the Articles never clearly gave Congress the power to do any of this. The new Constitution did.

The consent rules have been tested. Maine was split from Massachusetts in 1820 with Massachusetts' approval. During the Civil War, West Virginia broke away from Virginia in 1863. The "consent" came from a pro-Union government that claimed to speak for Virginia, not from the government in Richmond that had joined the Confederacy. That is why West Virginia's creation is still debated.
:::

## Guaranteeing a republican government {orig=13-14}

**6. Guarantees to the states.** The United States shall

> "guarantee to every State in the Union a republican form of government; to protect each of them against invasion; and on application of the legislature, or of the executive (when the legislature cannot be convened), against domestic violence."

In a confederacy founded on republican principles and made up of republics, the overall government clearly ought to have the authority to defend the system against changes toward aristocracy or monarchy. The closer the union, the more each member cares about the political institutions of the others. And the more right each has to insist that the forms of government under which they made their agreement be kept, at least in *substance*. But a right implies a remedy. And where else could the remedy be placed than where the Constitution places it?

Governments built on different principles and forms have proved less suited to any kind of federal union than governments that are alike. "As the confederate republic of Germany," says Montesquieu, "consists of free cities and petty states, subject to different princes, experience shows us that it is more imperfect than that of Holland and Switzerland." "Greece was undone," he adds, "as soon as the king of Macedon obtained a seat among the Amphictyons," the council of the ancient Greek league. In that last case, no doubt the new member's far greater strength, as well as its monarchy, played a part in what happened.

Someone may ask why such a precaution is needed. Someone may also ask whether it could become an excuse for changing the state governments without the states' consent. Both questions are easy to answer.

If the national government never needs to step in, this provision will just be a harmless extra in the Constitution. But who can say what experiments may come from the whims of particular states, the ambition of bold leaders, or the plots and influence of foreign powers?

As for the second question, if the national government steps in under this power, it will of course be bound to stay within the power. And the power goes no further than a *guarantee* of a republican form of government. A guarantee assumes that a government of that form already exists. So as long as the states keep their existing republican forms, the federal Constitution guarantees them. Whenever the states choose to replace them with other republican forms, they have the right to do so, and to claim the federal guarantee for the new ones. The only limit on them is that they may not trade a republican constitution for an anti-republican one. I assume hardly anyone will see that as a hardship.

:::vocab
Republic: A government in which the people elect representatives to make decisions for them, instead of deciding everything directly.
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged upper class, often based on birth.
Monarchy: Government by a king or queen.
Montesquieu: A French writer (1689–1755) whose book *The Spirit of the Laws* was widely read in America. He argued for separating the powers of government.
Amphictyons: Members of the Amphictyonic Council, a league of ancient Greek states.
:::

:::commentary
This is called the **guarantee clause**. The king of Macedon Madison mentions was Philip II, the father of Alexander the Great. After Philip won a place in the Greek council, Macedon soon came to dominate the Greek city-states.

The clause has rarely been enforced by the courts. In *Luther v. Borden* (1849), which grew out of a fight between two rival governments in Rhode Island, the Supreme Court said that deciding which state government is truly "republican" is a question for Congress and the president, not judges. So the clause has mostly stayed in the background, as Madison hoped, a "harmless superfluity" unless a real crisis came.
:::

## Protection from invasion and violence {orig=15-17}

Every society owes protection against invasion to the parts that make it up. The broad wording used here seems to protect each state not only from foreign enemies, but also from the ambitious or vengeful schemes of its more powerful neighbors. The history of both ancient and modern confederacies proves that the weaker members of a union should pay close attention to the wisdom of this article.

Protection against domestic violence is added just as properly. It has been noted that even the Swiss cantons, which are not strictly under one government, provide for this. The history of that league tells us that the cantons often ask for and give each other help, the most democratic cantons as well as the others. And a recent, well-known event here at home has warned us to be ready for emergencies of the same kind.

At first glance, it might not seem to fit republican theory to suppose either that a majority lacks the right to overturn a government, or that a minority will have the force to do it. On that theory, the national government would only ever be asked to step in when stepping in would be wrong. But in this case, as in most others, theory must be adjusted by the lessons of practice.

Why couldn't a majority of a state, especially a small state, form an illegal group for violent purposes, just as a majority of a county or a district within a state might? And if the state's authority should protect local officials in the second case, shouldn't the national authority support the state's authority in the first? Besides, some parts of the state constitutions are so woven together with the federal Constitution that a violent blow to one can't help wounding the other.

An uprising in a state will rarely lead the national government to step in, unless the number of rebels is fairly large compared with the friends of the government. In such cases, it is much better for the overall government to put down the violence than to leave the majority to defend its cause in a bloody, drawn-out fight. And the existence of a right to step in will usually prevent the need to use it.

:::commentary
The "recent, well-known event" is Shays' Rebellion. In 1786 and 1787, armed farmers in western Massachusetts, deep in debt and facing the loss of their farms, shut down courts to stop debt cases. The Confederation Congress could do almost nothing to help. Massachusetts had to raise its own army, partly paid for by wealthy private citizens. Many leaders saw the rebellion as proof that the Articles were too weak.

Note the limit in the clause: the national government protects against domestic violence only when the state legislature, or the governor if the legislature can't meet, asks for help. That kept the national government from using this clause as an excuse to march into a state uninvited.
:::

## When force and right are on different sides {orig=18-21}

Is it true that in republican governments, force and right must always be on the same side? Couldn't the smaller party have so much more money, so much more military skill and experience, or so much secret help from foreign powers, that it would also win in a fight? Couldn't a tighter, better position tip the balance its way, against a larger number of people who are too spread out to gather their strength quickly? Nothing is more fanciful than to think that in a real test of force, victory can be predicted by counting people in a census, or by the rules that decide an election!

In short, couldn't a minority of *citizens* become a majority of *persons*? This could happen if it were joined by foreign residents, by a chance crowd of adventurers, or by people whom the state constitution has not given the right to vote. I say nothing of an unhappy class of people found in large numbers in some states. During the calm of regular government, they are pushed down below the level of men. But in the storms of civil violence, they may rise up into their human character and give greater strength to whatever side they join.

Where it is doubtful which side is in the right, what better referees could two violent factions want, as they take up arms and tear a state apart, than representatives of the other states, who are not heated by the local fire? They would combine the fairness of judges with the goodwill of friends. How happy it would be if all free governments could enjoy such a remedy for their weaknesses! How happy if an equally effective plan could be set up for the peace of all mankind!

Someone might ask what remedy there could be for a rebellion that spread through *all* the states and had a greater share of the whole country's force, though not a constitutional right. The answer is that such a case would be beyond the reach of human remedies. Fortunately, it is also beyond the reach of human likelihood. It is reason enough to support the federal Constitution that it reduces the risk of a disaster that no constitution could ever cure.

Among the advantages of a confederate republic that Montesquieu lists, an important one is "that should a popular insurrection happen in one of the States, the others are able to quell it. Should abuses creep into one part, they are reformed by those that remain sound."

:::vocab
Insurrection: A violent uprising against a government.
Suffrage: The right to vote.
Chimerical: Imaginary; wildly unrealistic.
Confederate republic: A union of several republics joined under a common government.
:::

:::commentary
The "unhappy class of people" Madison refers to are **enslaved people**. He is saying that in normal times, the law treats them as less than human, but in a civil war they might rise up and fight, and their numbers could decide the outcome. His words show that he knew slavery denied the humanity of enslaved people. They also show a fear common among white Southerners: that enslaved people would revolt. The promise of national help against "domestic violence" could also mean help in putting down a slave revolt.

Madison's list of people who can't vote, such as foreign residents and others "whom the constitution of the State has not admitted to the rights of suffrage," is also a reminder of who was left out in 1788. Women, enslaved people, and in most states men without enough property could not vote. Madison's point is that a group's strength in a fight doesn't match its share of voters.

His claim that a rebellion across *all* the states was beyond "human probability" was tested in a different form. In 1860 and 1861, eleven Southern states tried to leave the Union, starting the Civil War, the very kind of disaster he said no constitution could cure.
:::

## Honoring the old government's debts {orig=22-24}

**7. Old debts.** The new government is

> "to consider all debts contracted, and engagements entered into, before the adoption of this Constitution, as being no less valid against the United States, under this Constitution, than under the Confederation."

This can only be seen as a statement of something already true. It may have been added, among other reasons, to reassure the foreign creditors of the United States. They surely know the false theory that changing a society's form of government magically cancels its moral duties.

One of the smaller criticisms of the Constitution is that it should have declared these agreements binding *in favor of* the United States, as well as *against* it. In the spirit typical of petty critics, this omission has been twisted and blown up into a plot against the nation's rights. The people who made this discovery can be told what few others need to be told. Agreements by their nature bind both sides. So declaring them valid on one side necessarily makes them valid on the other. And since the article only states an existing principle, stating it in one case is enough for every case.

They can also be told that every constitution must limit its precautions to dangers that are not completely imaginary. There is no real danger that the government would *dare*, with or without this statement in the Constitution, to forgive debts that are justly owed to the public, on the excuse condemned here.

:::vocab
Creditor: A person or business that has lent money and is owed repayment.
Declaratory: Stating something that is already true or already the law, rather than creating something new.
:::

:::commentary
The United States had borrowed heavily to win the Revolution, from France, from Dutch bankers, and from its own citizens. Under the Articles, Congress couldn't raise taxes to repay these debts, and it fell far behind on payments. This clause, in Article 6, promised that switching governments wouldn't be an excuse to skip out on them.

The promise was kept. In 1790, Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton persuaded Congress to pay the Confederation's debts in full, and to take over the states' war debts as well. His plan was controversial, and Madison, by then a member of Congress, opposed parts of it. But it established the nation's credit.
:::

## Amending the Constitution {orig=25-26}

**8. Amendments.** The Constitution provides "for amendments to be ratified by three fourths of the States under two exceptions only."

Everyone could foresee that experience would suggest useful changes. So it was necessary to provide a way to make them. The method the Convention chose seems right in every way. It guards equally against two extremes. Amending can't be so easy that the Constitution would change too often. And it can't be so hard that the Constitution's discovered faults would last forever. The method also lets both the national government and the state governments start the process of correcting errors, as experience on either side points them out.

The exception protecting the equal vote of each state in the Senate was probably meant as a shield for the remaining sovereignty of the states. That sovereignty is implied and secured by equal representation in one house of Congress. The exception was probably demanded by the states most attached to that equality. The other exception must have been accepted for the same reasons that produced the privilege it protects.

:::vocab
Amendment: A formal change or addition to a constitution.
Ratify: To formally approve.
Palladium: A safeguard or protection. The word comes from a statue of the goddess Pallas Athena that was believed to protect the city of Troy.
Residuary sovereignty: The authority left over for the states after the national government's listed powers are taken out. The 10th Amendment later put this idea into words.
:::

:::commentary
Article 5 gives two ways to propose an amendment: by a two-thirds vote of both houses of Congress, or by a national convention called at the request of two-thirds of the states. Either way, three-fourths of the states must then approve it. Every one of the 27 amendments so far has been proposed by Congress. The convention method has never been used.

Madison doesn't name the "other exception," but readers should know what it was. Article 5 barred any amendment before 1808 to the clause protecting the **slave trade** (see [No. 42](/papers/42/)), and to a related clause on direct taxes. "The privilege defended by it" was the right of states to keep importing enslaved Africans for twenty years. The Senate exception is still in force: no state can lose its equal vote in the Senate without its own consent.

Madison's claim that the process is neither too easy nor too hard is still debated. Supporters say it has allowed major changes, like ending slavery and giving women the vote, while protecting the Constitution from passing fads. Critics say it is now so hard to amend the Constitution that big questions end up being decided by courts instead of by the people.
:::

## Ratification by nine states {orig=27-31}

**9. Ratification.** "The ratification of the conventions of nine States shall be sufficient for the establishment of this Constitution between the States, ratifying the same."

This article speaks for itself. Only the direct authority of the people could give the Constitution proper legitimacy. Requiring all thirteen states to ratify would have put the essential interests of the whole country at the mercy of the whims or corruption of a single state. It would have shown a lack of foresight in the Convention that our own experience would have made inexcusable.

Two very delicate questions come up here:

1. The Confederation has the solemn form of an agreement among the states. On what principle can it be replaced without the unanimous consent of the states that made it?
2. What will the relationship be between the nine or more states that ratify the Constitution, and the few remaining states that do not join?

The first question is answered at once by turning to the absolute necessity of the case. It is answered by the great principle of self-preservation. It is answered by the highest law of nature and of nature's God, which declares that the safety and happiness of society are the goals of all political institutions, and that all such institutions must be sacrificed to those goals.

*Perhaps* an answer can also be found without looking beyond the principles of the agreement itself. As earlier papers noted, one defect of the Confederation is that in many states it was approved only by the legislature, not by the people. Fairness seems to require that its hold on the other states be reduced to the same level. An agreement between independent sovereign states, based on ordinary acts of their legislatures, can claim no higher force than a league or treaty between them.

It is an established rule about treaties that all the articles are conditions of each other. Breaking any one article breaks the whole treaty. And a breach by any party frees the others, and entitles them, if they wish, to declare the agreement broken and void. Suppose it unfortunately becomes necessary to appeal to these delicate truths, to justify ending the federal agreement without the consent of certain states. Won't the states that complain find it hard to answer the *many* and *serious* violations they themselves may be charged with? There was a time when we all had a duty to keep these ideas hidden. The situation has now changed, and with it the role that the same motives call on us to play.

The second question is just as delicate. Since we can happily hope it will stay only hypothetical, it would be unwise to discuss it too closely. It is one of those cases that must be left to take care of itself. In general, though, we can say this. Although no political relationship can exist between the states that agree and those that refuse, their moral relationships will not be canceled. The claims of justice on both sides will still be in force and must be fulfilled. The rights of humanity must always be properly respected by each side. And we can hope that shared interests, and above all the memory of the dear times we have shared, and the hope of soon overcoming the obstacles to reunion, will not plead in vain for *moderation* on one side and *prudence* on the other.

:::quote
...the transcendent law of nature and of nature's God, which declares that the safety and happiness of society are the objects at which all political institutions aim, and to which all such institutions must be sacrificed.

Meaning: Governments exist to keep people safe and happy. When a government fails at that, the people may replace it. Madison is echoing the Declaration of Independence, which speaks of "the laws of nature and of nature's God."
:::

:::vocab
Compact: A formal agreement between two or more parties.
Ratifying convention: A special meeting of delegates elected by the people of a state to decide whether to approve the Constitution.
Sovereign: Holding supreme, independent political authority.
:::

:::commentary
This is one of the boldest arguments in the Federalist Papers. Article 13 of the Articles of Confederation said they could only be changed if *every* state agreed. The Constitution ignored that rule and said nine states were enough. Critics said this was illegal. Madison gives two answers. First, the people's safety is a higher law than any agreement, the same argument Americans had used against Britain in 1776. Second, the states had broken the Articles so often, mainly by refusing to pay what Congress asked, that the agreement was already broken.

His second question was not just hypothetical. New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify in June 1788, so the Constitution took effect. But North Carolina did not ratify until late 1789, and Rhode Island not until 1790, after the new government was already running. For a time, they were outside the Union. Madison's gentle closing words, hoping for "moderation" and "prudence," show that the Federalists wanted to win over the holdouts, not punish them.
:::

:::today
Several of the powers in this paper are at the center of debates today. People argue about how long copyrights should last in a digital world. Residents of Washington, D.C., still have no voting member of Congress, and some want statehood. And many people believe the amendment process is so hard that the Constitution can no longer change through the method Madison praised. Other people say that difficulty is exactly what keeps the Constitution stable. Madison's argument about ratification also raises a lasting question: when is it right to break the rules of an old agreement to create a better one?
:::
