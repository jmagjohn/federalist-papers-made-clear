:::summary
Madison begins with a surprising fact from history. When ancient peoples created governments on purpose, they almost always handed the job to **one wise lawgiver**, like Solon of Athens or Lycurgus of Sparta, instead of a group. America improved on this by using an elected convention, but the history shows how hard and risky such projects are. Madison then compares America to a **sick patient** who called in trusted doctors. They all agreed on a cure, but now a crowd of critics says the medicine is poison, even though the critics **can't agree with each other** on what is wrong or what to do instead. Most of their objections apply ten times more strongly to the **Articles of Confederation**. In fact, the weak old Congress has already had to take powers it was never given.
:::

:::keypoints
- In ancient times, governments made by deliberate choice were usually designed by **a single lawgiver**, not an assembly.
- The Greeks, who were very protective of their liberty, still trusted one man. They must have feared **disagreement in a group** more than betrayal by one person.
- Solon and Lycurgus faced great **difficulties**. This shows how risky it is to keep redesigning a government.
- The Constitution's mistakes, if any, will probably come from **lack of experience**, and only a real trial will reveal them.
- America is like a **patient** whose chosen doctors all agree on a cure. Critics call the cure poison, but they **disagree with each other** on almost every point.
- A second convention made up of these critics would be full of **conflict**.
- A plan doesn't need to be perfect. It only needs to be **better than the one it replaces**.
- Most objections to the Constitution apply even more strongly to the **Articles**, including the **slave trade**, which the Constitution allows Congress to stop after twenty years.
- Congress has already taken over the **western lands** and begun forming new states, with no constitutional authority at all.
:::

:::context Background
This essay continues the introduction Madison began in [No. 37](/papers/37/), before he starts examining the Constitution part by part. By January 1788, when this essay appeared, opponents of the Constitution had published a flood of objections. They came from people in different states with very different interests and views, and they often contradicted each other. Madison uses those contradictions as evidence that the critics had no better plan.

At the time, some critics were calling for a **second convention** to write a new plan. Madison argues that such a convention would only produce endless conflict.
:::

## The ancient lawgivers {orig=1-3}

It is quite remarkable that ancient history reports the same thing in every case where a government was created by careful planning and consent. The job of designing it was not given to an assembly. It was done by a single citizen of outstanding wisdom and proven honesty.

We learn that:

- **Minos** was the first founder of the government of Crete, and **Zaleucus** of the government of the Locrians, a Greek people in southern Italy.
- **Theseus** first, and after him **Draco** and **Solon**, set up the government of Athens.
- **Lycurgus** was the lawgiver of Sparta.
- The foundation of Rome's first government was laid by **Romulus**. Two of his elected successors, **Numa** and **Tullius Hostilius**, completed the work.
- When Rome got rid of its kings, **Brutus** replaced them with a government led by consuls. He came forward with a plan for the reform, which he said had been prepared by Tullius Hostilius. His skill won the approval of the senate and the people.

The same is true of confederate governments. **Amphictyon**, we are told, created the league that carried his name. The Achaean League was first founded by **Achaeus**, and founded again by **Aratus**.

We can't always know how much these famous lawgivers really did, or how far they had the lawful authority of the people. But in some cases, the process was completely proper. The people of Athens seem to have given Draco unlimited powers to reform their government and laws. And according to Plutarch, Solon was more or less forced by the votes of all his fellow citizens to take sole and total power to remake the constitution. The process under Lycurgus was less regular. But even there, as far as the supporters of an orderly reform won out, they all looked to the efforts of that one famous patriot and wise man. They did not try to bring about change through an assembly of citizens.

:::vocab
Lawgiver: A person who creates a nation's basic laws or system of government.
Antiquity: The ancient past, especially ancient Greece and Rome.
Sparta: A powerful city-state in ancient Greece, famous for its military.
Consul: In ancient Rome, one of two top officials elected each year after Rome got rid of its kings.
Amphictyons: Members of the Amphictyonic Council, a league of ancient Greek states.
Achaean League: An alliance of Greek city-states (about 280–146 BC) that was eventually conquered by Rome.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's list mixes real historical figures with legends. **Minos**, **Theseus**, **Romulus**, **Amphictyon**, and **Achaeus** come from myths and stories, and most historians doubt they were real people. Lycurgus may be legendary too. **Draco** and **Solon** were real Athenian lawmakers of the late 600s and early 500s BC. Draco's laws were so harsh that we still use the word "draconian" for very severe rules. Solon is remembered for canceling many debts and freeing Athenians who had been enslaved for debt. **Aratus** was a real general who rebuilt the Achaean League in the 200s BC.

Madison's "Tullius Hostilius" seems to mix up two different Roman kings: **Tullus Hostilius** and **Servius Tullius**. Roman tradition gave credit for organizing the government, and for the plan Brutus used, mainly to Servius Tullius. **Plutarch**, whom Madison cites, was a Greek writer of the first and second centuries AD whose biographies of famous Greeks and Romans were widely read in 1787.
:::

## Why trust one man? {orig=4}

The Greeks guarded their liberty jealously. So why would they give up all caution and put their future in the hands of one citizen? Consider the Athenians in particular. They wouldn't let an army be commanded by fewer than ten generals. They needed no proof that a citizen threatened their liberties other than his great reputation. So why would they think one famous citizen was a better keeper of their own fortunes and their children's than a chosen group of citizens? A group's shared discussions could have been expected to bring more wisdom, and more safety too.

We can't fully answer these questions unless we suppose that they feared disagreement and division among many advisers more than they feared betrayal or incompetence in one person.

History also tells us about the difficulties these famous reformers had to struggle with, and the methods they had to use to put their reforms into effect. Solon seems to have been more willing to bend to circumstances. He admitted that he had not given his countrymen the government best suited to their happiness, but the one their prejudices could best put up with. Lycurgus stuck more firmly to his goal. But he had to mix some violence with the authority of religious superstition. And he made his success final by giving up, first his country, and then his life.

On one hand, these lessons should make us admire how America has improved on the ancient way of preparing and setting up plans of government. On the other hand, they also warn us about the risks and difficulties of such experiments. They show how unwise it would be to repeat them without need.

:::vocab
Posterity: Future generations.
Superstition: Here, religious belief and ritual, especially beliefs Madison considered unfounded.
Ostracism: Being shut out from a group or from society. In ancient Athens, it meant being banished from the city by a public vote.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's line about Athenians fearing "the illustrious merit of a fellow-citizen" refers to **ostracism**. Each year, Athenians could vote to banish a citizen for ten years, sometimes simply because he had become too famous and powerful. Athens also had ten elected generals, partly so no single commander could grow too strong.

The story about Lycurgus comes from Plutarch. Lycurgus supposedly made the Spartans swear to keep his laws until he returned from a trip to the oracle at Delphi. Then he never came back, and by some accounts he starved himself to death so they would be bound by their oath forever. Madison returns to this story later in the essay.
:::

## Mistakes that only experience can show {orig=5}

Is it unreasonable to guess that any errors in the Convention's plan come from a lack of earlier experience with this complicated and difficult subject, rather than from careless study of it? If so, those errors won't be discovered until an actual trial points them out.

This guess is made likely by many general reasons. It is also supported by the particular case of the Articles of Confederation. When the Articles were sent to the states to be ratified, the states suggested many objections and amendments. Yet not one of them points to the great and basic error that the actual trial has revealed. And except for the comments New Jersey made, which came more from her location than from any special foresight, it's doubtful that a single suggestion was important enough to justify changing the system.

Still, there is plenty of reason to think that, unimportant as these objections were, some states would have clung to them with dangerous stubbornness. That would have happened if their devotion to their own opinions and supposed interests had not been overpowered by a stronger feeling, the need for self-preservation. We may remember that one state refused for several years to agree to the Articles, even though the enemy remained at our gates the whole time, or rather deep inside our country. And she gave in at last only for a strong reason: the fear of being blamed for dragging out the public suffering and endangering the outcome of the war. Every fair-minded reader will draw the right conclusions from these important facts.

:::vocab
Ratify: To formally approve.
Candid: Fair and open-minded. (In 1787, "candid" meant fair in judging others, not just frank.)
:::

:::commentary
The state that held out was **Maryland**. Several states, especially Virginia, claimed huge western lands that stretched toward the Mississippi River. Maryland had no such claims. It refused to approve the Articles until the states with western claims agreed to give them to the national government. Maryland finally ratified in early 1781, and the Articles took effect. By then the war had been going on for nearly six years, and British armies were still fighting in the South.

The "great and radical error" Madison means is the one Hamilton described in [No. 15](/papers/15/): Congress could only make requests of the states, not laws for individuals. Madison's point is that no one saw this flaw in 1777–1781. It became clear only through experience. So the new Constitution's flaws, too, may only show up in practice, and the amendment process can fix them.
:::

## The sick patient {orig=6}

Imagine a patient who finds his illness getting worse every day. He sees that he can't put off a strong remedy any longer without great danger. After thinking calmly about his situation and about the qualities of different doctors, he chooses and calls in those he thinks most able to help him and most worthy of his trust.

The doctors arrive. They examine the patient carefully and consult with each other. They all agree that the symptoms are serious. But they also agree that the case is far from hopeless. With proper and timely treatment, it may even end with his health being better than before. They are just as united in prescribing the remedy that will produce this happy result.

But as soon as the prescription is made known, a number of people step in. They don't deny that the illness is real or dangerous. But they assure the patient that the prescription will poison his constitution, and they forbid him to take it, on pain of certain death.

Before following their advice, couldn't the patient reasonably demand that they at least agree among themselves on some other remedy? And if he found that they disagreed as much with each other as with his first advisers, wouldn't it be wise to try the treatment his doctors all recommended? Wouldn't that be better than listening to people who couldn't deny he needed a quick remedy, and yet couldn't agree on one?

:::commentary
Madison's comparison has a clever double meaning. A patient's "constitution" is his body's basic health. A nation's "constitution" is its basic system of government. When the critics say the remedy "will be poison to his constitution," Madison is playing on both meanings.

The analogy is persuasive, but notice how it frames the debate. The Convention delegates are the expert doctors, chosen by the patient. The critics are outsiders who just "interpose," or butt in. An Anti-Federalist might answer that the Convention went beyond its instructions, which were only to revise the Articles, and that some of the "critics" were themselves delegates, like George Mason, who refused to sign.
:::

## The critics can't agree {orig=7}

America is exactly such a patient in exactly this situation. She has felt her illness. She has received regular and unanimous advice from men she chose carefully. And others are warning her not to follow this advice, or she will suffer the most fatal results.

Do these critics deny that her danger is real? No. Do they deny that she needs a quick and strong remedy? No. Do they agree, do even two of them agree, on what is wrong with the proposed remedy, or on what should replace it? Let them speak for themselves.

- One says the Constitution should be rejected because it is not a confederation of the states, but a government over individuals.
- Another admits it should be a government over individuals to some degree, but not nearly as far as proposed.
- A third doesn't object to a government over individuals, or to its extent, but to the lack of a bill of rights.
- A fourth agrees a bill of rights is absolutely necessary, but says it should declare the rights kept by the *states* as political bodies, not the personal rights of individuals.
- A fifth thinks a bill of rights of any kind would be useless and out of place. He says the plan would be fine except for the deadly power to regulate the times and places of elections.

Here are more:

- An objector in a large state protests loudly against the unfair equal representation of the states in the Senate. An objector in a small state protests just as loudly against the dangerous unequal representation in the House.
- From one direction, we are warned about the huge expense of all the people needed to run the new government. From another direction, and sometimes from the same one at a different time, the cry is that Congress will be only a shadow of representation, and the government would be much better if the number of members, and the expense, were doubled.
- A patriot in a state that doesn't import or export much sees unbeatable objections to the power of direct taxation. His fellow critic in a state with large imports and exports is just as unhappy that the whole tax burden might be placed on goods people buy.
- One politician finds in the Constitution a direct and unstoppable tendency toward monarchy. Another is equally sure it will end in aristocracy. Another can't say which of these forms it will finally take, but clearly sees it must be one or the other. Meanwhile, a fourth declares just as confidently that the Constitution leans so little toward either danger that it won't have enough weight on that side to stay upright and steady against the opposite tendencies.

Another group of critics says the legislative, executive, and judicial branches are mixed together in a way that goes against every idea of orderly government and every needed protection for liberty. As long as this objection is stated in vague, general terms, a few people support it. But ask each of them to explain exactly what he means, and hardly any two agree.

- To one, the worst part of the design is that the Senate joins the President in the important job of appointing officials, instead of giving this executive power to the President alone.
- To another, the worst part is that the House of Representatives is left out. He says only its large size could guard against corruption and favoritism in using such a power.
- To another, letting the President have any share at all in a power that must always be a dangerous tool in the hands of an executive is an unforgivable violation of the rules of republican caution.
- Some say nothing in the plan is less acceptable than having the Senate try impeachments. They point out that the Senate is part of both the legislative and executive branches, when this power clearly belongs to the judicial branch. "We fully agree with your objection to this part of the plan," others reply. "But we can never agree that giving impeachments to the judges would fix the mistake. Our main complaint about the design is that the judicial branch already has too much power."

Even among the eager supporters of a council of state, to advise the President, there is total disagreement about how it should be set up. One gentleman demands that the council be small and be appointed by the larger branch of the legislature. Another would prefer a larger council, and thinks it is essential that the President himself appoint it.

:::vocab
Bill of rights: A formal list of rights that the government may not violate, such as freedom of speech or the right to a jury trial.
Monarchy: Government by a king or queen.
Aristocracy: Government by a small, privileged upper class, often based on birth.
Impeachment: The process of formally charging a government official with serious wrongdoing, which can lead to removal from office.
Direct tax: A tax charged directly on people or property, such as a tax on land or a head tax, rather than on goods that are bought and sold.
Council of state: A small group of advisers to the executive. Some critics wanted one to share the President's powers and watch over him.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's list is a fair sample of real Anti-Federalist arguments. The critics truly did disagree on many points. Some wanted to keep a league of states. Others accepted a strong national government but wanted it limited. **George Mason** of Virginia, one of the delegates who refused to sign, began his list of objections with the lack of a bill of rights, and he also wanted a council to advise the President. Many critics worried about Congress's power over "the Times, Places and Manner" of elections (Article I, Section 4), fearing it could be used to manipulate them.

But Madison's argument has a weakness. Critics disagreeing with each other doesn't prove that any one of their objections is wrong. And one demand did unite many of them: a **bill of rights**. In the end, the Federalists gave in on this. Madison himself wrote the amendments that became the **Bill of Rights**, and they were ratified in 1791.
:::

## What would a second convention do? {orig=8}

The writers against the Constitution shouldn't mind if we suppose the following. As they are the most eager of those who think the Convention was not up to its task, let's suppose they are also the wisest. Let's suppose that a wiser and better plan could and should replace the Convention's.

Let's further suppose that the country agrees, both with their high opinion of themselves and with their low opinion of the Convention. So it makes them into a second convention, with full powers, for the specific purpose of revising and remaking the first one's work.

Suppose this experiment were seriously tried, though it takes some effort to take it seriously even as make-believe. I leave it to be judged from the sample of opinions I just showed. With all their hostility to the delegates before them, would they really differ from them in any way, except in the conflict and turmoil of their own meetings?

And suppose the Constitution now before the public were adopted at once and stayed in force, not until a BETTER one, but until ANOTHER one was agreed on by this new assembly of lawgivers. Wouldn't it have as good a chance of lasting forever as the one Lycurgus gave Sparta? He made any change in it depend on his own return from exile and death.

:::commentary
Madison's joke at the end is sharp. Lycurgus's laws could never be changed, because they could only be changed when he came back, and he was dead. Madison says the critics would agree on a new plan about as soon as Lycurgus would return from the grave.

The idea of a second convention was a real proposal. New York's ratifying convention, for example, sent a letter in 1788 calling for a new convention to consider amendments. Federalists like Madison feared that a second convention might undo the whole plan. Instead, the first Congress proposed amendments itself, in 1789.
:::

## The old system is far worse {orig=9}

It is both surprising and sad that those who raise so many objections against the new Constitution never seem to remember the defects of the one it would replace. The new one doesn't have to be perfect. It's enough that the old one is *less* perfect.

No one would refuse to trade brass for silver or gold just because the silver or gold had some impurity in it. No one would refuse to move out of a cracked and shaky house into a solid and comfortable one just because the new one had no porch, or because some rooms were a little bigger or smaller, or the ceilings a little higher or lower, than he would have liked.

But let's set aside comparisons like these. Isn't it obvious that most of the main objections to the new system apply ten times more strongly to the current Confederation?

- **Is an unlimited power to raise money dangerous in the hands of the national government?** The current Congress can make requisitions for any amount it likes, and the states are constitutionally bound to pay them. It can print paper money as long as it can pay for the paper. It can borrow, at home and abroad, as long as anyone will lend a shilling.
- **Is an unlimited power to raise troops dangerous?** The Confederation gives Congress that power too, and it has already begun to use it.
- **Is it wrong and unsafe to mix the different powers of government in one body of men?** Congress, a single body, holds all the federal powers.
- **Is it especially dangerous to put the keys to the treasury and command of the army in the same hands?** The Confederation puts both in the hands of Congress.
- **Is a bill of rights essential to liberty?** The Confederation has no bill of rights.
- **Is it wrong that the new Constitution lets the Senate, together with the President, make treaties that will be the law of the land?** The current Congress, with no such check, can make treaties. Congress itself has declared them to be the supreme law of the land, and most of the states have agreed.
- **Does the new Constitution allow the importing of slaves for twenty years?** The old one allows it forever.

:::quote
It is not necessary that the former should be perfect; it is sufficient that the latter is more imperfect.

Meaning: The Constitution doesn't have to be flawless to be worth adopting. It only has to be better than the Articles it would replace.
:::

:::vocab
Alloy: A mixture of metals. Here, a cheaper metal mixed into gold or silver, which lowers its purity.
Bills of credit: Paper money issued by a government as a promise to pay later.
Requisition: A formal request. Under the Articles, Congress asked the states for money and soldiers but couldn't make them provide either.
Slave trade: The capture of people in Africa and their transport across the Atlantic to be sold into slavery. It is also called the Atlantic or transatlantic slave trade.
:::

:::commentary
**Paper money:** During the Revolution, Congress printed huge amounts of paper money, called "Continentals." Their value collapsed, giving rise to the saying "not worth a Continental."

**The slave trade:** Article I, Section 9 of the Constitution said Congress could not ban "the Migration or Importation" of enslaved people before 1808. Madison's claim that the Articles allowed the trade "forever" is true in one sense: Congress under the Articles had no power to ban it. But it leaves out that individual states could ban the trade themselves, under both the Articles and the Constitution, and many had already done so. The twenty-year clause was a concession to South Carolina and Georgia, whose delegates insisted on it. At the Convention, Madison himself had objected that twenty years would do all the harm that could be feared from the trade. Congress did ban the importing of enslaved people as soon as it was allowed, starting January 1, 1808. But slavery itself, and the buying and selling of enslaved people *within* the United States, continued and grew until the Civil War. Madison, like many of the founders, was himself a slaveholder. He discusses the clause again in [No. 42](/papers/42/).
:::

## Congress has already overstepped {orig=10-11}

I will be told that however dangerous this mix of powers may be in theory, it is made harmless because Congress depends on the states for the means to use them. I'll be told that however large the pile of powers may be, it is really a lifeless pile.

Then I say, first, that the Confederation is guilty of the even greater foolishness of declaring certain powers absolutely necessary for the national government, while making them absolutely useless. Second, if the Union is to continue and no better government replaces this one, real powers must either be given to the current Congress or taken by it. Either way, the contrast I just described will still hold.

But that's not all. Out of this lifeless pile, an unnatural new power has already grown. It tends to bring about all the dangers that anyone could fear from a badly designed national government.

It is no longer just a guess or a hope that the Western territory is a mine of great wealth for the United States. It isn't the kind of wealth that can rescue us from our present troubles, or provide regular money for public expenses for some time. But with proper management, it should later be able to pay off the domestic debt little by little, and to send generous sums to the national treasury for a certain period.

The individual states have already handed over a very large part of this land. And we can reasonably expect the remaining states to stop holding back similar proof of their fairness and generosity. So we can count on a rich and fertile region, as large as the settled part of the United States, soon becoming national property.

Congress has taken over the management of this property. It has begun to make it pay. And Congress has gone further. It has begun to form new states, set up temporary governments, appoint officials for them, and set the terms on which those states will be admitted into the Confederacy. All this has been done, and done without the slightest hint of constitutional authority. Yet no one has whispered any blame. No one has sounded an alarm.

A GREAT and INDEPENDENT source of money is passing into the hands of a SINGLE BODY of men. This body can RAISE TROOPS in UNLIMITED NUMBERS, and spend money to support them for an UNLIMITED TIME. And yet some men have not only watched this quietly, but actually support the system that allows it. At the same time, they raise against the new system the objections we have heard. Wouldn't it be more consistent for them to support the new system? It is needed just as much to protect the Union from the future powers and resources of a body built like the current Congress, as to save it from the dangers caused by that body's present weakness.

I don't mean, by anything I have said, to blame Congress for what it has done. I know it couldn't have done otherwise. The public interest and the needs of the situation forced it to leap over its constitutional limits. But isn't this an alarming proof of the danger of a government that doesn't have regular powers equal to its tasks? Such a government always faces a terrible choice: the Union falls apart, or the government seizes powers it was never given.

:::quote
A dissolution or usurpation is the dreadful dilemma to which it is continually exposed.

Meaning: A government too weak for its job will either collapse or be forced to grab powers it doesn't legally have. Either result is dangerous.
:::

:::vocab
Cession: The act of giving up land or rights. The states' "cessions" handed their western land claims to Congress.
Domestic debt: Money a government owes to its own citizens.
Usurpation: Seizing power that one has no right to.
Nugatory: Having no force or effect; useless.
:::

:::commentary
Madison is describing the **Northwest Ordinance**, passed by Congress in July 1787 while the Constitutional Convention was meeting in Philadelphia. It set up a government for the lands north of the Ohio River and laid out how new states would be formed there and admitted to the Union as equals. It also banned slavery in that territory. Earlier laws, such as the Land Ordinance of 1785, arranged for the land to be surveyed and sold. Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin, and part of Minnesota were later carved out of this region.

Most historians consider the Northwest Ordinance one of the great achievements of the Confederation Congress. But Madison is right that the Articles gave Congress no clear power to do it. The Constitution fixed this in Article IV, Section 3, which lets Congress govern territories and admit new states.

These lands were not empty. They were home to many Native nations, including the Shawnee, Miami, and Delaware, many of whom had never agreed to give them up. Madison's "mine of vast wealth" was a source of land for white settlers, and the years that followed brought a long series of wars over it.
:::

:::today
Madison's main argument, that a plan should be judged against the real alternative rather than against perfection, still applies to almost every big public decision. Critics of a new law or policy often point out its flaws, and they are often right. But Madison would ask: compared to what? Is the current system better? And do the critics agree on anything better? On the other hand, critics today might reply that pointing out problems is still valuable, even without a full alternative, and that the Anti-Federalists' objections did lead to the Bill of Rights.
:::
