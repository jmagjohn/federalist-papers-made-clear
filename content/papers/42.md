:::summary
Madison continues his review of the national government's powers. First he covers powers over dealings with foreign nations: making treaties, sending and receiving ambassadors and consuls, punishing piracy and crimes at sea, and regulating foreign trade. That last power includes a clause that bars Congress from banning the import of enslaved Africans until 1808. Madison wishes the ban could have come sooner, but he calls the clause a gain for humanity. In fact, it protected the slave trade for twenty more years. He then covers powers that keep peace among the states: regulating trade between the states and with Native nations, coining money, punishing counterfeiting, setting weights and measures, making uniform rules for citizenship and bankruptcy, recognizing other states' official records, and running post offices.
:::

:::keypoints
- If America is one nation in any respect, it should be one nation in dealing with **other nations**.
- The Constitution fixes gaps in the Articles of Confederation on **treaties**, **ambassadors**, and **consuls**. Even small gaps matter, because they tempt the government to take powers it wasn't given.
- Congress can define and punish **piracy**, **felonies at sea**, and **offenses against international law**, so that no single state can drag the country into a conflict.
- The Constitution barred Congress from banning the **import of enslaved people before 1808**. Madison wished it had been sooner but called the clause a gain for humanity. It let the trade continue legally for **twenty years**.
- Without power over **trade between the states**, states would tax goods passing through them, causing anger and possibly conflict.
- The Constitution clears up confusing limits in the Articles on **trade with Native nations**.
- **Uniform rules** for money, weights and measures, citizenship, and bankruptcy prevent confusion, fraud, and one state's laws overriding another's.
- **Post offices and post roads** are a harmless power that can make life much easier.
:::

:::context Background
This is the second of Madison's papers reviewing the powers of the new government. In [No. 41](/papers/41/), he sorted those powers into six classes and covered the first, security against foreign danger. Here he covers the second class (dealing with foreign nations) and the third (keeping harmony among the states).

Much of this paper compares the Constitution to the **Articles of Confederation**, the country's first constitution. Under the Articles, Congress could make treaties but couldn't control trade, and each state set its own trade taxes and citizenship rules. Madison was a Virginian and a slaveholder. At the Convention, the slave trade was one of the bitterest issues. Delegates from South Carolina and Georgia said their states would not accept a Constitution that let Congress end the trade right away.
:::

## Dealing with foreign nations {orig=1-2}

The *second* class of powers given to the national government covers dealings with foreign nations. These powers are:

- to make treaties;
- to send and receive ambassadors, other public ministers, and consuls;
- to define and punish piracies and felonies committed on the high seas, and offenses against the law of nations;
- to regulate foreign trade, including a power to ban the importation of slaves after the year 1808, and to set a tax of up to ten dollars per person in the meantime, to discourage such imports.

This class of powers is an obvious and essential part of the national government's job. If we are to be one nation in any respect, it clearly ought to be in our dealings with other nations.

:::quote
If we are to be one nation in any respect, it clearly ought to be in respect to other nations.

Meaning: Whatever else the states keep for themselves, the country must speak to the rest of the world with one voice.
:::

:::vocab
Consul: An official sent to a foreign city to help his own country's merchants and citizens there. A consul has a lower rank than an ambassador.
Ambassador: The highest-ranking official sent by one government to represent it in another country.
Law of nations: The rules that nations were expected to follow in dealing with each other. Today we call this international law.
High seas: The open ocean, outside the control of any one country.
:::

## Treaties, ambassadors, and consuls {orig=3-4}

The powers to make treaties and to send and receive ambassadors explain themselves. Both are in the Articles of Confederation, with only two differences.

First, the Convention's plan removes an exception in the Articles that let state regulations largely defeat a treaty. Second, it expressly and wisely adds a power to appoint and receive "other public ministers and consuls" to the old provision about ambassadors. If the word *ambassador* is read strictly, as the Articles seem to require, it covers only the highest rank of public ministers. It leaves out the lower ranks, which the United States will most likely prefer when it needs to send officials abroad. And no stretch of the word could include consuls. Yet Congress has found it useful to use lower-ranking ministers and to send and receive consuls, and it has done so.

It's true that some trade treaties call for each country to appoint consuls, whose work is connected with trade. In those cases, accepting foreign consuls may fall under the power to make trade treaties. And where there is no such treaty, sending American consuls abroad *might* be covered by the Articles' power to appoint the civil officers needed to manage the general affairs of the United States. But nothing in the Articles seems to allow foreign consuls into the United States when no treaty has already provided for it.

Filling this gap is one of the smaller ways the Convention has improved on the old model. But even the smallest provisions become important when they remove the need, or the excuse, for slow and unnoticed grabs of power. Many people who haven't paid attention would be quite surprised by a list of the times the Confederation's defects have tricked or forced Congress into breaking the limits of its own charter. That list would be a strong argument for the new Constitution. The new plan seems to have fixed the smaller defects of the old one as carefully as the large and obvious ones.

:::commentary
This may seem like a lot of fuss over job titles. But Madison's real point comes at the end. When a government lacks a power it truly needs, it usually takes the power anyway, quietly. Each time it does, it gets more used to ignoring its own rules. Madison made the same argument in [No. 41](/papers/41/): a constitution should give the government the powers it will actually need, so that it never has an excuse to break the rules.
:::

## Piracy and crimes at sea {orig=5-6}

The power to define and punish piracies and felonies on the high seas, and offenses against the law of nations, also belongs properly to the national government. It is an even bigger improvement on the Articles of Confederation.

The Articles say nothing about offenses against the law of nations. So they leave any careless state free to drag the whole Confederacy into trouble with foreign nations. And on piracies and felonies, the Articles only go as far as setting up courts to try these crimes.

Defining piracy could perhaps be left to the law of nations without causing problems, though most countries define it in their own laws. But a definition of felonies at sea is clearly needed. *Felony* is a loose term, even in the common law of England, and it means different things in different English statutes. Still, neither the common law nor the statutes of England, or of any other nation, should guide our own courts unless our own lawmakers first adopt them. Using English law would be dishonorable and improper. Using the states' definitions would be just as unworkable. The meaning of felony is not exactly the same in any two states, and it changes in each one whenever it revises its criminal laws. So for the sake of certainty and uniformity, this power to define felonies at sea was necessary and proper in every way.

The regulation of foreign trade has come up so often already in these papers that we don't need more proof that it belongs to the national government.

:::vocab
Felony: A serious crime, usually one punished more harshly than a minor offense.
Common law: Law based on court decisions and custom built up over centuries, rather than on written statutes. American law grew out of English common law.
Statute: A written law passed by a legislature.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's worry was practical. If an American attacked a foreign ship or insulted a foreign ambassador, the foreign country would blame the United States as a whole. But under the Articles, only the states could punish such acts, and a state might not bother. In 1784, a Frenchman attacked a French diplomat in Philadelphia, and Congress had no power to punish him itself. The case caused real embarrassment. Under the Constitution, Congress could make such acts national crimes.
:::

## The slave trade clause {orig=7-8}

No doubt it would have been better if the power to ban the importation of slaves had not been put off until 1808. It would have been better still if it could have taken effect right away. But it is not hard to explain why this limit was put on the national government, or why the whole clause is worded the way it is.

It should be seen as a great gain for humanity that within twenty years, this trade may end forever within these states. The trade has long and loudly exposed the barbarism of modern policy. During those twenty years, the national government will discourage it a good deal. And the trade may be abolished completely if the few states that still continue this unnatural traffic follow the example of the great majority of the Union, which have banned it. How happy the unfortunate Africans would be if they had an equal hope of being rescued from the oppression of their European brothers!

Some have tried to twist this clause into an objection against the Constitution. On one side, they call it a criminal tolerance of a wicked practice. On the other, they claim it is meant to stop free and useful immigration from Europe to America. I mention these false readings not to answer them, since they deserve no answer. I mention them as examples of the manner and spirit in which some people have chosen to carry on their opposition to the proposed government.

:::commentary
Here is what the clause actually said. Article 1, section 9 stated that "the migration or importation of such persons as any of the states now existing shall think proper to admit, shall not be prohibited by the Congress prior to the year one thousand eight hundred and eight." It also allowed a tax of up to ten dollars on each person imported. The Constitution never used the word "slave," but everyone knew what "such persons" meant. Article 5 went further: it barred any amendment to this clause before 1808.

So the clause did not end the slave trade. It **protected** the trade from national interference for twenty years. During those years, enslaved Africans continued to be brought into the country legally. South Carolina, which had paused imports, reopened the trade in 1803, and large numbers of captives were brought in before the deadline. The clause also didn't require a ban in 1808. It only allowed one. Congress did pass a ban, signed by President Jefferson in 1807, that took effect on January 1, 1808. But smuggling continued, and slavery itself grew. The trade in enslaved people *within* the United States, from older states to newer ones in the South and West, expanded greatly in the following decades.

Madison calls the clause "a great point gained in favor of humanity," and he condemns the trade in strong words. But he defends a compromise that kept it going, and he calls critics who saw it as "a criminal toleration" unworthy of an answer. Some opponents of the Constitution did object for exactly that reason. Defenders of the compromise argue that without it, the Deep South would not have joined the Union and no national ban would have been possible. Critics reply that the founders chose national unity over the freedom of the people being bought and sold. Both views are worth weighing.
:::

:::vocab
Slave trade: The capture of people in Africa and their transport across the Atlantic to be sold into slavery. It is also called the Atlantic or transatlantic slave trade.
:::

## Keeping peace among the states {orig=9-10}

The powers in the *third* class are the ones that keep harmony and proper dealings among the states.

The limits placed on the states, and certain powers of the courts, could be included under this heading. But the limits on the states are saved for a separate class. The courts will be examined in detail when we reach the structure and organization of the government. So I will limit myself to a quick review of the remaining powers in this third class. They are:

- to regulate trade among the states and with the Indian tribes;
- to coin money and regulate its value, and the value of foreign coins;
- to provide for punishing the counterfeiting of the coins and securities of the United States;
- to set the standard of weights and measures;
- to set a uniform rule of naturalization, and uniform laws of bankruptcy;
- to set the way the public acts, records, and court proceedings of each state shall be proved, and the effect they shall have in other states;
- to establish post offices and post roads.

:::commentary
The "limits placed on the states" are covered in [No. 44](/papers/44/). The courts are covered in Hamilton's Nos. 78–83, especially [No. 78](/papers/78/).
:::

## Trade between the states {orig=11-12}

Experience has clearly shown that one defect of the present Confederacy is its lack of power to regulate trade among its members. Earlier papers have given proofs and comments on this subject. I can add that without this extra power, the great and essential power to regulate *foreign* trade would have been incomplete and useless.

A very important purpose of this power is to protect states that import and export through other states from unfair charges by those states. If states were free to regulate trade with each other, we can be sure they would find ways to tax imports and exports passing through their territory. These taxes would fall on the people who make the exports and the people who buy the imports.

Past experience tells us that future schemes would bring in this practice. Both experience and common knowledge of human affairs tell us that it would breed endless hatred, and would quite likely end in serious breaks in the public peace. To anyone not looking at the question through passion or self-interest, the trading states' desire to collect hidden taxes from their non-trading neighbors must seem as unwise as it is unfair. It would push the injured state, out of anger as well as interest, to find other, less convenient routes for its foreign trade. But the mild voice of reason, pleading for a broad and lasting interest, is too often drowned out by loud, impatient greed for quick and excessive gain. That is true of public bodies as well as individuals.

Other confederacies besides ours have shown the need for an overall authority over trade among their members. In Switzerland, where the union is very loose, each canton must let goods pass through its territory to other cantons without raising tolls. In Germany, it is a law of the empire that the princes and states may not set tolls or customs on bridges, rivers, or passages without the consent of the emperor and the Diet. But as a quotation in an earlier paper showed, practice in Germany, as in many other matters there, has not followed the law. It has produced there the very harms we foresee here. And among the limits the union of the Netherlands places on its members is that they may not set taxes that hurt their neighbors without general permission.

:::vocab
Canton: One of the small, self-governing states that made up Switzerland.
Diet: The assembly of the German Empire, where its princes and cities were represented.
Commerce clause: The part of Article 1, section 8 that gives Congress power to regulate trade with foreign nations, among the states, and with the Indian tribes.
:::

:::commentary
In the 1780s, states with big ports, like New York, collected taxes on imported goods. Much of what came through New York's port was then sold in neighboring states like New Jersey and Connecticut. So the people of those states were paying New York's taxes without any say in them. This kind of trade conflict was one of the main reasons the Constitutional Convention was called.

The power to regulate "commerce among the several states" has become one of the most important powers Congress has. In *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824), the Supreme Court read it broadly and struck down a New York steamboat monopoly. In the 1900s, Congress used it to pass laws on wages, workplace safety, and civil rights. People still argue about how far it reaches. Some say it should cover only actual buying, selling, and shipping between states. Others say it covers any activity that has a large effect on the national economy.
:::

## Trade with Native nations {orig=13}

The Constitution wisely frees the power to regulate trade with the Indian tribes from two limits in the Articles of Confederation. Those limits make the provision unclear and self-contradictory.

The Articles limit the power to Indians who are not members of any state. And they say it must not violate or infringe a state's right to make laws within its own borders. Which Indians should count as members of a state has never been settled. It has often caused confusion and dispute in Congress. And how trade with Indians who are not members of a state, but who live within its borders, can be regulated by an outside authority without intruding on the state's right to make its own laws is completely impossible to understand.

This is not the only case where the Articles of Confederation have thoughtlessly tried to do the impossible. They try to combine partial sovereignty in the Union with complete sovereignty in the states. They try to overturn a law of mathematics by taking away a part and leaving the whole.

:::vocab
Sovereignty: Supreme, independent political authority.
Axiom: A basic truth that everyone accepts without needing proof.
:::

:::commentary
Madison's joke at the end is pointed: you can't take a piece away from something and still have the whole thing. Under the Articles, the states claimed full sovereignty while also giving some of it to Congress. Madison says that was a contradiction from the start.

The Constitution's commerce clause lists "the Indian tribes" alongside foreign nations and the states. This reflected the fact that Native nations governed themselves and made treaties with the United States. But the treatment of Native peoples by the national and state governments was often unjust. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832), the Supreme Court ruled that Georgia's laws had no force inside Cherokee territory. Yet under the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the Cherokee and other nations were still forced from their homelands in the 1830s, and thousands died on the journey known as the Trail of Tears. Notice that Madison's concern here is about which government controls trade, not about the rights of Native peoples themselves.
:::

## Money, counterfeiting, and weights and measures {orig=14-16}

All that needs to be said about the power to coin money, and to regulate its value and that of foreign coins, is this. By including foreign coins, the Constitution fills an important gap in the Articles of Confederation. The present Congress can regulate only coins *made* by its own authority or by the states. It is easy to see that the uniform *value* of our money could be destroyed if each state set its own rules for the value of foreign coins.

The power to punish counterfeiting of public securities, as well as of coins, naturally goes to the same authority that is to protect the value of both.

The power to regulate weights and measures is carried over from the Articles of Confederation. It rests on the same reasons as the power to regulate coins.

:::vocab
Counterfeiting: Making fake money or fake official papers and passing them off as real.
Securities: Official papers, such as government bonds, that promise to repay money.
:::

:::commentary
In 1788, Americans used a confusing mix of money, including coins from Britain, France, Portugal, and especially Spain. The Spanish silver dollar was so common that the American dollar was later based on it. If each state had valued these coins differently, a dollar in one state might be worth more or less than a dollar in the next. Congress set up the U.S. Mint in 1792.
:::

## Uniform rules for citizenship {orig=17}

Differences among the states' rules for naturalization have long been seen as a fault in our system. They create complicated and delicate legal questions.

Article 4 of the Confederation says "that the FREE INHABITANTS of each of these States, paupers, vagabonds, and fugitives from justice, excepted, shall be entitled to all privileges and immunities of FREE CITIZENS in the several States; and THE PEOPLE of each State shall, in every other, enjoy all the privileges of trade and commerce." The wording here is remarkably confused. Why does it say *free inhabitants* in one part, *free citizens* in another, and *people* in another? And what is meant by adding "all the privileges of trade and commerce" to "all privileges and immunities of free citizens"? It is hard to say.

But one reading seems almost unavoidable. People who count as *free inhabitants* of one state, even if they are not citizens of it, get all the privileges of *free citizens* in every other state. That means they get more rights elsewhere than they have in their own state. So each state is forced to give the rights of citizenship in other states not only to people it makes its own citizens, but to anyone it allows to live within its borders.

Suppose instead we read "inhabitants" to mean citizens only. That only shrinks the problem without removing it. Each state would still keep the very improper power to naturalize foreigners in every other state. In one state, living there a short time gives a person all the rights of citizenship. In another, more serious requirements must be met. So a foreigner who is legally barred from certain rights in the second state can escape that bar just by living first in the first state. In this way, one state's law would absurdly become supreme over another state's law, inside the other state's own territory.

It is pure luck that we have avoided very serious trouble on this point so far. The laws of several states placed bans on certain kinds of foreigners who had made themselves hated. These bans went against not only the rights of citizenship but even the right to live there. What if such people had become citizens under another state's laws, by living there or otherwise, and then claimed the right to live in, and be citizens of, the state that had banned them? Whatever the legal result might have been, other results would likely have followed that were too serious not to guard against. So the new Constitution very properly guards against them, and against every other problem caused by this defect in the Confederation. It lets the national government set a uniform rule of naturalization throughout the United States.

:::vocab
Naturalization: The legal process by which a person born in another country becomes a citizen.
Alien: A person living in a country who is not a citizen of it.
Privileges and immunities: The basic rights and protections that come with being a citizen.
:::

:::commentary
The "foreigners who had made themselves hated" were probably people tied to the British side in the Revolution. Several states passed harsh laws against Loyalists after the war.

Congress passed the first national naturalization law in 1790. It allowed only "free white persons" to become naturalized citizens. That racial limit, later loosened for people of African descent after the Civil War, lasted in some form until 1952. The Constitution also didn't define who was a citizen by birth. In the *Dred Scott* decision (1857), the Supreme Court said Black Americans could not be citizens. The 14th Amendment (1868) reversed this by making everyone born in the United States a citizen.
:::

## Bankruptcy, state records, and post offices {orig=18-20}

The power to set uniform bankruptcy laws is closely tied to the regulation of trade. It will also prevent many frauds when the people involved, or their property, are in different states or are moved into different states. So it seems unlikely that anyone will question whether it is wise.

The power to set, by general laws, how the public acts, records, and court proceedings of each state shall be proved, and what effect they shall have in other states, is an obvious and valuable improvement on the clause about this in the Articles of Confederation. The meaning of the old clause is very unclear, and it can have little importance however you read it. The new power can become a very useful tool of justice. It will be especially helpful near the borders between states, where property that a court might seize can be moved quickly and secretly into another state's territory at any stage of a lawsuit.

The power to establish post roads must be harmless from every point of view. With wise management, it may also become very convenient for the public. Nothing that makes it easier for the states to communicate with each other can be considered unworthy of the public's care.

:::vocab
Bankruptcy: A legal process for a person or business that can't pay its debts. It decides how what they own is divided among the people they owe.
Post roads: Roads used for carrying the mail.
Full faith and credit clause: Article 4, section 1 of the Constitution. It requires each state to respect the public acts, records, and court decisions of the other states.
:::

:::commentary
The records clause is called the **full faith and credit clause**. It means, for example, that a court judgment won in one state can be enforced in another. A debtor can't escape by moving across a state line.

Madison's guess that the post office might become "productive of great public conveniency" proved true. In the early republic, the post office was the national government's largest civilian operation, and newspapers sent through the mail helped tie a spread-out country together.
:::

:::today
Many of the powers in this paper are so basic today that we rarely think about them: one national currency, one set of citizenship rules, mail that crosses state lines, and court rulings that count in every state. Two of them are still at the center of major debates. The **commerce clause** is one of the main sources of Congress's power, and courts still argue about its limits. And the **slave trade clause** is a key example in a larger debate about the Constitution and slavery. Some people see the founders' compromises as a betrayal of the Declaration's promise that "all men are created equal." Others see them as a painful price for creating a Union that would later abolish slavery. Madison's own words show both the founders' discomfort with slavery and their willingness to protect it.
:::
