:::summary
Publius continues his history lesson with three confederacies that still existed in 1787. The first is the **Germanic body**, the Holy Roman Empire. On paper its assembly, the Diet, and its emperor had broad powers. But because the empire was "a community of sovereigns," its laws were addressed to princes and cities, not people, and its history was one long story of wars, ignored requests for troops and money, and foreign meddling. **Poland**, a government over local lords, was so weak that its neighbors had just taken a third of it. The **Swiss cantons** hardly formed a confederacy at all. They stayed together mainly because they were small and afraid of their neighbors, and religious quarrels had split them into rival camps with opposite foreign allies. Every case supports the same principle: a government that acts on member states instead of people does not work.
:::

:::keypoints
- The German empire grew out of the **feudal system**. Over centuries, the great nobles broke free of the emperor, and his power shrank to "symbols and decorations."
- On paper, the **Diet** and the **emperor** could make laws, wage war, collect troops and money, and punish disobedient members with the "ban of the empire."
- But because the empire is "a community of sovereigns," it is weak, unable to control its members, and always in turmoil.
- German history is full of wars between the emperor and the princes, wars among the princes, ignored requests for troops and money, and foreign interference.
- Military coercion of members either fails or turns into civil war. In the case of **Donauwörth**, the duke sent to enforce the law simply took the city for himself.
- Foreign powers want to keep the empire weak and would block any real reform.
- **Poland**, a government over local sovereigns, can neither govern nor defend itself, and its neighbors have just taken a third of it.
- The **Swiss** have no common treasury, army, money, or courts. Religious conflicts split them into Protestant and Catholic groups with opposite foreign alliances.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 18](/papers/18/), Publius looked at two leagues from ancient Greece. Now he turns to confederacies of his own time. Educated Americans in 1787 knew these examples. The Holy Roman Empire was a patchwork of hundreds of states in central Europe. Poland had just been partly carved up by its neighbors. Switzerland was often praised as a lasting league of free republics.

Opponents of the Constitution sometimes pointed to Switzerland and the German empire as proof that loose unions could survive for centuries. Publius wants to show that they survived only in a weak and troubled way, and only because of special circumstances. The next essay, [No. 20](/papers/20/), finishes the series with the Dutch Republic.
:::

## How the German empire began {orig=1-2}

The examples of ancient confederacies in my last paper are not the only lessons experience offers on this subject. Some existing institutions are built on a similar principle and deserve close attention. The first is the Germanic body.

In the early centuries of Christianity, Germany was home to seven separate nations with no common leader. One of them, the Franks, conquered Gaul and set up the kingdom that is named after them, France. In the ninth century, Charlemagne, their warlike king, carried his victorious armies in every direction, and Germany became part of his vast realm. When his empire was broken up under his sons, this part became a separate and independent empire.

Charlemagne and his immediate descendants had real imperial power, not just its symbols and dignity. But the chief vassals, whose lands had become hereditary, made up the national assemblies, called diets, which Charlemagne had not abolished. Little by little, these vassals threw off the emperor's control. They rose to sovereign authority and independence.

The emperor's power was not strong enough to hold back such powerful subjects or to keep the empire united and peaceful. The princes and states fought the most furious private wars against each other, bringing every kind of disaster. The emperor's authority could not keep public order. It slowly declined until it almost disappeared in the anarchy of the long period between the death of the last emperor of the Swabian line and the rise of the first emperor of the Austrian line. In the eleventh century, the emperors had full sovereignty. By the fifteenth, they had little more than the symbols and decorations of power.

:::vocab
Germanic body: Publius's name for the Holy Roman Empire, a loose union of German princes, cities, and other states under an elected emperor.
Feudal system: The way medieval Europe was organized. Kings granted land to lords (barons) in exchange for loyalty and military service. The lords often became more powerful than the kings.
Vassal: In the feudal system, a person who received land from a lord in exchange for loyalty and service.
Fief: Land granted by a lord to a vassal in return for loyalty and service.
Diet: The assembly of the German Empire, where its princes and cities were represented.
:::

:::commentary
Charlemagne, king of the Franks, was crowned emperor in Rome in the year 800. His empire covered much of today's France, Germany, and Italy. Madison simplifies the breakup a little. The famous division of the empire, the Treaty of Verdun in 843, was actually made among Charlemagne's *grandsons*. The eastern part later became the core of what was called the Holy Roman Empire.

The "Swabian line" was the Hohenstaufen family of emperors. After the last of them died in the 1250s, there was a long gap with no generally accepted emperor, known as the Great Interregnum. It ended in 1273 with the election of Rudolf of Habsburg. The Habsburgs are the "Austrian line" Madison mentions. By 1787, they had held the title of emperor almost without a break for centuries.
:::

## How the empire is organized {orig=3-5}

Out of this feudal system, which itself has many of the main features of a confederacy, grew the federal system that makes up the German empire. Its powers belong to three bodies:

- a **Diet**, which represents the members of the confederacy;
- the **emperor**, who is the chief executive and can veto the Diet's decisions;
- the **imperial chamber** and the **aulic council**, two courts with supreme authority over disputes that concern the empire or that arise among its members.

The Diet has the general power to make laws for the empire. It can make war and peace, form alliances, set each member's quota of troops and money, build fortresses, regulate coins, and admit new members. It can also put disobedient members under the **ban of the empire**, which strips the guilty party of his sovereign rights and takes away his lands.

The members are expressly forbidden to:

- make agreements that harm the empire;
- put tolls and taxes on trade with each other without the consent of the emperor and the Diet;
- change the value of money;
- do injustice to one another;
- give help or shelter to people who disturb the public peace.

Anyone who breaks these rules can be put under the ban. Members of the Diet, in their official role, can always be judged by the emperor and Diet. As private persons, they can be judged by the aulic council and the imperial chamber.

The emperor has many special powers. The most important are his sole right to propose measures to the Diet and to veto its decisions. He also names ambassadors, grants ranks and titles, fills vacant electorates, founds universities, grants privileges that don't harm the states of the empire, receives and uses the public revenue, and generally watches over public safety. In certain cases, the electors serve as his council.

As emperor, he owns no territory within the empire and receives no income to support himself. But the lands and income he holds in his other roles make him one of the most powerful princes in Europe.

:::vocab
Ban of the empire: A punishment that stripped a German prince or city of its rights and property, making it an outlaw within the empire.
Aulic council: One of the two high courts of the Holy Roman Empire. "Aulic" means "of the court," because this council was tied to the emperor's court.
Imperial chamber: The other high court of the Holy Roman Empire.
Electors: The handful of powerful German princes who had the right to elect the emperor. Their territories were called electorates.
Quota: A fixed share that each member was supposed to contribute.
Prerogative: A special power or right that belongs to an office.
:::

:::commentary
Look at how much this resembles the Articles of Confederation. The Diet, like Congress, could make war and peace, set quotas of troops and money, and regulate coins. It even had courts, which the Articles mostly lacked. Madison is setting up a "strong on paper" picture, just as he did with the Amphictyonic Council in [No. 18](/papers/18/), so that he can knock it down.

The emperor's real power came from his family's own lands. In 1787, the emperor was Joseph II of the Habsburg family, who also ruled Austria, Bohemia, Hungary, and other territories directly. Without those lands, the title of emperor would have meant little.
:::

## Strong on paper, weak in fact {orig=6-7}

With such an impressive list of powers in the representatives and the head of this confederacy, you would naturally expect it to be an exception to the usual character of similar systems. Nothing could be further from the truth.

The basic principle it rests on is that the empire is a community of sovereigns, the Diet is a body representing sovereigns, and the laws are addressed to sovereigns. This principle makes the empire a body without strength. It can't control its own members. It isn't safe from outside dangers. And it is constantly in turmoil inside.

The history of Germany is a history of:

- wars between the emperor and the princes and states;
- wars among the princes and states themselves;
- the strong doing as they please and the weak being oppressed;
- foreign invasions and foreign plots;
- requests for men and money that were ignored or only partly met;
- attempts to enforce those requests that failed completely, or that brought slaughter and destruction to the innocent along with the guilty;
- general weakness, confusion, and misery.

:::quote
The fundamental principle on which it rests, that the empire is a community of sovereigns, that the diet is a representation of sovereigns and that the laws are addressed to sovereigns, renders the empire a nerveless body...

Meaning: Because the empire's laws spoke only to princes and cities, not to people, it had no real strength.
:::

:::vocab
Sovereign: Having supreme, independent authority; not subject to any higher power. As a noun, a ruler or state that has such authority.
Imbecility: In 1787, weakness or helplessness, not a lack of intelligence.
Requisition: A formal request for money or soldiers.
:::

## Wars against the emperor and among the members {orig=8}

In the sixteenth century, the emperor, with one part of the empire on his side, fought against the other princes and states. In one of these conflicts, the emperor himself was forced to flee and was very nearly captured by the elector of Saxony.

The late king of Prussia fought against his own emperor more than once, and usually beat him. Quarrels and wars among the members themselves have been so common that German history books are crowded with bloody pages describing them.

Before the Peace of Westphalia, Germany was laid waste by a war of thirty years. In it, the emperor and half of the empire were on one side, and Sweden and the other half were on the other. Peace was finally negotiated and dictated by foreign powers. And the terms of that peace, to which foreign powers are parties, became a basic part of the German constitution.

:::commentary
Each of Madison's examples is real:

- **The emperor in flight:** In 1552, Maurice, the elector of Saxony, turned against Emperor Charles V and made a surprise attack. Charles had to flee from Innsbruck and barely escaped.
- **"The late king of Prussia":** Frederick the Great, who died in 1786, just a year before this essay. Prussia was officially part of the empire, yet Frederick fought several wars against Austria, the home of the Habsburg emperors, and took the rich province of Silesia from it.
- **The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648):** It began largely as a religious conflict between Catholic and Protestant states in the empire. It drew in much of Europe and killed a huge share of Germany's population. Madison simplifies the sides. France also fought against the emperor, especially in the later years.
- **The Peace of Westphalia (1648):** Sweden and France helped write the treaty and became its guarantors. That gave foreign powers a legal excuse to interfere in German affairs.
:::

## Too slow to defend itself {orig=9-10}

Sometimes an emergency forces the nation to unite for self-defense. Even then, its situation is pitiful. Before it can prepare for war, it must get through endless tiresome debates. These come from the jealousy, pride, separate goals, and clashing claims of sovereign bodies. By the time the Diet settles its plans, the enemy is already in the field. And by the time the federal troops are ready to march, the enemy is heading home to winter quarters.

The small national army thought necessary in peacetime is poorly maintained and badly paid. It is full of local prejudices. And it is supported by irregular and unequal payments to the treasury.

:::commentary
Americans had seen a version of this problem during the Revolution. George Washington's army often went without pay, food, and supplies, because Congress could only ask the states for money and the states paid late or not at all. Hamilton, who served on Washington's staff, saw this firsthand. That experience shaped his arguments for a national power to raise armies and taxes in [No. 23](/papers/23/) and [No. 30](/papers/30/).
:::

## The circles, and what happened at Donauwörth {orig=11-12}

It proved impossible to keep order and deliver justice among these sovereign subjects. So the empire tried an experiment. It divided itself into nine or ten **circles**, or districts. It gave each one an internal organization, and made each responsible for using military force to carry out the laws against members who failed in their duties or refused to obey.

This experiment only showed more clearly the basic flaw in the constitution. Each circle is a small-scale picture of everything wrong with this political monster. The circles either fail to carry out their duties, or they carry them out with all the destruction and slaughter of civil war. Sometimes whole circles fail in their duties, and then they make worse the very problem they were created to fix.

We can judge this system of military enforcement from an example given by the historian Thuanus. Donauwörth was a free imperial city in the circle of Swabia. The Abbé de St. Croix, the head of a monastery there, had certain special rights that had been reserved for him. When he used these rights on some public occasions, the people of the city attacked and insulted him.

As a result, the city was put under the ban of the empire. The Duke of Bavaria, though he was in charge of a different circle, got himself appointed to enforce the ban. He soon showed up outside the city with ten thousand troops. He had secretly planned from the start to revive an old claim. He said that his ancestors had allowed the city to be cut off from his territory. Finding this a good opportunity, he took over the city in his own name. He disarmed and punished its people and added the city back to his own lands.

:::note
Publius's footnote cites the historian Pfeffel, who says the duke's excuse was different: he claimed he took the city to pay himself back for the cost of the expedition.
:::

:::vocab
Circle: One of the regional districts the Holy Roman Empire was divided into, each responsible for keeping order among its members.
Contumacious: Stubbornly refusing to obey authority.
Delinquent: Failing to do what one is required to do.
:::

:::commentary
The Donauwörth affair began in 1606. The town was mostly Protestant, and the trouble began when townspeople attacked a Catholic religious procession from the local monastery of the Holy Cross ("St. Croix" in French). The Catholic Duke Maximilian of Bavaria enforced the ban and then kept the town. The incident deepened the distrust between Catholic and Protestant states and is often listed among the events that led to the Thirty Years' War.

Madison's point is that when a confederacy enforces its laws with armies, the enforcer can use the job to grab power. This is the danger Hamilton described in [No. 16](/papers/16/): a union that acts only on states can enforce its laws only through war.
:::

## What keeps the empire together {orig=13}

You might ask what has kept this disjointed machine from falling completely apart for so long. The answer is obvious. Several causes support a weak and shaky union:

- Most of the members are weak, and they don't want to be left at the mercy of foreign powers.
- Even most of the main members are weak compared with the mighty powers all around them.
- The emperor gets great weight and influence from his separate, hereditary lands.
- The emperor has a strong interest in keeping a system tied to his family's pride, which makes him the first prince in Europe.

At the same time, every sovereign naturally pushes back against outside control, and time keeps strengthening this tendency. That prevents any reform based on a proper uniting of the empire.

Even if this obstacle could be overcome, we shouldn't imagine that the neighboring powers would allow a revolution that gave the empire the strength and leading place it deserves. Foreign nations have long believed they have a stake in any changes to this constitution. On many occasions, they have revealed their policy of keeping it weak and disorderly forever.

:::commentary
This is a sharp observation. Weakness can be stable if your neighbors *want* you to stay weak. France in particular had long worked to keep the German states divided. Madison is warning Americans that European powers would happily keep thirteen quarreling states weak, too, as Jay argued in [No. 4](/papers/4/) and [No. 5](/papers/5/).

The Holy Roman Empire lasted until 1806, when it was dissolved during the Napoleonic Wars. Germany did not become a single unified nation until 1871.
:::

## Poland: a government over local sovereigns {orig=14}

If we needed more direct examples, Poland, as a government over local sovereigns, also deserves mention. No clearer proof could be given of the disasters that come from such institutions. Poland is equally unable to govern itself and to defend itself. For a long time it has been at the mercy of its powerful neighbors. Recently, they were "merciful" enough to relieve it of one third of its people and territory.

:::commentary
Poland's kings were elected, and its powerful nobles controlled the national assembly, called the Sejm (another "Diet"). Under a rule called the *liberum veto*, a single member could block a decision and end a session. This often made the government unable to act.

In 1772, Russia, Prussia, and Austria seized large parts of Poland in what is called the First Partition. Madison's sarcasm about their "mercy" refers to this. His warning turned out to be even more accurate than he knew. Poland adopted a reform constitution in 1791, but its neighbors split up the rest of the country in 1793 and 1795. Poland disappeared from the map until 1918.
:::

## The Swiss cantons: barely a confederacy {orig=15-17}

The connection among the Swiss cantons hardly counts as a confederacy, though people sometimes point to it as an example of how stable such institutions can be.

They have no common treasury, no common army even in war, no common money, no common courts, and no other common mark of sovereignty.

They are held together by several things:

- their unusual geography;
- their individual weakness and unimportance;
- fear of powerful neighbors, one of which once ruled them;
- the few causes of conflict among a people with such simple and similar ways of life;
- their shared interest in the territories they rule together;
- the help they need from each other to put down uprisings and rebellions, which their agreements require and which they have often asked for and given;
- the need for some regular, permanent way to settle disputes among the cantons.

Their method is this. When two cantons disagree, each chooses four judges from the neutral cantons. If these judges disagree, they choose an umpire. This court takes an oath to be fair and gives a final decision, which all the cantons are bound to enforce.

You can judge how well this works from a clause in the cantons' treaty of 1683 with Victor Amadeus, the Duke of Savoy. In it, he promised to step in as a mediator in disputes between the cantons, and to use force against the side that refused to obey, if necessary.

:::vocab
Canton: One of the small, self-governing states that made up Switzerland.
Bailiwick: A district governed by an official called a bailiff. The Swiss cantons jointly ruled several such districts, called the "common bailages."
Homogeneous: All of the same kind; very similar.
Mediator: Someone who helps two sides settle a dispute.
:::

:::commentary
The neighbor that "formerly" ruled the Swiss was the Habsburg family of Austria. The Swiss cantons broke free of Habsburg control over the late Middle Ages. Their mountains made them hard to conquer.

The treaty with Savoy is Madison's sharpest point here. A confederacy that needs a *foreign* ruler to enforce its own court's decisions has admitted that it can't govern itself.
:::

## Religion splits the Swiss league {orig=18-19}

As far as Switzerland's unusual situation allows a comparison with the United States, it supports the principle I am trying to prove. However well the union may have worked in ordinary times, it failed the moment a serious disagreement arose that tested its strength.

Disputes over religion have three times led to violent and bloody fighting. In fact, they can be said to have broken the league apart. Since then, the Protestant and Catholic cantons have had their own separate diets. All the most important business is settled there. The general diet is left with little to do except look after the territories they rule in common.

That separation had another result that deserves attention. It led to opposite alliances with foreign powers. Bern, at the head of the Protestant group, allied with the United Provinces, the Dutch Republic. Lucerne, at the head of the Catholic group, allied with France.

:::commentary
Madison is probably thinking of the religious wars of Kappel (1529 and 1531) and Villmergen (1656 and 1712). Exactly which three conflicts he meant is not certain.

His final point matters most for Americans. Once a league splits into rival groups, each group looks for a foreign protector. That is how foreign powers get a foothold. Publius had warned in [No. 5](/papers/5/) that separate American confederacies would each make alliances with different European nations.

Switzerland later reformed. After a short civil war in 1847, it adopted a new constitution in 1848 that created a real federal government. Its two-house national legislature was partly modeled on the U.S. Congress.
:::

:::today
Countries and alliances today still face the problems Madison describes. When a union's members keep full control of their own money, armies, and laws, the union often struggles to act quickly in a crisis. Some argue that such loose unions are more respectful of local self-government and local differences. Others argue that, as in the German empire, they leave members open to outside pressure and endless internal quarrels. Switzerland itself shows that a loose league can become a successful federation, but only after major reform.
:::
