:::summary
Hamilton starts with a lesson in reasoning. Every argument rests on "first principles," basic truths that a fair mind accepts right away, like the rules of geometry. Politics has such truths too: the means should match the end, and a power should be as large as the job it has to do. People accept strange ideas in math without complaint, but in politics their interests and passions get in the way. From these first principles, Hamilton restates his case that the national government needs a general power to tax. Then he takes up the critics' main fear: that an unlimited national taxing power would swallow up the states' taxes. He answers that fears about the government grabbing power it wasn't given are guesses, and should be judged by how the government is built. If anything, the states are more likely to overpower the Union than the reverse.
:::

:::keypoints
- All reasoning depends on **first principles**, truths so clear that a fair mind accepts them without proof.
- Geometry has such truths, and so do ethics and politics: **"the means ought to be proportioned to the end"** and **"every power ought to be commensurate with its object."**
- People accept even strange ideas in math, because math doesn't stir their passions. In politics, **bias and self-interest** cloud their judgment.
- Hamilton's case in four steps: a government needs all the powers its job requires; defense needs can't be limited; revenue is the engine for meeting those needs; and asking the states for money doesn't work. So the Union needs **a full power to tax**.
- The critics fear the national government will use its power to **take over all taxes** and starve the states.
- Fears of **usurpation** are endless guesses. The real protection is the **structure** of the government and its dependence on the people.
- The states are **at least as likely** to invade the Union's powers as the other way around, and they will usually have more of the people's support.
- In the end, **the people** hold the balance between the state and national governments.
:::

:::context Background
In [No. 30](/papers/30/), Hamilton argued that the national government needs its own power to tax. This essay continues that argument. It is also his first answer to the strongest objection to it. Anti-Federalists, the Constitution's opponents, warned that if Congress could tax anything, it would eventually take every source of tax money for itself. Combined with the "supremacy clause" and the "necessary and proper clause," they said, this would leave the states with nothing.

Hamilton opens in an unusual way, by talking about geometry and logic. Educated readers of the 1700s admired Euclid's geometry, which builds a whole system of proofs from a few basic truths. Hamilton wants his political argument to look just as solid.
:::

## First principles {orig=1}

In discussions of every kind, there are certain basic truths, or first principles, on which all later reasoning must depend. These truths carry their own evidence inside them. Before any further thought or argument, they win the mind's agreement. When they don't, it must be because something is wrong with a person's powers of perception, or because some strong interest, passion, or prejudice is getting in the way.

The rules of geometry are truths of this kind: "the whole is greater than its part; things equal to the same are equal to one another; two straight lines cannot enclose a space; and all right angles are equal to each other."

Ethics and politics have rules of the same kind. Here are some:

- there can't be an effect without a cause;
- the means should be proportioned to the end;
- every power should match its object, or purpose;
- there should be no limit on a power meant to achieve a purpose that itself can't be limited.

There are other truths in ethics and politics that may not count as axioms. But they follow so directly from the axioms, are so obvious in themselves, and fit so well with plain common sense that they win the agreement of a sound and fair mind with almost the same force.

:::vocab
First principles: The most basic ideas that an argument starts from, which are accepted without needing proof.
Axiom: A basic truth so obvious that it doesn't need to be proven.
Maxim: A short statement of a general truth or rule of conduct.
Commensurate: Equal in size or matching in amount.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's four geometry rules come from the list of axioms in Euclid's *Elements*, as it was printed in Hamilton's day. Euclid was a Greek mathematician who wrote the *Elements* around 300 BC. For two thousand years, it was the standard textbook of geometry. Euclid starts with a few basic statements and proves everything else from them. Students in Hamilton's time studied it closely.

The move here is clever. Hamilton puts his own political rules, such as "the means ought to be proportioned to the end," on the same list as "the whole is greater than its part." If you accept one, he suggests, you should accept the other. But not everyone agrees that political rules are as certain as math. A critic could say Hamilton is presenting his conclusions as if they were axioms, which is a way of persuading rather than proving.
:::

## Why people argue more about politics than math {orig=2-3}

The subjects of geometry are so far removed from the things that stir up the unruly passions of the human heart that people accept them easily. They accept not only the simpler rules, but even some strange ideas. These ideas may be provable, but they clash with what the mind would naturally believe without the help of science.

Take the INFINITE DIVISIBILITY of matter. This is the idea that a FINITE thing can be divided an INFINITE number of times, down past the smallest atom. Experts in geometry agree on it. Yet to common sense it is just as hard to understand as any of the mysteries of religion that unbelievers have attacked so hard.

But in ethics and politics, people are much harder to convince. Up to a point, this is right and useful. Caution and careful study are necessary armor against error and trickery. But this stubbornness can go too far. It can turn into plain obstinacy, contrariness, or dishonesty.

It can't be claimed that moral and political knowledge is, in general, as certain as mathematics. But it is much more certain than we would think, judging from how people act in particular situations. Far more often than not, the confusion lies in the reasoner's passions and prejudices, not in the subject itself. Too often, people don't give their own minds a fair chance. Instead, they give in to some unfortunate bias. Then they get tangled up in words and confused by fine distinctions.

:::commentary
Hamilton makes a sharp point about human nature. Nobody gets angry about a geometry proof, because nobody's money or power depends on it. In politics, people's interests are at stake, so they find reasons to reject even clear arguments. Modern psychologists have studied the same tendency, sometimes called "motivated reasoning": people tend to believe what they want to be true.

Of course, the same charge could be made against Hamilton. He was a New Yorker with close ties to merchants and lenders who stood to gain from a strong national government. His opponents could say that *his* interests were shaping *his* reasoning. Hamilton half admits this possibility in the next paragraph, when he says "if we admit the objectors to be sincere."
:::

## The case for a general taxing power {orig=4-8}

How else could it happen (if we assume the critics are sincere) that arguments as clear as those for a general power of taxation should meet any opponents among thoughtful people? I have stated these arguments fully elsewhere. But it may be useful to sum them up here, before looking at the objections to them. In substance, they are as follows.

**First,** a government should contain within itself every power needed to fully achieve the goals placed in its care, and to completely carry out the duties it is responsible for. It should be free of every other control except concern for the public good and the will of the people.

**Second,** the duties of overseeing the national defense and keeping the public peace against foreign or domestic violence require preparing for accidents and dangers that have no possible limits. So the power to make those preparations should have no limits except the nation's needs and the community's resources.

**Third,** revenue is the essential engine for getting the means to meet the nation's needs. So the power to provide for those needs must include the power to raise revenue in full.

**Fourth,** both theory and practice prove that the power to raise revenue is useless when it is used on the states as collective bodies. So the national government must have an unlimited power of taxation in the ordinary ways.

:::quote
As revenue is the essential engine by which the means of answering the national exigencies must be procured, the power of procuring that article in its full extent must necessarily be comprehended in that of providing for those exigencies.

Meaning: Money is what makes everything else possible. So if the government is responsible for meeting the nation's needs, it must also have the power to raise all the money that job requires.
:::

:::vocab
Exigency: An urgent need or emergency.
Revenue: Money a government collects, mainly through taxes, to pay for its work.
Requisition: A formal request from Congress to the states for money or soldiers.
:::

:::commentary
These four steps are built like a geometry proof. Each one leads to the next: the job requires power, the job has no limits, money is the key power, and the states can't be relied on to supply it. So the conclusion seems to follow automatically.

The important phrase in the first step is "free from every other control but a regard to the public good and to the sense of the people." Hamilton isn't saying the national government should be free of all limits. He is saying it shouldn't depend on the *state governments* to do its job. The check on it should be the voters.
:::

## The critics' main objection {orig=9-10}

If experience didn't show otherwise, it would be natural to think that these points alone would prove the national government should have a general power to tax. No further argument or example would be needed. But in fact, the Constitution's opponents don't accept these points as just or true. They seem to aim their main and most passionate attack at exactly this part of the plan. So it may be helpful to examine the arguments they use against it.

The arguments they have worked hardest on come down to this:

"Just because the Union's needs may have no limits, it doesn't follow that its power to tax should have no limits. The local governments need revenue as much as the Union does. And their work is at least as important to the people's happiness. So it is just as necessary for the state governments to be able to raise money for their needs as for the national government to do so for the Union's needs.

"But an unlimited power of taxation in the national government might, and probably would in time, take away the states' means of meeting their own needs. It would leave them entirely at the mercy of Congress. The laws of the Union are to be the supreme law of the land. Congress will have power to pass all laws NECESSARY for carrying out its powers. So the national government could at any time abolish the taxes the states have set for their own purposes. It could claim that they interfere with its own taxes, and that this is necessary to make the national revenue work. In this way, all sources of taxation might gradually become a national monopoly. The state governments would be completely shut out and destroyed."

:::commentary
This was a real and serious fear. The Anti-Federalist writer "Brutus" made a similar argument in New York newspapers at about the same time. Brutus warned that Congress's taxing power, together with the supremacy clause and the necessary and proper clause, would leave the states with no reliable money of their own.

Hamilton answers this objection over the next several essays. He takes up the two clauses directly in [No. 33](/papers/33/), and in [No. 32](/papers/32/) he explains that the states keep their own power to tax.
:::

:::vocab
Monopoly: Complete control of something by one party, with no one else allowed to share it.
Supreme law of the land: The Constitution's phrase, in Article 6, for the Constitution and the national laws and treaties made under it. They win out over any state law that conflicts with them.
:::

## Fears of usurpation lead nowhere {orig=11}

This kind of reasoning sometimes seems to assume that the national government will usurp power, or seize power it wasn't given. At other times, it seems to be only a conclusion about how its powers would work under the Constitution. Only in the second sense can the argument claim to be fair.

The moment we start guessing about what powers the national government might grab, we fall into a bottomless pit and put ourselves beyond the reach of all reasoning. The imagination can wander freely until it gets lost, like someone in the mazes of an enchanted castle. It no longer knows which way to turn to escape the confusion it so carelessly wandered into. Whatever limits we place on the Union's powers, it is easy to imagine an endless chain of possible dangers. And if we give in to too much suspicion and fear, we may end up doubting everything and unable to decide anything.

I repeat here what I have said in substance elsewhere. All arguments based on the danger of usurpation should be aimed at the makeup and structure of the government, not at the nature or extent of its powers.

The state governments, under their original constitutions, have complete sovereignty. What protects us against usurpation by them? Surely it is the way they are formed, and the fact that the people who run them properly depend on the people. If a fair examination shows that the proposed national government offers the same kind of protection to a proper degree, then all fears about usurpation should be set aside.

:::vocab
Usurpation: Taking power that one has no right to.
Sovereignty: Supreme, independent political authority.
:::

:::commentary
This is the same move Hamilton made in [No. 23](/papers/23/). Don't argue about how *much* power the government has. Argue about whether it is *built* well enough to be trusted with that power. The protections he has in mind include elections, two houses of Congress, and the separation of powers, which Madison discusses in Nos. 47–51.

Critics found this answer too easy. They replied that it is wiser to set clear limits on power in advance than to hope that elections will stop abuses later. This disagreement helped lead to the Bill of Rights. The **10th Amendment** (1791), for example, says that powers not given to the national government are reserved to the states or to the people.
:::

## The states are the bigger danger {orig=12}

We should not forget that the state governments are just as likely to invade the rights of the Union as the Union is to invade the rights of the states. Which side would win such a conflict would depend on the means each side could use to succeed.

In republics, strength is always on the side of the people. And there are strong reasons to believe that the state governments will usually have the most influence over them. So the natural conclusion is that such contests will most likely end badly for the Union. The member states are more likely to invade the powers of the national government than the other way around.

But clearly, all guesses of this kind must be very vague and unreliable. By far the safest course is to set them aside completely and focus only on the nature and extent of the powers as the Constitution describes them. Everything beyond this must be left to the good sense and firmness of the people. They will hold the scales in their own hands. We may hope they will always take care to keep the constitutional balance between the national and state governments.

On this ground, which is clearly the true one, it won't be hard to answer the objections that have been made to an unlimited power of taxation in the United States.

:::quote
As in republics strength is always on the side of the people, and as there are weighty reasons to induce a belief that the State governments will commonly possess most influence over them, the natural conclusion is that such contests will be most apt to end to the disadvantage of the Union...

Meaning: In a republic, whoever has the people's support wins. Since people feel closer to their state governments, the states would probably win any power struggle with the national government.
:::

:::commentary
Hamilton's prediction that the states would usually win was partly right and partly wrong. In the early republic, states often resisted national power, and in 1861 eleven Southern states tried to leave the Union entirely to protect slavery. But over the long run, especially after the Civil War, the New Deal of the 1930s, and the civil rights era, the national government grew much stronger compared to the states. Many people today feel more connected to national politics than to their state governments. Madison makes a similar prediction in favor of the states in [No. 45](/papers/45/) and [No. 46](/papers/46/).
:::

:::today
Hamilton's idea that the people "hold the scales" between national and state power is still at work today. Americans keep arguing over which level of government should handle issues like education, health care, drug laws, and elections. Some people favor giving more power to the states, saying they are closer to the people and can try different approaches. Others favor stronger national standards, saying some rights and problems are too important to vary from state to state. Courts help settle these disputes, but voters also shape the balance by deciding which level of government to look to for answers.
:::
