:::summary
Critics said the Constitution violated a sacred rule of free government: that the legislative, executive, and judicial powers must be kept separate. Madison fully agrees that putting all power in the same hands is "the very definition of tyranny." But he argues the critics have misunderstood the rule. Montesquieu, the famous writer who made the rule popular, based it on the British constitution, where the branches overlap in many ways. What Montesquieu really meant is that no branch should hold the **whole** power of another branch. Some overlap is fine. Madison then shows that every state constitution, even those that declare strict separation, mixes the branches far more than the new Constitution does.
:::

:::keypoints
- "The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands... may justly be pronounced **the very definition of tyranny**."
- Critics charged that the Constitution blends the branches dangerously.
- **Montesquieu** took Britain as his model of liberty, yet in Britain the king, Parliament, and judges all share some of each other's powers.
- So the rule only means that the **WHOLE** power of one branch must not be held by the same people who hold the **WHOLE** power of another.
- **Every state constitution** mixes the branches, often much more than the federal Constitution, even when it declares the powers "forever separate."
- The real question isn't whether the branches touch, but whether the mixture tends toward a dangerous concentration of power.
:::

:::context Background
This essay begins a five-part discussion (Nos. 47–51) of how the new government divides power among its branches. The separation of powers was one of the most admired ideas of the 1700s. Many state constitutions written during the Revolution declared it in strong words. Anti-Federalists pointed out that the new Constitution seemed to break the rule: the president could veto laws, the Senate could approve treaties and appointments and try impeachments, and the vice president presided over the Senate. To many, this looked like a dangerous blending of powers.
:::

## The charge against the Constitution {orig=1-2}

Having looked at the general form of the proposed government and the total amount of power given to it, I now turn to the government's specific structure, and to how that power is divided among its parts.

One of the main objections raised by the more respectable opponents of the Constitution is that it supposedly violates the political rule that the legislative, executive, and judicial branches should be separate and distinct. They say the design of the national government seems to pay no attention to this essential protection for liberty. The different branches, they say, are divided and blended in a way that destroys all balance and beauty of form, and puts some essential parts of the building in danger of being crushed by the excessive weight of others.

:::vocab
Separation of powers: The principle that the three kinds of government power, making laws (legislative), carrying them out (executive), and judging cases under them (judicial), should be held by different people or bodies.
Maxim: A short statement of a general rule or principle.
:::

## Madison agrees with the principle {orig=3}

Certainly no political truth is more valuable in itself, or backed by more respected defenders of liberty, than the one this objection is based on. The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judicial, in the same hands, whether of one person, a few, or many, and whether hereditary, self-appointed, or elected, may fairly be called the very definition of tyranny.

So if the national Constitution really did concentrate power, or mixed powers in a way that tended toward concentration, no further argument would be needed to make everyone reject it. But I am confident that it will become clear to everyone that this charge can't be supported, and that the rule it relies on has been completely misunderstood and misapplied. To think about this important subject correctly, we need to figure out the sense in which liberty requires the three great branches to be separate and distinct.

:::quote
The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands, whether of one, a few, or many, and whether hereditary, self-appointed, or elective, may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny.

Meaning: When the same person or group makes the laws, enforces them, and judges them, that is tyranny, no matter whether power is held by a king, a small group, or even an elected majority.
:::

:::commentary
Notice the phrase "whether of one, a few, or many." Madison is saying that even an elected legislature, representing *many* people, would be a tyranny if it held all three kinds of power. Tyranny is about the concentration of power, not just who holds it. This connects to his fear of majority tyranny in [No. 10](/papers/10/).
:::

## What Montesquieu actually meant {orig=4-7}

The authority everyone consults and quotes on this subject is the famous Montesquieu. Even if he didn't invent this valuable rule of political science, he deserves credit for explaining it and bringing it to the world's attention most effectively. So let's first try to work out what he meant.

The British constitution was to Montesquieu what Homer's poems have been to writers who teach the rules of epic poetry. Those writers treated Homer's work as the perfect model from which all the principles of epic poetry should be drawn and against which all similar works should be judged. In the same way, this great political thinker seems to have seen the English constitution as the standard, or in his own words, "the mirror of political liberty." He presented the main features of that particular system as basic truths. So to be sure we understand him correctly, let's go back to the source of his rule.

Even a quick look at the British constitution shows that its legislative, executive, and judicial branches are by no means totally separate from each other:

- The king, the executive, is part of the legislature, since laws need his approval.
- He alone can make treaties with foreign rulers, which, once made, have the force of law in certain ways.
- He appoints all the judges. He can remove them if both houses of Parliament request it. And judges serve as one of his official councils when he chooses to consult them.
- One house of the legislature, the House of Lords, is also a great official council to the king. It alone holds judicial power in impeachment cases, and it is the highest court of appeal in all other cases.
- The judges, in turn, are so connected to the legislature that they often attend and take part in its debates, though they can't vote.

From these facts, which guided Montesquieu, we can clearly conclude what he meant when he said, "There can be no liberty where the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or body of magistrates," or, "if the power of judging be not separated from the legislative and executive powers." He did not mean that these branches should have no PARTIAL ROLE in, or no CONTROL over, each other's actions.

His meaning, as his own words suggest, and as the example he had in mind proves even more clearly, can't be more than this: when the WHOLE power of one branch is held by the same people who hold the WHOLE power of another branch, the basic principles of a free constitution are destroyed.

That would have happened in the British constitution if the king, who alone holds executive power, also had complete legislative power or the supreme power to judge. Or if the entire legislature also had supreme judicial or executive power. But that is not one of the British constitution's flaws:

- The king, who holds all executive power, can't make a law on his own, though he can veto any law. He can't judge cases in person, though he appoints the judges.
- The judges can't use any executive power, even though they are appointed by the executive. They can't make laws, though the legislature may ask their advice.
- The entire legislature can't perform any judicial act, though two of its parts together can remove judges, and one of its parts is the final court of appeal.
- The entire legislature can't use executive power, though one of its parts is the king himself, and another can try and convict lower executive officials when the third brings impeachment charges.

:::vocab
Montesquieu: Charles de Montesquieu (1689–1755), a French writer whose book *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) made separation of powers famous. The founders quoted him more than almost any other modern author.
Homer: The ancient Greek poet credited with the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*. Later writers treated his poems as the model for all epic poetry.
House of Lords: The upper house of the British Parliament, made up mostly of nobles and bishops in the 1700s. It also served as Britain's highest court until 2009.
:::

:::commentary
Madison makes a sharp argument here. Opponents of the Constitution loved to quote Montesquieu. But Montesquieu's model of liberty was Britain, and in Britain, the branches overlapped everywhere. So Montesquieu couldn't have meant total separation. He must have meant something narrower: no branch should hold the **complete** power of another.

This distinction is the key to the American system. The branches are separate, but they are also connected, so they can check each other. Political scientist Richard Neustadt later described the result as "separated institutions sharing powers."
:::

## Montesquieu's own reasons prove the point {orig=8}

The reasons Montesquieu gives for his rule are further proof of what he meant. "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person or body," he says, "there can be no liberty, because apprehensions may arise lest THE SAME monarch or senate should ENACT tyrannical laws to EXECUTE them in a tyrannical manner." Again: "Were the power of judging joined with the legislative, the life and liberty of the subject would be exposed to arbitrary control, for THE JUDGE would then be THE LEGISLATOR. Were it joined to the executive power, THE JUDGE might behave with all the violence of AN OPPRESSOR."

Some of these reasons are explained more fully in other passages. But even stated briefly like this, they are enough to confirm the meaning we have given to this famous rule by this famous author.

:::commentary
Montesquieu's reasons are easy to picture. If the same person who writes a law also enforces it, they can write unfair laws and enforce them unfairly. If a judge also writes the laws, the judge can make up a law and then convict you under it. If a judge is also the police, the judge can punish whoever they want. Each danger comes from one person holding *the whole* of two powers. That supports Madison's reading.
:::

## The state constitutions mix powers too {orig=9-20}

If we look at the constitutions of the states, we find that despite the strong, and sometimes absolute, language in which they declare this principle, not one of them keeps the branches completely separate.

**New Hampshire**, whose constitution was written most recently, seems to have fully understood that it is impossible and unwise to avoid all mixing of the branches. It qualified the principle by declaring "that the legislative, executive, and judiciary powers ought to be kept as separate from, and independent of, each other AS THE NATURE OF A FREE GOVERNMENT WILL ADMIT; OR AS IS CONSISTENT WITH THAT CHAIN OF CONNECTION THAT BINDS THE WHOLE FABRIC OF THE CONSTITUTION IN ONE INDISSOLUBLE BOND OF UNITY AND AMITY." Accordingly, its constitution mixes the branches in several ways. The state Senate, part of the legislature, is also the court for impeachment trials. The president of the state, the head of the executive, also presides over the Senate, with a vote in all cases and a tie-breaking vote. If no candidate wins a majority, the legislature elects the executive each year, and it chooses his council each year from its own members. The legislature also appoints several state officers, and the executive appoints the judges.

**Massachusetts** expresses this basic principle of liberty with enough care, though less directly. Its constitution declares "that the legislative department shall never exercise the executive and judicial powers, or either of them; the executive shall never exercise the legislative and judicial powers, or either of them; the judicial shall never exercise the legislative and executive powers, or either of them." This matches Montesquieu's rule exactly, as we've explained it, and the Convention's plan doesn't violate it at a single point. It only forbids any *entire* branch from using the powers of another. And the Massachusetts constitution itself allows a partial mixing of powers. The governor has a limited veto over the legislature. The Senate, part of the legislature, is the court for impeaching executive and judicial officials. The governor appoints judges, and can remove them if both houses of the legislature request it. Finally, the legislature appoints a number of officials every year. Since appointing officials, especially executive officials, is by nature an executive function, the authors of that constitution have, at least on this last point, broken their own rule.

I'll skip the constitutions of **Rhode Island** and **Connecticut**, because they were written before the Revolution, before anyone was paying attention to this principle.

**New York**'s constitution says nothing on this subject. But it was clearly written with an eye to the danger of improperly blending the branches. Still, it gives the governor partial control over the legislature. What's more, it gives the same control to the judiciary, and even combines the executive and the judiciary in using it. In its council of appointment, members of the legislature join the governor in appointing both executive and judicial officers. And its court for impeachment trials and correcting legal errors is made up of one house of the legislature plus the top judges.

**New Jersey**'s constitution blends the powers of government more than any of these. The governor is appointed by the legislature. He also serves as the state's chancellor and probate judge, sits on the highest appeals court, and presides over one house of the legislature with a tie-breaking vote. That same house also acts as the governor's executive council, and together with him forms the Court of Appeals. Judges are appointed by the legislature and can be removed by one house of it when the other brings impeachment charges.

In **Pennsylvania**, the president of the state, the head of the executive, is elected every year by a vote dominated by the legislature. Together with an executive council, he appoints the judges and forms a court for impeachment trials of all officials, judicial as well as executive. Supreme Court judges and justices of the peace also seem removable by the legislature, and in some cases the power to pardon belongs to the legislature. Members of the executive council automatically serve as justices of the peace throughout the state.

In **Delaware**, the legislature elects the chief executive every year. The speakers of the two legislative houses are vice presidents of the executive branch. The chief executive, along with six others, three appointed by each house of the legislature, forms the Supreme Court of Appeals, and he joins the legislature in appointing the other judges. Throughout the states, it seems, members of the legislature can also serve as justices of the peace. In Delaware, members of one house are automatically justices of the peace, and so are members of the executive council. The legislature appoints the main executive officials, and one of its houses serves as the court of impeachments. Any official can be removed if the legislature requests it.

**Maryland** adopted the principle in the most absolute terms, declaring that the legislative, executive, and judicial powers ought to be forever separate and distinct from each other. Yet its constitution has the legislature appoint the governor, and the governor appoint the judges.

**Virginia**'s language is even more direct. Its constitution declares "that the legislative, executive, and judiciary departments shall be separate and distinct; so that neither exercise the powers properly belonging to the other; nor shall any person exercise the powers of more than one of them at the same time, except that the justices of county courts shall be eligible to either House of Assembly." Yet besides this stated exception for lower-court judges, we find that the legislature appoints the governor and his executive council. Two members of the council can be removed every three years whenever the legislature wishes. And the legislature fills all the main executive and judicial offices. In one case, the power to pardon also belongs to the legislature.

**North Carolina**'s constitution declares "that the legislative, executive, and supreme judicial powers of government ought to be forever separate and distinct from each other." At the same time, it gives the legislature the power to appoint not only the governor, but all the main officials of both the executive and judicial branches.

In **South Carolina**, the legislature elects the governor. It also appoints the judges, even justices of the peace and sheriffs, and executive officers all the way down to captains in the state's army and navy.

**Georgia**'s constitution declares "that the legislative, executive, and judiciary departments shall be separate and distinct, so that neither exercise the powers properly belonging to the other." Yet the legislature fills the executive offices and has the final power to pardon. It even appoints justices of the peace.

:::commentary
This long survey is Madison's knockout punch. Opponents of the Constitution claimed to love their state constitutions, which declared separation of powers in bold words. Madison shows that those same constitutions broke the rule constantly, usually by giving the legislature control over the governor and the judges.

The pattern is important. In most states, the legislature chose the governor, appointed judges, and controlled pardons. Americans had just escaped a king, so they made their governors weak and their legislatures strong. Madison thought this was the real danger, and he develops that argument in [No. 48](/papers/48/), where he describes the legislature "drawing all power into its impetuous vortex."
:::

:::vocab
Justice of the peace: A local judge who handles minor cases.
Chancellor: A judge who handles certain kinds of cases, especially about property, wills, and contracts, in a "court of chancery."
Ex officio: Holding a position automatically because of another office you hold. Latin for "by virtue of office."
Casting vote: A tie-breaking vote. The U.S. vice president has one in the Senate.
:::

## What the survey proves {orig=21}

By pointing to these cases, where the legislative, executive, and judicial branches have not been kept totally separate, I don't mean to defend how any particular state government is organized. I know very well that, along with the many excellent principles they show, they bear strong signs of the haste, and even stronger signs of the inexperience, with which they were written.

It's all too obvious that in some cases the basic principle has been violated by too much mixing of the different powers, and even by actually combining them. And in no case has a state made adequate provision for keeping the branches separate in practice, as they are on paper.

What I wanted to show is that the charge against the proposed Constitution, that it violates the sacred rule of free government, isn't supported by the true meaning of that rule as its author intended it, or by the way it has been understood in America so far. I will continue this interesting subject in the next essay.

:::commentary
Madison's final point sets up the next essays. Writing down "the powers shall be separate" isn't enough. The states all did that, and their legislatures took over anyway. The real question is how to make separation work *in practice*. His answer, which he gives in [No. 51](/papers/51/), is to give each branch the tools and the motive to defend itself against the others.
:::

:::today
The American system still depends on the balance Madison describes: separate branches that share some powers. The president signs or vetoes laws, the Senate confirms judges and cabinet members, and courts review laws and executive actions. Modern debates often turn on where the lines are. Can a president make law through executive orders? Can federal agencies write rules that work like laws, enforce them, and then judge disputes about them? Critics argue that agencies combining all three powers come close to Madison's "very definition of tyranny." Supporters reply that modern government is too complex to work any other way.
:::
