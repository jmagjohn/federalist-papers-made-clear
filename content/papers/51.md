:::summary
Writing rules on paper won't keep the branches of government in their place. Instead, Madison says, the government must be built so its parts check each other. Each branch should be independent and should have both the power and the personal motive to resist the others: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." Because people are not angels, government needs two kinds of protection: control by the voters, plus built-in checks. In America, splitting power between the national and state governments adds a second layer of protection. And a large, diverse country makes it hard for any majority to unite against the rights of others.
:::

:::keypoints
- Each branch needs **a will of its own**, so its members should depend as little as possible on the other branches for their jobs and pay.
- The best protection is to give each branch's officials **the constitutional tools and the personal motives** to resist the others.
- Government must first be able to **control the people**, and then be forced to **control itself**.
- Elections are the main check on government, but "auxiliary precautions" (extra safeguards) are also needed.
- The legislature is naturally the strongest branch, so it is split into two houses. The weaker executive gets extra defenses.
- Dividing power between the states and the nation creates a **"double security"** for the people's rights.
- A large republic with many different interests protects minorities without needing a ruler who is independent of the people.
:::

:::context Background
This is the last of five essays (Nos. 47–51) about separation of powers. In [No. 47](/papers/47/), Madison agreed that putting all power in the same hands is tyranny. In [No. 48](/papers/48/), he showed that written boundaries ("parchment barriers") don't stop one branch from taking over another. In Nos. 49 and 50, he rejected the idea of calling on the people to settle every fight between branches. Now he gives his own answer.
:::

## The answer: let the parts check each other {orig=1}

So what can we rely on to keep power divided among the branches, the way the Constitution lays it out? There is only one answer. Outside protections, like written rules, are not enough. So we have to design the inside of the government so that its parts keep each other in their proper places.

I won't try to explain this important idea in full. But I will offer a few general observations. They may make the idea clearer and help us judge the principles and structure of the government the Convention has proposed.

## Each branch needs a will of its own {orig=2-3}

Nearly everyone agrees that keeping the powers of government separate is, to some degree, needed to protect liberty. To make this work, each branch should have a will of its own. So the members of each branch should have as little say as possible in choosing the members of the others.

If we followed this rule strictly, the people would choose all the top officials of all three branches. Each branch would be chosen through its own separate channel, with no connection to the others. Such a plan might be less difficult in practice than it seems. But it would bring some difficulties and some extra expense. So some exceptions to the rule have to be allowed.

The courts are a special case. It would be unwise to apply the rule strictly there, for two reasons. First, judges need special skills, so the main goal should be to choose them in the way most likely to find those skills. Second, judges keep their jobs for life. That soon ends any sense that they depend on whoever appointed them.

It is just as clear that members of each branch should depend as little as possible on the other branches for their pay. If the president or the judges depended on the legislature for their salaries, their independence in every other way would exist in name only.

:::commentary
Madison is explaining why the Constitution sets things up the way it does. The House is elected by the people. Senators were originally chosen by state legislatures (the 17th Amendment changed this to popular election in 1913). The president is chosen by electors. These are the "separate channels."

Judges are the exception: the president nominates them and the Senate confirms them. But because they serve "during good behavior," meaning for life unless they are impeached, they don't need to please the people who appointed them. Madison's point about pay shows up in the Constitution too: Congress cannot change the president's salary during a term, or cut a judge's salary at all. Hamilton explains this further in [No. 73](/papers/73/) and [No. 79](/papers/79/).
:::

:::vocab
Department: In the Federalist Papers, this usually means a *branch* of government: legislative, executive, or judicial.
Emoluments: Salary and other payments that come with a job.
:::

## Ambition must counteract ambition {orig=4}

But the best protection against power slowly piling up in one branch is this. Give the people who run each branch the legal tools and the personal motives to resist attacks by the others. As in every other case, the defense must be strong enough to match the danger of attack. Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. A person's self-interest must be tied to the constitutional rights of his office.

It may say something unflattering about human nature that we need tricks like this to control abuses of government. But what is government itself, if not the greatest of all reflections on human nature? If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, there would be no need for any controls on government, inside or outside.

When you design a government that will be run by humans over other humans, the great difficulty is this: you must first enable the government to control the governed, and then oblige it to control itself. Depending on the people, through elections, is of course the main control on government. But experience has taught humanity that extra precautions are also necessary.

:::quote
Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.

Meaning: Instead of hoping officials will be selfless, the system should use their ambition. A senator who wants to protect the Senate's power will resist a president who tries to grab it, and vice versa.
:::

:::quote
If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary.

Meaning: We need government because people aren't perfect. We need limits on government because the people running it aren't perfect either.
:::

:::commentary
This paragraph may be the most famous in the Federalist Papers, and it sums up the founders' view of human nature. They were neither cynical nor naive. They believed people are capable of reason and virtue, but also selfish and ambitious. So they designed a system that doesn't require good people in order to work.

Notice the two "controls." The **external** control is the people: officials who abuse power can be voted out. The **internal** controls are the "auxiliary precautions" built into the government itself: two houses of Congress, the veto, judicial independence, Senate approval of appointments, impeachment, and so on.

Madison's idea is that each official will defend the power of his own office, not because he loves the Constitution, but because he loves his own power. "The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place." Some modern scholars argue this works less well when party loyalty is stronger than loyalty to one's branch. For example, members of Congress may defend a president of their own party instead of defending Congress's powers.
:::

## The same idea runs through all of life {orig=5}

People often use rival interests to make up for a lack of better motives. You can see this all through human life, private as well as public. We see it most clearly in how lower-level offices are set up. The goal is to arrange the jobs so that each one checks the others. That way, each person's private interest stands guard over the public's rights. These sensible tricks are just as needed when we divide the highest powers of the nation.

:::commentary
Think of how a business keeps one employee from both writing checks and approving them, or how a game has a referee. Madison is saying the Constitution applies the same common-sense idea to the most powerful offices in the country.
:::

## Balancing a strong legislature and a weak executive {orig=6-7}

But it is not possible to give each branch an equal power to defend itself. In a republican government, the legislative branch naturally dominates. The fix is to divide the legislature into two parts. Each part should be elected in a different way and work by different rules. They should be as separate as possible, given that they share the same job and answer to the same people. It may even be necessary to protect against dangerous attacks with more precautions.

While the strength of the legislature requires that it be divided, the weakness of the executive may require that it be strengthened. At first glance, an absolute veto over the legislature might seem like the natural defense to give the executive. But it might be neither completely safe nor enough by itself. In ordinary times, the executive might not use it firmly enough. In extraordinary times, it might be used treacherously. Here is another idea. What if the weaker branch, the executive, had a limited link to the weaker half of the stronger branch, the Senate? Then the Senate might help defend the executive's rights, without turning its back on its own branch.

If the principles behind these observations are correct, as I am convinced they are, then let's use them to test both the state constitutions and the federal Constitution. We will find that even if the federal Constitution doesn't match them perfectly, the state constitutions do far worse.

:::commentary
Here Madison is describing the design of Congress and the presidency. Congress is split into the House and the Senate, which are elected differently and serve different term lengths. That way, the most powerful branch is divided against itself.

The president gets a **qualified** veto instead of an absolute one. Congress can override it with a two-thirds vote in both houses. Madison worried that an absolute veto would either be too scary to use or too easy to abuse. The "qualified connection" with the Senate refers to how the Senate shares in some executive powers, like approving treaties and appointments.

Notice that this analysis puts the legislature, not the president, as the biggest threat. That reflects the experience of the 1780s, when state legislatures were very powerful and governors were weak. Many people today worry more about the opposite problem.
:::

:::vocab
Absolute negative: A veto that cannot be overridden.
Encroachment: Gradually pushing into someone else's territory or taking over their powers.
:::

## Two special advantages of the American system {orig=8-9}

There are two more points that apply especially to America's federal system, and they show it in a very interesting light.

First, in a single republic, all the power the people hand over goes to one government. Abuses are prevented by dividing that government into branches. In the compound republic of America, the people's power is first split between two governments, the national and the state. Then each government's share is split again among branches. This creates a double security for the rights of the people. The different governments will control each other, while each one is also controlled by itself.

:::quote
Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people. The different governments will control each other, at the same time that each will be controlled by itself.

Meaning: Power is split twice: between the nation and the states, and then among the branches within each. That gives two layers of protection.
:::

:::vocab
Compound republic: Madison's name for the American system, which combines national and state governments.
Federalism: The division of power between a national government and state governments.
:::

## Protecting the minority from the majority {orig=10}

Second, in a republic it is very important not only to protect society against oppression by its rulers, but also to protect one part of society against injustice by another part. Different classes of citizens naturally have different interests. If a majority is united by a shared interest, the rights of the minority will be in danger.

There are only two ways to guard against this. One is to create a will in the community that is independent of the majority, in other words, independent of the society itself. The other is to include so many different kinds of citizens in the society that an unjust alliance of a majority will be very unlikely, if not impossible.

The first method is used in all governments with hereditary or self-appointed rulers, like kings. At best, it is an unreliable protection. A power independent of the society might just as easily support the unjust goals of the majority as the rightful interests of the minority. It might even turn against both.

The second method will be used in the federal republic of the United States. All authority in it will come from and depend on the society. But the society will be broken into so many parts, interests, and classes of citizens that the rights of individuals or of the minority will be in little danger from self-interested alliances of the majority.

In a free government, civil rights must be protected the same way religious rights are. In one case, protection comes from having many different interests. In the other, it comes from having many different religious groups. How secure rights are in both cases depends on how many interests and groups there are. That number will likely depend on how large the country is and how many people live under the same government.

This should convince every true friend of republican government to support a proper federal system. Suppose the Union broke into smaller confederacies or separate states. It would become easier for a majority to unite and oppress others. The best protection for everyone's rights would get weaker. Then the only other protection, a ruler independent of the people, would have to get stronger.

Justice is the goal of government. It is the goal of civil society. People have always pursued it and always will, until they achieve it or until liberty is lost in the attempt.

Picture a society where the stronger faction can easily unite and crush the weaker. That society is in chaos, just like a state of nature, where a weak person has no protection from a strong one. In a state of nature, even strong people agree to live under a government, because their own safety is never certain. That government protects the weak as well as the strong. In the same way, even powerful factions will slowly come to want a government that protects all parties, weak and strong alike.

Imagine Rhode Island cut off from the Union and left on its own. In such a small state, rights would be so unsafe, and majorities would oppress others so often, that people would soon call for a ruler independent of the people. And the call would come from the very factions whose bad rule had caused the problem.

The United States is a large republic with a great variety of interests, parties, and religious groups. Here, a majority could rarely unite except around justice and the general good. Since the minority would face less danger from the majority, there would be less excuse to protect it by giving power to someone independent of the people.

Some people disagree, but this is both certain and important: the larger the society, as long as it stays a workable size, the better it can govern itself. And luckily for the republican cause, that workable size can be stretched very far by wisely mixing in the federal principle.

:::quote
Justice is the end of government. It is the end of civil society.

Meaning: The purpose ("end") of government, and of society itself, is justice.
:::

:::commentary
This section repeats the argument of [No. 10](/papers/10/): a large, diverse republic protects minority rights better than a small one, because no single interest can easily dominate. Madison adds a comparison to religious freedom. America has so many religious groups that none can take over the government, so all of them are safer. Madison had seen this firsthand in Virginia, where he fought to end government support for one official church.

His jab at Rhode Island is pointed. Rhode Island was the only state that refused to send delegates to the Constitutional Convention, and its legislature had passed paper-money laws that hurt creditors. To Madison, it was the perfect example of a small republic ruled by a faction.

Also notice what Madison is afraid of: if republics fail to protect rights, people will eventually ask for a king or dictator to restore order. He thought the success of American self-government depended on solving this problem.
:::

:::vocab
State of nature: A thought experiment used by philosophers like John Locke and Thomas Hobbes. It imagines what life would be like with no government at all.
Hereditary: Passed down through a family, as a king's crown passes to his child.
:::

:::today
Federalist No. 51 is the clearest explanation of why American government often seems slow and full of conflict. That was the design. When Congress and the president fight over budgets, when courts strike down laws, or when states sue the national government, the system is doing what Madison intended: making ambition counteract ambition. Critics say this design can also cause gridlock and make it hard to solve urgent problems. Supporters say the friction is the price of liberty. Either way, if you want to understand how American government works, or why it sometimes doesn't, this essay is the place to start.
:::
